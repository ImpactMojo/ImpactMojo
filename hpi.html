What is the HPI — and why it matters

The Happy Planet Index, first published in 2006 and now in its 7th edition from the Hot or Cool Institute, is the longest-running attempt to measure what economies are ultimately for. It multiplies life expectancy (UN Population Division) by life satisfaction (Gallup World Poll's 0–10 ladder) to get "happy life years", then divides by the ecological footprint (global hectares per person, York University's Ecological Footprint Initiative). Rich countries buy long, satisfied lives with footprints the planet can't sustain; many poorer countries live within the planet's means but with short or unsatisfied lives. The interesting places — Costa Rica, and much of South Asia — are the ones that get the most life out of the least planet.

Making sense of the numbers

What the index measures. HPI ≈ (life expectancy × life satisfaction) ÷ ecological footprint, scaled 0–100 (the published formula adjusts satisfaction for survey variance and footprint toward global biocapacity; this page shows only published scores). Components: life expectancy at birth (UN Population Division), life satisfaction (Gallup World Poll ladder, 0–10, as shared in the World Happiness Report), and ecological footprint (global hectares per person, York University Ecological Footprint Initiative). Where Gallup skips a country-year, the authors interpolate a linear trend between surveyed years; where no satisfaction data exists at all, no HPI is computed — that's why ranks say "of 134" in 2025, not "of 157".

Reading it responsibly. Life satisfaction is self-reported and shaped by expectations, culture and survey language — treat small cross-country gaps as noise. The footprint is a consumption measure (imports count against the consumer, not the producer). And the HPI's hardest caveat cuts close to home: a small footprint can reflect green efficiency or plain deprivation, and the index cannot tell them apart. South Asia's strong showing — Bangladesh #27, Nepal #37, Pakistan #45 — rides on footprints under 1 gha alongside some of the world's lowest satisfaction scores (India's 4.37 in 2025 is far below its neighbours' life-expectancy-adjusted peers). Read the ingredients, not just the headline: the compare view exists for exactly that.

The India puzzle worth teaching. India ranked #11 in the world in 2006; in 2025 it ranks #65. Life expectancy rose more than seven years and the footprint stayed near 1.1 gha — what collapsed is reported life satisfaction: 5.35 in 2006, bottoming at 3.25 in 2019, and 4.37 in 2025 — among the largest sustained declines anywhere in the data. Whether that reflects economics, inequality, survey measurement, or something deeper is an open scholarly debate — the trend view lets you watch it happen year by year.
