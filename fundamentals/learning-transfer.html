Fundamentals

Why a good training
still changes nothing

People leave a workshop having learned something and go back to work the way they worked before. Researchers call the gap a failure of transfer. This wheel lays out ten conditions that decide whether it happens, each with three positions, from conditions that make the new practice stick to conditions that kill it.

Open any ring for what it looks like, the research behind the factor and what to do. Or switch on Rate my programme and place your own training on the wheel.

How to read it

Closer to the centre, more likely to stick

Each of the ten spokes is one condition. Its three rings describe how well the trainee’s situation meets it. The ten fall into three groups: what the learner brings, what the training does, and what the workplace does afterward. Six of the ten sit in the workplace, which is why a well-run workshop can still fail.

Centre · Likely to stickThe condition is met in full. Using the skill is the easy, expected thing to do.
Middle · At riskThe condition is partly met. Whether the skill gets used depends on the individual’s drive and luck.
Outer · Unlikely to stickThe condition is missing or works against the new practice.

Where the wheel is weak

The ten factors are supported by research, and the three-position scale is a practitioner’s device. No study has validated these exact cut-points, and the points in the summary are a prompt for discussion, not a measure. Treat a result as a list of questions to put to your team.

The evidence base is also narrow. Most transfer research comes from corporate, military and US or European public-sector training. How the factors weigh against each other in a government training in an Indian district, or an NGO’s field-staff workshop, is not well studied, and the ranking of importance may differ.

Transfer is also not the same as impact. A trainee can apply a skill faithfully and the programme can still fail because the skill was the wrong one. The Theory of Change Studio is where to test that.

Whose ideas these are

The framework

Timothy Baldwin and Kevin Ford reviewed the transfer research in 1988 under three headings: the trainee’s characteristics, the design of the training and the work environment. Lisa Burke and Holly Hutchins (2007) and Brian Blume and colleagues (2010) reviewed the evidence on each.

Personnel Psychology, 1988

The wheel format

The idea of ten factors with three positions each, drawn as a wheel with the best position at the centre, was popularised in a graphic by Matt Furness. This page does not copy his graphic or his wording. The descriptions of each position are written here against the research literature.

What ImpactMojo added

The interactive wheel, the wording of each position, the research notes, the “what to do” and “ask in your programme” prompts, and the rating mode.

Sources

    The notes under each factor say what a source’s abstract says it studied and no more. Each source was looked up on 13 November 2026. Volume and page numbers are left off because they were not confirmed. Read the paper before quoting a finding.

    Using this in a workshop

    Ask the people who ran a training, and some of the people who attended it, to rate it separately. The places where they disagree are usually where the useful conversation is.

    To plan the training itself, use the Participatory Methods Studio. For what to measure afterward, use the LogFrame Builder.
