The power cube
Three questions to ask about any decision

John Gaventa’s power cube is a way of analysing who has power over a decision. It asks where the decision is made, at which level, and in what form the power is used. Each question has three possible answers, which gives nine categories. Click any category to see the Indian evidence for it, with the source and year.

Eight Indian cases are placed on all three questions at once, further down the page.

How to read it

Three questions, asked together

Gaventa argues that the three dimensions have to be read together, because they affect one another. Take a seat in an invited local space. It is worth little if the decision was taken at another level, in a form nobody can see. A reading that answers one question and stops can miss that.

SpacesClosed, invited, or claimed. Who set the table, and on whose terms.
LevelsLocal, national, global. Where the binding decision actually sits.
FormsVisible, hidden, invisible. Whether you can observe the power at work.

What the cube cannot do

Invisible power, the bottom face, is the hardest to evidence. It is a claim about what people have come to accept as normal, and the figures on this page fit that reading without proving it. A survey cannot tell a norm someone accepts from a constraint they cannot escape.

Hidden power has a milder version of the same problem. A careful analysis names a specific issue that was kept off the table and says who gains from its absence. A careless one finds hidden power everywhere, and so explains everything.

The cube describes how power is used. It does not say who holds it or who it falls on. The Wheel covers who holds power, and the Ladder covers how much of it moved.

What it builds on

The interactive cube, 23 pieces of Indian evidence across the nine categories, and eight documented Indian cases plotted on all three dimensions, each with a named source and year.
