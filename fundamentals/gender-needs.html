How to read it

Indian women spend 305 minutes a day on unpaid work. Indian men spend 56.

Both figures are from the same survey in the same year. Almost every intervention aimed at women is aimed at the first number — making those 305 minutes less punishing. Moser’s question is what, if anything, is aimed at the fact that the minutes are hers.

A practical gender needArises from the role a woman already occupies. Meeting it makes that role less costly and does not question it: a closer water source, a gas connection, a toilet, a wage.
A strategic gender interestArises from her subordinate position. Meeting it changes the division of labour, of assets or of authority: a title deed, a reserved seat, a law that is enforced.
Neither ranks above the otherA programme that meets only strategic interests while a woman walks four kilometres for water has not understood the day. One that only ever meets practical needs leaves the arrangement that produced them intact.

Where the distinction is contested

The most serious objection is that it can be read as a hierarchy, with strategic work treated as the real feminism and practical work as welfare. Moser argued the opposite, and the framework has been used in both ways since. Nothing on this page is arranged so that the upper half is better than the lower.

A second objection is that the two are not cleanly separable in practice. A gas connection issued in a woman’s name is a practical provision that also puts a formal entitlement and an account in her name; a reserved seat is a strategic gain whose exercise depends on whether she has the hours to attend. Several cases below sit deliberately near a boundary for that reason.

And “women’s interests” is not one thing. Caste, class, disability and marital status divide what any of these interventions is worth, and a two-axis plot cannot show that. Read this one alongside the Wheel of Power, which is built for exactly that division.

Whose framework this is

Practical and strategic gender needs

Caroline O. N. Moser, from “Gender Planning in the Third World: Meeting Practical and Strategic Gender Needs”, World Development 17(11), 1989, developed in Gender Planning and Development: Theory, Practice and Training (1993).

The distinction underneath it

Maxine Molyneux, “Mobilisation Without Emancipation? Women’s Interests, the State and Revolution in Nicaragua”, Feminist Studies 11(2), 1985. The separation of practical from strategic interests is Molyneux’s, and is frequently attributed to Moser alone.

Using this in a workshop

Take a programme the group runs and place it on the plot before reading anything here. Then ask two questions of the placement. What in the programme’s design would have to change for it to move upward — and would that make it harder to deliver, or only less comfortable to describe?

The most useful argument this framework starts is usually about a programme the group is proud of. A high relief score is a real achievement and the plot is not built to take it away.
