Fundamentals

The frameworks the sector runs on,
checked against the evidence

Development work leans on a small number of diagrams. They get drawn on whiteboards, put into proposals and taught in workshops, mostly without anyone checking what the Indian evidence behind them says.

Fundamentals takes one framework at a time, makes it interactive, and attaches the Indian evidence to every position in it. Two rules hold across the series. Nothing goes in without a named source and a year. And every position says what the evidence does not settle.

In the series

Ten so far

Each framework belongs to someone else and is credited in full on its own page. ImpactMojo supplies the evidence layer underneath.

The Wheel of Power & Powerlessness →

Twelve axes along which Indian society sorts people, three bands on each, and 113 sourced statistics, statutes and judgments behind the 36 positions. Asks who holds power.

Wheel by The Listeners Collective, Bengaluru · adapting Sylvia Duckworth

The Ladder of Citizen Participation →

Eight rungs from manipulation to citizen control, with the Indian law, scheme or judgment that puts a real practice on each. Asks what people actually got to decide.

Ladder by Sherry R. Arnstein, 1969

The Power Cube →

Spaces, levels and forms of power, with the Indian evidence for each of nine categories and eight cases plotted on all three at once. Asks how power is being exercised.

Cube by John Gaventa, IDS, 2006

The Capability Approach →

Seven resources followed from what a programme provides to what a person can actually do with it — school places against reading levels, toilets built against toilets used. Asks what the provision is worth to the person receiving it.

Approach by Amartya Sen; the ten central capabilities by Martha Nussbaum

Practical & Strategic Gender Needs →

Twelve Indian interventions plotted on two axes — how much each one lifts off a woman’s day, and how far it moves the division of labour, assets or authority. Asks whether a programme lightens the load or changes who carries it.

Distinction by Caroline Moser, 1989 · building on Maxine Molyneux, 1985

The Sustainable Livelihoods Framework →

Eight Indian livelihoods drawn as asset pentagons across five capitals, shown together so the shapes can be compared — a landless labourer’s beside a large landholder’s. Asks not what a household earns but what it holds.

Framework by DFID, 1999 · after Chambers & Conway, 1992, and Scoones, 1998

The Social Model of Disability →

Six Indian outcomes drawn as barrier stacks — attitudinal, environmental, institutional and communication — standing between a person and school, work, a bus, a public building, being counted, and the certificate. Asks whether the problem is the body or the arrangements.

Distinction by UPIAS, 1976 · named by Michael Oliver, 1983 · Indian critique by Anita Ghai

The Results Chain →

Six large Indian programmes drawn as the same five links, each marked by how it is actually measured. The counting stops between outputs and outcomes in all six. Asks which link you have evidence for.

The logical framework, Practical Concepts Incorporated for USAID (c. 1969) · OECD DAC definitions (2002)

Resources, Agency, Achievements →

Four Indian series, each drawn beside the companion series from the same survey that changes how it reads — spousal violence over a lifetime against the last twelve months, participation against its U-shaped education curve. Asks whether the indicator measured a choice.

Naila Kabeer, Development and Change (1999) · NFHS and PLFS, pulled from source

Who Counts →

Eight gaps in India’s official data, what each one costs, and the nearest substitute. Asks who the state does not see.

ImpactMojo · the one framework in the series that is our own

How a position earns its place

Every entry in the series has to be backed by a national statistical source, a statute or constitutional provision, or a reported judgment. Where a figure cannot be sourced to a named publication and year, it is not on the page.

That leaves gaps, and the pages show them rather than filling them with plausible numbers. India collects no data on sexual orientation. It records disability at 2.2% of the population against a global estimate nearer 16%. It has not enumerated caste beyond SC and ST since 1931. Where a placement rests on a pattern rather than a statistic, the entry says so.

Corrections are wanted. If a figure here is stale, misattributed or misread, tell us or open an issue on GitHub.
