Fundamentals

The frameworks the sector runs on,
checked against the evidence

Development work leans on a small number of diagrams. They get drawn on whiteboards, put into proposals and taught in workshops, mostly without anyone checking what the Indian evidence behind them says.

Fundamentals takes one framework at a time, makes it interactive, and attaches the Indian evidence to every position in it. Two rules hold across the series: nothing goes in without a named source and a year, and every position says what the evidence does not settle.

In the series

Ten so far

Nine of the ten frameworks belong to other authors, who are credited in full on each page. ImpactMojo supplies the evidence underneath them, and Who Counts is ImpactMojo’s own.

The Wheel of Power & Powerlessness →

Shows who holds power. Twelve axes along which Indian society sorts people, with three bands on each, and 113 sourced statistics, statutes and judgments behind the 36 positions.

Wheel by The Listeners Collective, Bengaluru · adapting Sylvia Duckworth

The Ladder of Citizen Participation →

Shows what people actually got to decide. Eight rungs run from manipulation to citizen control, and each carries the Indian law, scheme or judgment that puts a real practice on it.

Ladder by Sherry R. Arnstein, 1969

The Power Cube →

Shows how power is being exercised. It asks where a decision is made, at which level and in what form. Each of nine categories carries Indian evidence, and eight cases are placed on all three questions at once.

Cube by John Gaventa, IDS, 2006

The Capability Approach →

Shows what a provision is worth to the person who receives it. Seven resources are followed from what a programme provides to what a person can do with it, such as school places against reading levels and toilets built against toilets used.

Approach by Amartya Sen; the ten central capabilities by Martha Nussbaum

Practical & Strategic Gender Needs →

Shows whether a programme lightens a woman’s load or changes who carries it. Twelve Indian interventions are placed on two axes: how much each one lifts off a woman’s day, and how far it moves the division of labour, assets or authority.

Distinction by Caroline Moser, 1989 · building on Maxine Molyneux, 1985

The Sustainable Livelihoods Framework →

Shows what a household holds as well as what it earns. Eight Indian livelihoods are drawn as asset pentagons across five capitals and shown together so the shapes can be compared, a landless labourer’s beside a large landholder’s.

Framework by DFID, 1999 · after Chambers & Conway, 1992, and Scoones, 1998

The Social Model of Disability →

Shows whether the problem lies in the body or in the arrangements around it. Six Indian outcomes are drawn as stacks of barriers, attitudinal, environmental, institutional and communication, between a person and school, work, a bus, a public building, being counted and the disability certificate.

Distinction by UPIAS, 1976 · named by Michael Oliver, 1983 · Indian critique by Anita Ghai

The Results Chain →

Shows which link in a programme’s chain you have evidence for. Six large Indian programmes are drawn as the same five links, and each link is marked by how it is actually measured. In all six, the counting stops between outputs and outcomes.

The logical framework, Practical Concepts Incorporated for USAID (c. 1969) · OECD DAC definitions (2002)

Resources, Agency, Achievements →

Shows whether an indicator measured a choice. Four Indian series are each drawn beside a companion series from the same survey that changes how it reads: spousal violence over a lifetime against the last twelve months, and participation against its U-shaped curve across education.

Naila Kabeer, Development and Change (1999) · NFHS and PLFS, pulled from source

Who Counts →

Shows who the state does not see. Eight gaps in India’s official data, what each one costs, and the nearest substitute.

ImpactMojo · the one framework in the series that is our own

How a position earns its place

Every entry in the series has to be backed by a national statistical source, a statute or constitutional provision, or a reported judgment. A figure that cannot be traced to a named publication and year is left off the page.

That leaves gaps, and the pages show them and leave them unfilled. The census and the national surveys this series draws on do not ask about sexual orientation. Census 2011 recorded disability at 2.2 per cent of the population, against a World Health Organization estimate of 16 per cent worldwide. The last census to publish a count of every caste was in 1931, and Census 2027 will enumerate caste in its second phase, in February 2027. Where a placement rests on a pattern and not on a statistic, the entry says so.

Corrections are wanted. If a figure here is stale, misattributed or misread, tell us or open an issue on GitHub.
