An interactive version of the Indian Wheel of Power & Powerlessness. Twelve axes, three bands on each, thirty-six positions a person can occupy. Open any band to see the Indian evidence behind where it sits: the survey, the statute or the judgment, with the source and year given.

Every axis also says what the evidence leaves unsettled.

How to read it

The centre is power. Everything else is distance from it.

Each wedge is one axis along which Indian society sorts people, and each ring is a position on it. Groups nearer the hub tend to hold more of the power in a room: in law, in assets, and in who gets counted at all.

Inner band — closer to powerThe default position. The law was drafted around this group and the survey categories were built to fit it.
Middle band — in betweenReal advantages and real limits together. Most people are somewhere in here, and it is the hardest band to draw.
Outer band — pushed to the marginWorse outcomes on most measures, weaker standing in law, and in several cases no official count at all.

What this wheel is not

There is no score. Nothing here totals up, and no ranking of who has it worst comes out of it. Adding the bands together would turn a map of structures into a contest between people.

The axes do not simply add. A Dalit woman’s position is not “woman, plus caste”. That was Kimberlé Crenshaw’s point in naming intersectionality, and Gopal Guru’s in writing that Dalit women talk differently. Concentric rings cannot show two axes acting on each other.

Placement moves. It changes by state, by decade and by room. A Christian in Mizoram, a Muslim in Lakshadweep and a Hindi speaker in Chennai all sit somewhere the national average will not predict.

Instead of marking yourself

Twelve questions, and the wheel’s own definitions applied to your answers

Marking yourself measures how you see your position, and that is patterned by power in the direction that matters most: people near the centre routinely place themselves further out. These questions ask about categories instead, then show you which band each answer falls in and the definition that put it there — so you can see the reasoning and disagree with it.

Nothing here is transmitted. There is no request in this code and no server involved. Skip any question you would rather not answer.

Method

How a band earns its place

A placement on this page had to be backed by at least one of three things: a national statistical source (Census, NFHS, PLFS, NSS, NCRB, AISHE, UDISE+, the Time Use Survey), a statute or constitutional provision, or a reported judgment. Where a figure could not be sourced to a named publication and year, it is not on the page.

That rule left gaps. They are still gaps here rather than being filled with plausible-sounding numbers:

  • Body and appearance has the least behind it. No Indian official survey collects skin tone, body size or appearance-based discrimination, so the placement rests on market and case-law evidence. The panel says as much.
  • Sexual orientation has no population data at all. The Census, NFHS and PLFS do not ask, so every figure in that wedge describes law rather than people.
  • Disability is measured at 2.2% of the population against a global estimate nearer 16%. The outer band is largely a population the state has not counted, and being uncounted is part of the disadvantage.
  • Caste beyond SC and ST has not been enumerated nationally since 1931, so the OBC band rests on state surveys and sample data rather than a census.

Two figures are drawn from the NITI Aayog National MPI baseline round (NFHS-4, 2015–16) rather than the 2023 Progress Review, because the baseline is where the social-group breakdown is published. They are labelled that way in the panels.

If a figure here is stale, misattributed or misread, please tell us or open an issue on GitHub.

The Indian Wheel of Power & Powerlessness

The Listeners Collective in Bengaluru, a multidisciplinary group of mental health practitioners working from a social-justice frame. They saw the need for a wheel centred on Indian social reality and built it with mental health professionals, subject-matter experts and people with lived experience.

The idea underneath it

Kimberlé Crenshaw named intersectionality in 1989, and Patricia Hill Collins gave it the “matrix of domination”. Every wheel of this kind is a diagram of that argument.

Crenshaw 1989 · Collins 1990

The Indian lineage

The argument that Indian power has to be read through caste and gender together is much older than the diagram: B.R. Ambedkar (Annihilation of Caste, 1936), Savitribai and Jotirao Phule, Uma Chakravarti on brahmanical patriarchy, Sharmila Rege on Dalit feminist standpoint, and Gopal Guru’s “Dalit Women Talk Differently” (1995).

What ImpactMojo added

The interactive map, and the evidence: 113 sourced statistics, statutes and judgments attached to the 36 bands, each with a named publication and year, plus a note on each axis about what the evidence does not settle.

Using this in a classroom or workshop

The wheel works better as a conversation opener than as a handout. One sequence that works: let people mark themselves privately, pick one axis where their own placement surprised them, then read the evidence panel together and argue with it.
