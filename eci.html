What this shows — and why it matters

Voter turnout is the share of registered electors who actually voted — the most basic measure of how much people use their democratic voice. India's 2024 election was the largest exercise of the franchise in human history: nearly two billion electors, 1.28 billion votes, a 65.79% national turnout. Turnout varies enormously by state — from the high-participation north-east and south to the large northern states where it dips below 57% — and one landmark stands out: women turned out at nearly the same rate as men, a quiet reversal of a decades-old gap.

Making sense of the numbers

What turnout measures. Turnout here is votes polled as a percentage of registered electors, for the 2024 Lok Sabha election, aggregated from all 543 parliamentary constituencies. It counts votes recorded at polling stations (the ECI's headline 65.79%). It does not tell you who people voted for, and a high turnout is not automatically "healthier" democracy — compulsory registration, competitive contests and local mobilisation all move the number.

Reading it responsibly. Low turnout in a large state like Bihar or Uttar Pradesh reflects many things — migration (voters registered where they no longer live), heat, and the sheer scale of the electorate — not simply apathy. Compare a state against the All-India line and against its own neighbours rather than reading any single figure as a verdict.

Women's participation. A defining feature of recent Indian elections is that women now vote at essentially the same rate as men (the ECI reported women's participation at parity in 2024) — a remarkable shift from the large gender gap of earlier decades, and one of the strongest signals of women's growing political voice. Constituency-level gender turnout is published by the ECI in its detailed statistical reports.
