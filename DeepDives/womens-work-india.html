What India’s new women workers do

Between 2017-18 and 2023-24 the share of Indian women aged 15 and over counted as working rose from 22.0% to 40.3%. The rise is real in the survey and it is large. The survey’s own records show what kind of work it is: mostly unpaid help and own-account work, mostly on farms, and more and more of it counted as a side activity.

Analysis by the ImpactMojo team
Every figure below is computed from the unit-level records of the Periodic Labour Force Survey for July 2017 to June 2018 and July 2023 to June 2024. Before any chart was drawn, our tables reproduced MoSPI’s published participation rates for women (all India, rural and urban) and for men in both years, to the decimal. The method is at the foot of the page.
House Pick
The number

The labour force participation rate of women aged 15 and over, in usual status, was 23.3% in 2017-18 and 41.7% in 2023-24, according to the National Statistical Office. For men it moved from 75.8% to 78.8%. The rise for women has been cited as evidence that more Indian women are taking up jobs.

Participation counts anyone working or looking for work. This page uses the share of women who work, which rose from 22.0% to 40.3%, a gain of 18.3 percentage points, and asks three questions of the 330,620 and 319,773 people aged 15 and over in the two survey rounds. What kind of work is it? In which industry? And is it the woman’s main activity or something she does beside it?

Section 01

What kind of work

Women aged 15 and over who work, by kind of work, each as a share of all women aged 15 and over.

Unpaid helpers in a household enterprise rose from 7.0% to 14.8% of all women aged 15 and over, and own-account workers from 4.3% to 12.1%. Together they account for 15.6 of the 18.3-point rise, or 85%. A helper works in the family farm or business and is not paid for it. An own-account worker runs an enterprise alone or with family help and hires no one.

Regular wage and salaried work, the kind that comes with a pay slip, rose by 1.8 points, from 4.6% to 6.4%. Casual labour barely moved. The rise is in the forms of work that are hardest to see from outside the household.

Section 02

On the farm

Women aged 15 and over who work, by whether the work is in agriculture, as a share of all women aged 15 and over.

Women working in agriculture rose from 12.5% to 26.0% of all women aged 15 and over: 13.5 of the 18.3 points, or 74% of the rise. In villages the share went from 17.3% to 35.8%. In towns and cities, where agriculture barely figures, women’s work rose from 18.2% to 26.0%, a much smaller change, and most of it is outside farming.

Section 03

State by state

Share of women aged 15 and over who work, 2017-18 and 2023-24, for 21 states, with the part of each state’s rise that came from unpaid helpers and own-account workers.

Women’s work rose in every state shown, and most of the largest rises came in states where it began low. In Bihar the share went from 4.0% to 30.1%, and in Assam from 11.0% to 48.1%; unpaid helpers and own-account workers make up 90% and 91% of those rises. In Uttar Pradesh the figures are 13.1% to 33.6%, with 95% from the same two kinds of work.

Kerala, where women’s schooling is highest, is the exception among large states: its share rose from 20.4% to 36.0%, and only 40% of that came from helpers and own-account work. Andhra Pradesh, which started high, barely moved (40.8% to 43.1%).

Section 04

Main work or side work

Of all workers aged 15 and over, the share counted as working only because of a subsidiary activity, work of at least 30 days in the year done beside a main activity outside the labour force.

Usual status counts two things: a person’s principal activity over the past year, and any subsidiary work she did for at least 30 days. In 2017-18, 10.5% of women counted as workers were counted only through a subsidiary activity. In 2023-24 it was 27.3%. For men the figure went from 0.7% to 2.8%.

Measured against all women aged 15 and over, women working only in subsidiary status rose from 2.3% to 11.0%. That is 8.7 points, about 48% of the whole rise. A large part of the increase is women whose main activity is still recorded as domestic duties, and who also work on the family’s land or in its business for part of the year.

Section 05

What the numbers can and cannot settle

The questionnaire and the definitions were the same in both rounds, so the rise is not an artefact of a new definition of work. Three readings fit the records, and this data cannot choose between them.

The first is that more women are working, because households need the income or because more family enterprises exist. The second is that work women always did on the farm or in the shop is now being recorded, because investigators ask about it more carefully than before. The third is distress: a household whose income fell, or whose men left for work elsewhere, puts women to work on its own land. Each predicts something different about earnings, hours and who the women are, and settling it needs those measures, which this page does not yet report.

What the data do settle is the description. India’s new women workers are, for the most part, unpaid or self-employed, rural, in agriculture, and increasingly counted through work they do beside their main activity. A claim that women have moved into jobs in the sense of wage employment is not supported by these records. The companion page on who is still at work at five o’clock shows, from the Time Use Survey, how much unpaid work women do on top of whatever paid work they have.

Section 06

Readings

The data behind this page, and research on why women’s participation in India fell for two decades before it rose.

Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation, “Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS) – Annual Report [July, 2023 – June, 2024]”, press release, Press Information Bureau, 2024

MoSPI’s summary of the 2023-24 round, with the published participation rate (41.7% for women) and worker population ratio (40.3%) that our tables reproduce.

National Statistical Office, Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS), July 2023–June 2024, unit-level data, MoSPI Microdata Catalogue 213

The 2023-24 records this page is computed from. Free to download after registration.

National Statistical Office, Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS), July 2017–June 2018, unit-level data, MoSPI Microdata Catalogue 204

The first PLFS round, and the low point of the series for women’s participation.

Farzana Afridi, Taryn Dinkelman and Kanika Mahajan, “Why are fewer married women joining the work force in rural India? A decomposition analysis over two decades”, Journal of Population Economics 31(3): 783–818 (2018)

Explains the fall in rural women’s participation from 1987 to 2011. Changes in individual and household attributes account for all of it in 1987-1999 and more than half in 1999-2011, with rising education of married women and of the men in their households the largest factor. The background against which the recent rise has to be read.

Stephan Klasen and Janneke Pieters, “What Explains the Stagnation of Female Labor Force Participation in Urban India?”, World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 7222 (2015)

Urban married women’s participation stayed near 18% from 1987 to 2011 despite growth, falling fertility and rising education. On the supply side, rising household incomes and husbands’ education; on the demand side, the sectors that employ women grew least. Read it beside Section 02: the urban rise since 2017-18 is still small.

Erin K. Fletcher, Rohini Pande and Charity Troyer Moore, “Women and Work in India: Descriptive Evidence and a Review of Potential Policies”, Harvard Kennedy School working paper (2018)

Five descriptive facts about women and work from a national household survey, beginning with substantial demand for jobs among women not in the labour force and the difficulty they have finding one. Useful for the question this page leaves open: whether the new work is the work women wanted.

Indira Hirway (ed.), Mainstreaming Unpaid Work: Time-use Data in Developing Policies (Oxford University Press, 2017)

On how labour surveys miss or misclassify work done inside the household, which bears directly on the second reading in Section 05.

How these figures were made

Data. NSO Periodic Labour Force Survey, first-visit person records for rural and urban areas, July 2017-June 2018 and July 2023-June 2024 (MoSPI Microdata Catalogues 204 and 213), as released in CSV. Weight: MULT divided by 100 where the sub-sample is the combined one (NSS equal to NSC), else by 200, and divided by the number of quarters, as in MoSPI’s annual estimates.

Check against the published figures. Our code reproduces the published usual-status participation rates for women (all India, rural and urban) and for men, aged 15 and over, in both years, and the build stops if they differ.

Definitions. Usual status, principal and subsidiary, persons aged 15 and over. A person works if the principal or subsidiary status code is 11 to 51. Kind of work is the principal status where that is work, else the subsidiary status. Agriculture is NIC-2008 section A for the same activity. States are shown only where the sample holds at least 2,500 women aged 15 and over in each year.

Limits. No layout file was released with the CSV files, so this page uses only fields whose meaning the data confirm: status, industry, age, sex, sector, state and weights. Earnings and hours are not reported here for that reason. From 2025 the survey changed design and reports by calendar year, so its later figures are not set beside these. The aggregates are in data/deep-dives/womens-work.json, and the code that makes them is in scripts/womens-work/ in the ImpactMojo repository.
