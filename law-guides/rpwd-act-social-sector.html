The RPwD Act for the Social Sector

A plain-English guide to the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 — what it demands of your organisation as an employer, as a service provider, and as an advocate.

Section 01

From charity to rights — and why NGOs are covered

The RPwD Act, 2016 replaced the 1995 disability law to bring India in line with the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities. Its core move is a frame shift: disability is not a medical defect to be pitied but the interaction between impairments and barriers — attitudinal, physical, informational — that society builds. Rights, accessibility, and reasonable accommodation replace charity as the organising ideas.

Two things make it directly binding on social-sector organisations. First, "establishment" includes private bodies — an NGO is an establishment, with employer duties. Second, most development programmes are exactly the services (education, health, livelihoods, relief) where the Act demands non-discrimination and accessibility. An inaccessible training venue or an application form that screens out blind candidates is not just poor practice; it is the kind of barrier the Act exists to dismantle.

Section 02

The Act in six points

  1. 21 specified disabilities. Including blindness and low vision, deafness and hard of hearing, locomotor disability, intellectual disability, autism spectrum, mental illness, specific learning disabilities, speech and language disability, thalassemia, haemophilia, sickle-cell disease, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's, acid-attack victims, and dwarfism. Certification yields a disability certificate and the UDID card.
  2. Rights and non-discrimination. Equality, non-discrimination in employment, community living, protection from cruelty and exploitation, accessible voting and justice, and legal capacity. Discrimination on grounds of disability is prohibited unless the act is shown to be a proportionate means to a legitimate aim.
  3. Reasonable accommodation is a legal concept, not a favour. "Necessary and appropriate modification and adjustments, without imposing a disproportionate or undue burden" — screen readers, sign interpretation, flexible schedules, accessible formats, modified duties. Denying reasonable accommodation is a form of discrimination.
  4. Accessibility is mandated with standards and deadlines. Buildings, transport, ICT, and services must follow notified accessibility standards (the Harmonised Guidelines for the built environment; WCAG-aligned norms for digital). New public infrastructure must comply, and the Act set time-bound retrofit obligations for existing infrastructure.
  5. Benchmark disability unlocks affirmative measures. Persons with at least 40% of a specified disability get reservation in government employment (4%) and higher-education institutions (5%), plus scheme entitlements. Private employment has no quota — but full non-discrimination and accommodation duties apply.
  6. Enforcement machinery exists and is usable. Chief Commissioner and State Commissioners for Persons with Disabilities can inquire into deprivation of rights (suo motu or on complaint), special courts try offences, and Section 89 onwards penalise contraventions and atrocities against persons with disabilities.
Section 03

Your three hats under the Act

Employer · every NGO with staff

Publish an Equal Opportunity Policy and register it with the State Commissioner (establishments with 20+ employees must also appoint a Liaison Officer overseeing recruitment of persons with disabilities), keep records of employees with disabilities, provide reasonable accommodation, and never discriminate in recruitment, promotion, or conditions of work.

Service provider · every programme you run

Programmes open to the public must be accessible: venues, materials, digital platforms, communication. Disability-disaggregated data (the Washington Group questions are the field standard) is how you find out whether your "universal" programme actually reaches the 2.2%+ of Indians with disabilities — most MEL systems simply don't look.

Advocate and ally · with, not for

The Act gives campaigners concrete hooks — accessibility deadlines, scheme entitlements, UDID access, Commissioner complaints. The sector norm the disability movement insists on: "nothing about us without us" — build with organisations of persons with disabilities (OPDs), not merely for beneficiaries.

Section 05

Gray areas practitioners argue about

How much accommodation is "disproportionate burden"?

The Act's escape valve — accommodation need not impose an "undue burden" — is undefined in rupee terms. For a small NGO, a sign-language interpreter for every event may genuinely be disproportionate; refusing a screen-reader-compatible PDF never is. Document the analysis when you decline an accommodation: cost, alternatives considered, and what you offered instead.

Invisible and psychosocial disabilities

Mental illness, learning disabilities, and chronic blood disorders are specified disabilities, but disclosure is risky for employees and certification is patchy. Organisations default to wheelchair-symbol thinking while most disability in their workforce stays invisible. The duty to accommodate does not wait for a certificate — it is triggered by need.

Certification and UDID friction

Rights keyed to "benchmark disability" depend on medical boards whose assessments vary widely by district, and UDID coverage is incomplete. Programmes that hard-require certificates exclude exactly the people with least system access; good designs accept self-identification for participation while supporting certification for entitlements.

Old buildings, rented offices, field venues

Retrofit duties are clear for public infrastructure but murkier for a rented second-floor walk-up office or a panchayat bhavan training venue. The practical standard emerging from Commissioner complaints: control what you choose (venue selection is your decision) and mitigate what you inherit.

Section 06

Frequently asked questions

We're a 12-person NGO. Which employer duties actually apply to us?

The Equal Opportunity Policy, non-discrimination, and reasonable accommodation apply to every establishment. The Liaison Officer requirement and fuller record-keeping attach at 20+ employees. The government-job reservation does not apply to private employers at any size.

Are we required to hire persons with disabilities?

There is no private-sector quota. You are required not to discriminate and to accommodate — and government incentives exist for private employers who do hire. Many NGOs adopt voluntary targets, which is also what credible inclusion advocacy looks like from the inside.

Do our workshops legally need sign-language interpretation and accessible venues?

Public-facing services must be accessible, and reasonable accommodation must be provided on request — proportionate to your capacity. The defensible baseline: ask about access needs at registration, choose accessible venues when you control the choice, and budget for accommodations rather than treating each request as a crisis.

What can happen if we ignore all this?

Complaints to the State Commissioner (who has civil-court powers to summon and recommend), fines under Section 89 for contraventions of the Act and rules, and — for humiliation, exploitation, or denial of rights amounting to offences against persons with disabilities — prosecution. Plus the quieter penalty: funders now audit disability inclusion.

How does the RPwD Act interact with the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017?

They overlap deliberately: mental illness is a specified disability under RPwD, while the MHCA governs mental-health care, advance directives, and rights within treatment. Organisations in mental-health programming need both — RPwD for inclusion and accommodation, MHCA for service standards and consent.

Judgments that shaped this law

The judgments below are the ones that changed what this law requires in practice. Summaries are editorial orientation, not legal advice — the linked judgment is what is authoritative.

Rajive Raturi v. Union of India (accessibility standards)

Supreme Court of India · 8 November, 2024 · still good law

Held. Held Rule 15 of the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Rules 2017 ultra vires the 2016 Act, because it made accessibility guidelines recommendatory where the Act requires a mandatory floor, and directed the Union to frame mandatory accessibility standards under section 40.

What changed. Turns accessibility from something to aspire to into a standard that can be enforced. It came after years of non-compliance with the earlier phase of the same case, and the Court has stayed with it since &mdash; check the current orders, not only this one.

Read the judgment

State of Kerala v. Leesamma Joseph

Supreme Court of India · 28 June, 2021 · still good law

Held. Under the Persons with Disabilities Act 1995, a person with benchmark disability is entitled to be considered for reservation in promotion in posts identified for persons with disabilities, even where the service rules provide no such reservation and even where the employee did not enter service through the disability quota. The Court dismissed Kerala's appeal and directed the State to identify the posts and provide reservation in promotion within three months.

What changed. The route of entry into service does not decide eligibility. What counts is that the employee has the disability when promotion is considered. The Court noted that the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act 2016 now deals with reservation in promotion.

Read the judgment

Vikash Kumar v. Union Public Service Commission

Supreme Court of India · 11 February, 2021 · still good law

Held. The Court held that the appellant, who has writer's cramp, a chronic neurological condition listed in the Schedule to the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act 2016 but below the 40% benchmark, is entitled to a scribe for the Civil Services Examination and any other competitive selection conducted under government authority. It held that reasonable accommodation is an individualised duty and that a person should not be expected to be 'disabled enough' to claim it. It directed the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment to frame scribe guidelines in consultation with persons with disabilities within three months.

What changed. A candidate with a specified disability under Section 2(s) of the 2016 Act who falls short of benchmark disability can claim a scribe for the Civil Services Examination and other competitive selections under government authority. The Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment issued Office Memorandum F.No.29-6/2019-DD-III on 10 August 2022. In Gulshan Kumar v Institute of Banking Personnel Selection (3 February 2025, 2025 INSC 142) the Court directed the Ministry to revisit that memorandum, remove the restrictions and re-notify it within two months, so that scribe and compensatory time reach all candidates with disabilities.

Read the judgment

Disabled Rights Group v. Union of India

Supreme Court of India · 15 December, 2017 · still good law

Held. Directed enforcement of the statutory reservation for persons with disabilities in higher education institutions, including centrally funded ones.

What changed. Gave disability-rights organisations an enforceable handle on institutions that had treated the education quota as optional.

Read the judgment

Rajive Raturi v. Union of India

Supreme Court of India · 15 December, 2017 · still good law

Held. Directed Union and State governments to make public buildings, transport and information accessible within fixed timelines under the RPwD Act.

What changed. Converted accessibility from an aspiration in the Act into a supervised programme with deadlines, and gave organisations a citation for demanding accessible premises and formats.

Read the judgment

Jeeja Ghosh v. Union of India

Supreme Court of India · 12 May, 2016 · still good law

Held. Offloading a passenger with cerebral palsy without assessment violated her dignity; the Court awarded compensation and stressed the social model of disability.

What changed. Made dignity, not medical fitness, the test for excluding a disabled person from a service — with damages attached, which is what gets service providers to change protocol.

Read the judgment

Suchita Srivastava v. Chandigarh Administration

Supreme Court of India · 28 August, 2009 · still good law

Held. A three-judge bench held that a woman's right to make reproductive choices is a dimension of personal liberty under Article 21. It set aside a High Court direction to terminate the pregnancy of an adult orphaned woman with mild mental retardation (the judgment's term) who had expressed willingness to bear a child, holding that the Medical Termination of Pregnancy Act, 1971 requires her consent and that the State's guardianship could not be mechanically extended to decide termination.

What changed. For an adult woman with intellectual disability, termination of pregnancy needs her own consent, because the Act's exception for guardian consent covers minors and persons with mental illness, which the Court distinguished from mental retardation. A State or institution acting as guardian cannot treat its guardianship as consent to termination. Check the current text of the Act, which this judgment applied as it stood in 2009.
