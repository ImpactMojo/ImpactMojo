What this measures — and why it matters

The migration rate is the share of people whose last usual place of residence is different from where they live now — India's official count of internal migrants. It is one of the least-understood big numbers in the country: nearly three in ten Indians are migrants, yet policy, welfare and political representation are still overwhelmingly tied to where people were born. Two patterns dominate: migration is far higher in cities (drawing workers in), and the single largest reason overall is marriage — because most women in India move to their husband's household — while for men employment leads. This explorer maps the rate; the reasons are described below.

Making sense of the numbers

What "migrant" means here. A migrant is anyone whose last usual place of residence differs from the place they were enumerated — so it counts moves within a district, between districts and between states, for any reason. That is why the rate is high (29.1% all-India) and why it is dominated by women moving at marriage. It is not a measure of only work-driven or long-distance migration.

Why cities read high. Urban areas (34.6%) rank above rural (26.8%) because cities are net destinations — they pull in workers and families. A high state rate like Chandigarh's or the metros' reflects in-migration; it does not mean the local-born population is unusually mobile.

The reasons behind the rate. In the same survey, marriage is the leading reason for migration overall (driven by women, the overwhelming majority of whom migrate for marriage), while among men the leading reasons are employment and moving with an earning family member. Reasons are collected in granular sub-categories and split by rural/urban, so they are described here rather than charted, to avoid over-simplifying the mix.
