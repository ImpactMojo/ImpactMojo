Animal Welfare Law and the Fifty-Rupee Fine

ImpactMojo Editorial 19 August 2026 16 min read
animal welfareIndialawenforcement

India has a demanding animal welfare law that costs almost nothing to break.

In 2014 the Supreme Court, in the jallikattu judgment, read the word “life” in Article 21 to mean, for animals, something more than survival: a life with “some intrinsic worth, honour and dignity”. It said it expected Parliament to amend the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act so that breaches of section 11 met an effective deterrent. In 2017 the Law Commission quoted the Animal Welfare Board’s view that confining hens in battery cages breaks section 11(1)(e) of the Act, revised the draft egg-laying hens rules that had been pending since 2012, and said they should be considered for notification. On paper, the question of whether Indian animals are owed anything has had an answer for a decade, though not as a fundamental right: a Constitution Bench said in 2023 that the 2014 judgment does not lay down that animals have fundamental rights.

Under section 11 of the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act, 1960, the fine for a first offence of cruelty is at least ten rupees and at most fifty. Parliament last rewrote that sentence in 1982, and the amount has not been touched since.

The chart understates the problem, and it is worth being precise about how. The CPI-AL series used here is based on 1986-87, which is twenty-six years after the Act was passed, and after the penalty was last rewritten in 1982. Price rises before 1986-87 are missing from the series. Measured from that later starting line, the fine has still lost more than nine-tenths of its value.

The Law Commission made the same point in 2017: fine provisions that “may have been a sufficient deterrent in 1960” had “lost their significance due to inflation”.

A law that is enforced by not being enforced

At that level a penalty stops working as a penalty. It changes what the offence is, because it sets a price for the conduct. A fine of three rupees and seventy-nine paise in real terms is a cost that no commercial operation needs to weigh.

This shows most plainly in the rules for egg-laying hens. In 2012 the Animal Welfare Board advised the government that confining hens in battery cages breaks section 11(1)(e) of the Act, which bars keeping an animal in a cage too small to give it a reasonable opportunity for movement, and that existing cages should be phased out by 2017. The Law Commission set that letter out in its 2017 report and recommended that its revised draft rules be considered for notification. The rules the Centre did notify, on 27 February 2023, regulate cages instead of ending them. New or replacement cages must give each bird at least 550 square centimetres of floor space, and existing farms have until 1 January 2029 to comply.

The split in that chart matters. More than a third of the birds are backyard birds kept by households, and no cage rule reaches them. The cage rules concern laying hens in commercial flocks, a subset of the 534.74 million commercial birds, which are not all laying hens.

What we do not know, and should stop pretending we do

The figure quoted most often is that at least 70% of Indian eggs come from caged hens. Humane Society International publishes it. It is not a government statistic, and we found no official Indian series that publishes the cage-free share.

An empty cell is a real result. It means that when the 2023 rules reach existing farms in 2029, there will be no baseline we could find against which to measure whether anything changed. It also means the reform argument is being conducted, on both sides, with a number nobody has to stand behind.

The finding that fits nobody’s politics

One finding in the literature is awkward for both sides of the Indian debate.

A 2025 scoping review of dairy cattle welfare in India, published in the journal Animals, argues that slaughter bans can make welfare worse across a herd. The mechanism is simple and needs no one to act in bad faith.

A dairy farmer in a state that bans cow slaughter, with an animal that no longer produces milk and no lawful way to sell it for meat, faces a fixed feed budget and one more mouth. The animal is either kept and fed out of what would have gone to the others, or it is let go. The 2019 Livestock Census counted about five million stray cattle.

Neither outcome is what the restriction was for. The cost falls beyond the protected animals, on every animal in the herd whose ration got thinner.

Protecting cattle remains a legitimate aim. The point is that a welfare measure which does not fund the consequences it creates can work against its own purpose.

What would actually change something

The draft Prevention of Cruelty to Animals (Amendment) Bill has been sitting since 2022. It contains sixty-one proposed amendments, including a new category of “gruesome cruelty” punishable by a fine of ₹50,000 up to ₹75,000 (or the cost of the animal, if higher), imprisonment of one to three years, or both. It answers the Supreme Court’s 2014 expectation that Parliament would amend the Act.

It is a draft and not law, so check its status before citing it.

Three things follow from all of this, and none of them requires a new principle:

  • Revisit the 2023 hen rules. The Animal Welfare Board has read section 11 as forbidding battery cages since 2012, and the 2023 rules permit them with a minimum floor space and a 2029 deadline for existing farms.
  • Pass the penalty revision. A statute whose sanction has fallen to ₹3.79 in real terms is enforced, when it is enforced at all, at a price.
  • Publish the cage-free share. Without it, neither the reform nor its absence can be evaluated, and the argument stays anecdotal indefinitely.

In 2023 a Constitution Bench upheld the Tamil Nadu, Karnataka and Maharashtra amendments that permit jallikattu, kambala and bullock-cart racing. It did not accept the 2014 finding that jallikattu is not part of Tamil cultural heritage, and it said the 2014 judgment does not hold that animals have fundamental rights. Read alongside Nagaraja, it shows where the courts stopped: a legislature that regulated a practice instead of banning it was allowed to proceed, and the Bench directed that the amended law be strictly enforced. On rights it left the next step to the legislature. What remains is administrative (a notification, a schedule of fines, a statistical series) and none of it is waiting on anybody’s philosophy.

A prioritisation exercise reaches the same place

In August 2026 Animal Ask published Leverage Points, a strategic prioritisation of animal advocacy in India built from stakeholder consultation and two workshops with domestic advocates, researchers, industry and policy experts. It sets out seven priority areas for funders and organisations deciding where to put limited money and people.

The seventh is enforcement of existing law. Their assessment of it is blunter than ours: “arguably this would be the highest-leverage systemic reform if it could be achieved.” Elsewhere they put the case for it structurally, every welfare law, “from cage provisions to slaughter standards to tethering, is currently constrained by issues with enforcement, detection, inspection, and penalty gaps”, which makes reform there a multiplier on the value of every other policy win.

That is the same conclusion this piece reached from a different direction. We got here by deflating a single number; they got here by ranking seven problems against each other. It is worth saying plainly that neither route is evidence for the other, a shared conclusion reached twice is still one conclusion, but the report does something this piece could not, which is specify the ask.

Where we said “pass the penalty revision”, they name the mechanisms:

  • Make offences cognizable. Under section 31 of the PCA only offences under section 11(1)(l), (n) and (o) and section 12 are cognizable. The rest of section 11 is not, and in a non-cognizable case the police can investigate only on a magistrate’s order. The fine is the visible half of the enforcement problem; this is the half that stops a case beginning at all.
  • Give SPCA inspectors the power to impose monetary penalties directly. Detection without a sanction the detector can apply is a report that goes into a drawer.
  • Fund functional SPCAs with small teams of staff focused on farmed animals, who investigate and refer cases to Animal Husbandry officers for corrective action.

Two of their other findings sit awkwardly against the framing of this piece, which is a reason to include them rather than not.

The first is about scale, and it is a corrective to writing about Indian animal welfare through the lens of the courts. The report puts the number of layer hens alive at any moment at roughly 627 million, more than 85% of them on commercial farms where cages are widespread. Some 3.4 billion broiler chickens are slaughtered a year, and fast-growing breeds make up 97.6% of commercial production. The report extrapolates that around 320 million male chicks are killed annually. Shrimp production of about a million tonnes represents somewhere between 21 and 94 billion individuals. Fin-fish exceed ten billion a year and have, by the report’s account, the weakest evidence base of any major farmed group. Against those numbers, an estimated 90% of organised animal welfare groups in India work on stray and companion animals.

The second is about litigation, and it complicates the story this piece opens with. We began with Nagaraja and the Law Commission as evidence that the question had an answer a decade ago. The report’s reading is that litigation “has overpromised and underdelivered”, because judgments “have arrived before social, market, and political alignment existed to sustain them”. On that account the unrevised ₹50 fine is a sign of a win that arrived ahead of the conditions that would make it stick, which is a less comfortable diagnosis and points at slower work than notifying a rule.

There is also a finding here for anyone who designs campaigns rather than reads about them. The report records that Indian stakeholders “consistently report that asks framed around animal welfare alone face structural resistance, while the same underlying reform framed around farmer livelihoods, child nutrition, food safety, public health, employment, or environmental protection travels much further”, and that the religious and caste dimensions of food politics make this sharper still: asks that read as Western, Hindu-vegetarian or implicitly anti-Muslim carry substantial risk. Framing, in their words, is “a strategic variable, not a cosmetic one”. That is a claim about what works, made by people who asked practitioners, and it is testable in a way most advocacy advice is not.

Animal Ask are explicit about the limits of the exercise: substantial evidence gaps remain for aquatic animals, insects and wild populations; several interventions are exploratory; and they note that as a team working from outside India they will lack local knowledge that consultation can reduce but not remove. They ask that the seven areas be read as a starting framework rather than a fixed hierarchy. The full report is available as a PDF from the page linked above, and it is the version of record.

Where the sources are

Most of the figures above are drawn from the reading list assembled for this: Farm Animal Welfare in India, fourteen readings across the livestock data, the statute, the judgments and the peer-reviewed welfare literature, each with its source and year.

The inflation series is the Consumer Price Index for Agricultural Labourers, published by the Labour Bureau of the Ministry of Labour and Employment, base 1986-87 = 100, All India, December of each year. It was chosen over the general CPI because the penalty was written against a rural economy, and because the series reaches further back than the current CPI base.
