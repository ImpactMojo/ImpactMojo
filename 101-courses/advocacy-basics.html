Section One
What Advocacy Is
CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 · ImpactMojo 101 Series03www.impactmojo.in
ImpactMojoAdvocacy Basics 101www.impactmojo.in
Definition
Advocacy is the work of shifting power
Advocacy is the deliberate effort to influence the decisions, policies, practices and behaviours of those who hold power — so that public systems work better for people who are excluded. It is about changing the rules, not just delivering within them.
Advocacy
A planned process to influence policy, practice, power relations and social norms in favour of a marginalised group or cause — by changing what decision-makers decide and what is considered normal.
Advocacy asks a sharper question than service delivery: not 'how do we help these people?' but 'why are they excluded, and who can change that?'
Advocacy asksService delivery asks
Why does this need exist?How do we meet this need?
Who decided it would be this way?Who can we reach this year?
What rule would have to change?What can we do within the rule?
Whose voice is missing from the room?Who is missing from the queue?
Both sets of questions are legitimate and they lead to entirely different organisations. The distinction is not about ambition — it is about which lever the work is designed to pull.
Most organisations do both, badly, by treating advocacy as something the communications team does after the programme. Advocacy needs its own strategy, its own timeline and its own indicators.

Four Targets
What advocacy tries to change
Policy
Laws, schemes, budgets, official rules
Practice
How institutions actually behave day to day
Power
Who decides, and whose voice counts
Norms
What a society treats as normal or acceptable
A good law that is never implemented is a practice problem; a stigma that survives the law is a norms problem. Advocacy may need all four.
TargetChange looks likeTypical timeframe
PolicyA law, scheme, budget line or official ruleYears
PracticeInstitutions behaving differently day to dayYears, after the policy

Most campaigns win at the policy level and stall at practice, which is why a legal victory can leave conditions on the ground unchanged for a decade. Adoption and implementation are separate fights.
Norms work is the slowest and least fundable, and it is what makes the other three durable. A policy that runs against prevailing norms is reversed as soon as attention moves elsewhere.

In India the sharper constraint is charitable and foreign-funding status rather than lobbying registration. Getting the terminology right is part of managing that risk.

Lobbying, Precisely
Lobbying is the narrowest tool
Lobbying
Direct, private communication with legislators or officials to influence a specific law, regulation, budget line or decision. It is one tactic inside advocacy — not a synonym for it.
The distinction is legal as well as conceptual: in many jurisdictions lobbying is regulated and reportable, and a charity's freedom to lobby may be restricted. Broad public advocacy usually is not lobbying.
Lobbying involvesAdvocacy also includes
A specific bill, rule or budget lineNorms and public opinion
Named legislators or officialsCommunities, media, courts
Private communicationPublic campaigning
A defined decision momentYears of agenda-setting before one exists
Lobbying is the narrowest tactic and gets used as the name for the whole field, usually pejoratively. Keeping the terms distinct protects the rest of the work from that association.
It also matters for what you can register as. Charitable status and lobbying rules differ by jurisdiction, and describing campaigning as lobbying can create a compliance problem that did not exist.

Who Does It
Advocacy is for everyone, not just experts
  • Communities advocating for their own rights — the strongest legitimacy
  • NGOs & networks with reach, evidence and convening power
  • Coalitions that pool numbers and credibility
  • Researchers & media who put issues on the agenda
  • Insiders — sympathetic officials and reformers within the system
The most durable advocacy is led by the people affected, not done for them. We return to that distinction in Section Two.
ActorBringsLacks
Affected communitiesLegitimacy nothing else suppliesAccess, time, protection
NGOs and networksReach, evidence, convening powerStanding to speak for anyone
CoalitionsNumbers and breadthSpeed; a single voice
Researchers and mediaCredibility; agenda-settingA stake in the outcome
Sympathetic insidersTiming and inside knowledgeAbility to be seen helping
No actor has everything, which is the structural argument for coalitions. The combination that wins is usually community legitimacy plus NGO capacity plus an insider who knows when the door opens.
Insiders are the most valuable and least visible asset in the list. Protecting them — never naming them, never using them for a quick win — is a discipline campaigns often fail at once and never recover from.

Inside or Outside
Insider vs outsider advocacy
Insider
Quiet engagement with decision-makers: consultations, expert committees, private meetings, technical inputs. Access and trust; risk of co-option.
Outsider
Public pressure from outside: protest, media, petitions, mobilisation, litigation. Independence and visibility; risk of being shut out.
Most effective advocacy is both at once — an inside-track negotiator backed by an outside-track that makes the negotiator's offer look reasonable.
InsiderOutsider
CurrencyTrust and technical credibilityNumbers and public attention
RiskCo-option; softening the askLosing access entirely
Works whenThe target is willing but constrainedThe target is unwilling
Fails whenYou mistake being heard for being heededThe target cannot deliver even under pressure
The insider failure mode is subtle: you are consulted, thanked, and nothing changes. Being in the room is not influence, and the invitation itself can be the concession.
Most campaigns run both, through different organisations. The pairing works because the insider can say "I cannot control them" — a leverage that disappears if the same body does both.

Four broad approaches
Lobbying
Direct influence on officials & legislators
Campaigning
Sustained public push to build pressure
Litigation

Mobilisation
Organising people to act collectively
Few campaigns use only one. They are sequenced and combined — research to mobilisation to lobbying, with litigation held in reserve as leverage.
ApproachTime to effectReversibility of the win
LobbyingFast, if a window is openEasily reversed by the next official
CampaigningMonths to yearsMore durable — a constituency exists
LitigationYearsBinding, but enforcement is separate
MobilisationSlowestMost durable — power, not just outcome
Speed and durability trade off directly. The fastest route produces the win most easily undone, which is why campaigns that lobby alone find themselves relitigating the same issue every few years.
Sequencing exploits the trade-off: mobilise to build the constituency, campaign to create pressure, lobby to close, litigate when the route shuts. Each stage makes the next one cheaper.

The Courts
Litigation & PIL in South Asia
Public Interest Litigation (PIL) lets citizens and groups ask courts to enforce rights on behalf of those who cannot reach the court themselves. In India it has shaped rights to food, education and a clean environment.
Litigation can win a binding order overnight — but courts cannot run programmes. A judgment is the start of an implementation fight, not the end of advocacy.
Litigation offersAt the cost of
A binding order, without winning politicsYears, and legal expense
A forum where evidence countsA remedy the court cannot enforce alone
Standing for those excluded elsewhereA judgment that may be ignored
Agenda-setting attentionAntagonising a government you must later work with
Indian PIL has produced landmark orders on food, education and environment, and the implementation record is uneven. A judgment is a lever, not a result, and the follow-up litigation is usually longer than the original case.
Use litigation when the political route is genuinely closed, or to create leverage for negotiation. Filing first, before any engagement, forecloses the cheaper options.

Mass Action
Social mobilisation
Social mobilisation organises affected people into a visible, collective force — marches, jan sunwais (public hearings), dharnas, mass petitions. Numbers are the currency that decision-makers cannot ignore.
Jan Sunwai (public hearing)
A South Asian social-audit tool where officials answer to citizens in the open, comparing records against people's testimony. It turns abstract accountability into a face-to-face demand.
Jan sunwai elementWhy it works
Official records read aloudDiscrepancies are verified by those present
Held in the villageThose named can answer, or fail to
Officials invitedRefusal to attend is itself a public fact
A public record of the proceedingsA document to escalate with
Verification in public is what distinguishes a social audit from an allegation. A discrepancy confirmed by the people the record describes cannot be dismissed as motivated complaint.
Social audit is now written into MGNREGA’s design, which is unusual: a movement tactic became statutory procedure. It also means the quality varies enormously by state.

A Landmark
MKSS, the RTI movement & social audit
In Rajasthan, the Mazdoor Kisan Shakti Sangathan (MKSS) used public hearings to expose corruption in local works — building grassroots demand that helped drive India's Right to Information Act, 2005.
It shows the full repertoire in sequence: mobilisation generated evidence, evidence fuelled a campaign, the campaign won a law — and the law became a permanent tool for the next fight.
MKSS sequenceWhat it did
MobilisationOrganised workers around unpaid wages
Jan sunwaiRead out muster rolls in public, against people present
EvidenceDocumented discrepancies as a record, not an allegation
CampaignBuilt a demand for the right to information
LawContributed to the Right to Information Act, 2005
The public hearing worked because verification happened in front of the people the records described. A muster roll listing a dead man is unanswerable when read aloud in his village.
The sequence is the lesson: a local, concrete grievance became a national statutory right over roughly a decade. Nothing in it started as a legislative campaign.

Whose Voice
Advocacy WITH vs advocacy FOR communities
Advocacy WITH
Affected people set the agenda, lead, and speak. The organisation accompanies and amplifies. Builds power that outlasts the project.
Advocacy FOR
Outsiders speak on people's behalf. Faster, but risks substituting expert voice for lived voice — and leaves no power behind.
Default to with; use for only where speaking out is unsafe for those affected — and even then, on their terms.
Advocacy WITHAdvocacy FOR
Sets the agendaThe affected communityThe organisation
SpeaksCommunity representativesStaff or experts
SpeedSlowerFaster
What remains afterOrganised, confident peopleA policy win and no local capacity
Legitimacy if challengedStrongFragile
The fourth row is the honest test. Ask what the community can do next year that it could not do last year — if the answer is nothing, the campaign built power for the organisation rather than for them.
Advocacy FOR is sometimes the right choice: where speaking out is dangerous for the affected, an outsider taking the risk is appropriate. That is a decision to make with them, not on their behalf.

Representation
'Nothing about us without us'
Nothing about us without us.
— disability-rights movement, now a wider principle of legitimate advocacy
Legitimacy comes from proximity to the problem. Before you speak, ask: who gave me this mandate, and would the affected community recognise themselves in what I am saying?
Ask yourselfIf the answer is no
Who gave me this mandate?Get one, or say plainly you have none
Would they recognise this ask?Take it back to them
Is anyone affected in the room?Fix that before the next meeting
Who reports back to them?Build the route; it is not automatic
"Nothing about us without us" came from the disability-rights movement and now applies far beyond it. It is a claim about who is competent to define the problem, not only about courtesy in consultation.
Having no mandate is a workable position if stated honestly — researchers and journalists advocate without one. What damages credibility is implying a representativeness you do not have.

Choosing
There is no single 'right' approach
  • Match the approach to the target — courts, ministers, the public differ
  • Match it to your power — access, numbers, evidence or moral authority
  • Match it to the risk — what is safe for your constituents
  • Sequence & combine — insider and outsider, evidence and pressure
The choice is strategic, not ideological. The rest of this course is about making it deliberately.
Match the approach toQuestion
The targetWhat actually moves this person or body?
Your powerDo you have access, numbers, evidence or standing?
The riskWho bears the consequence — you or your constituents?
The momentIs a window open, or are you building for one?
The third question is the one organisations skip. The risk of a confrontational tactic is rarely borne by the staff who chose it; it falls on the people whose names and faces are visible locally.
Sequencing beats picking. Evidence first to earn a hearing, quiet engagement to test willingness, public pressure when it stalls, litigation when the route closes — each step preserving the option of the next.

The Core
Advocacy is applied power analysis
Every advocacy problem is, underneath, a question of power: who has it, how it works, and how it can shift. Before strategy, analyse power — otherwise you push on the wrong door.
This section borrows two classic frameworks: Steven Lukes' three faces of power and the activist-friendly power-over / with / to / within typology, plus John Gaventa's power cube.
Wrong assumption about powerConsequence
The target has authority they lackMonths spent lobbying someone who cannot deliver
The decision is made where it is announcedYou address the announcer, not the decider
Opposition is a single blocYou miss the split that would have won it
The issue is contestedIt is not on any agenda at all — a different problem
The first row is the commonest and most expensive error in advocacy. Formal authority and actual decision-making power diverge routinely, especially in bureaucracies with a strong finance department.
Test it early with a small ask. If your target cannot deliver something minor within their stated remit, they will not deliver the main ask either.

It is also the slowest and the least fundable, because it produces no policy outcome for years. Campaigns that skip it find they have no constituency when the window opens.

Four expressions of power
FormMeaningFor advocacy

ExpressionWhat a campaign does with it
Power OVERThe thing being contested — redistribute it

Only the first is zero-sum. The other three grow when exercised, which is why a campaign can lose its policy fight and still leave a community materially more powerful than it found them.
Power-within is the precondition for the rest and the least measurable. It is also what the third face of power destroys — people who believe nothing can change do not organise.

Positive Power
Not all power is domination
It is tempting to treat power as a dirty word. But power-with, power-to and power-within are exactly what advocacy builds. The goal is not to abolish power but to redistribute and democratise it.
A campaign that wins a policy but leaves the community no more confident or organised has spent power-over without building power-with. Ask what power your work creates.
Campaign outcomePower-overPower-with / to / within
Policy won, community not organisedShifted a littleUnchanged
Policy lost, community organisedUnchangedSubstantially built
BothShiftedBuilt — and defends the win
The middle row is a real result, not a consolation. A community that emerged organised and confident from a lost campaign is positioned to win the next one, which the first row is not.
This is why funder reporting that counts only policy wins distorts practice. It rewards the first row and penalises the second, when the second is often the better long-run investment.

Spaces
Closed, invited and claimed spaces
  • Closed: decisions made behind shut doors — you are not in the room
  • Invited: you are asked in — consultations, committees (watch for tokenism)
  • Claimed / created: spaces people open themselves — movements, public hearings
Strategy follows the space: prise open a closed space, use an invited one without being co-opted, and build claimed spaces where none exist.
SpaceTactic that fitsTrap
ClosedPrise it open — RTI, litigation, mediaAssuming a meeting exists to attend
InvitedShow up prepared; bring evidenceTokenism — consulted, then ignored
Claimed / createdBuild and defend it — hearings, movementsIsolation from any decision-making forum
The invited-space trap is the one to watch for. Consultation can absorb energy and confer legitimacy on a decision already taken, and refusing the invitation is sometimes the stronger move.
Test an invited space by asking what changed after the last one. If the previous consultation produced no visible alteration to the proposal, treat the invitation as a public-relations exercise.

Step 1
Research: know the problem and the power
Good advocacy rests on two kinds of homework: evidence about the problem (its scale, causes, who it hurts) and power analysis about the decision (who decides, what moves them).
  • What exactly is wrong, and for whom?
  • What are the root causes (Section Five)?
  • Who has the power to change it (Section Six)?
  • What is the realistic, winnable ask?
Research questionEvidence needed
What is wrong, and for whom?Data on scale and distribution
What is the root cause?Analysis, not just description
Who decides?Rules of business; who signs, who funds
What has been tried?Previous attempts, and why they failed
What would it cost?A costing, ready before you are asked
The fourth row is the cheapest research and the most often skipped. Most issues have been campaigned on before, and knowing why the last attempt failed is worth more than new data on the problem.
Power research is different work from problem research and usually needs different people — someone who reads rules of business and budget documents, not survey data.

Step 2
Strategy: turn analysis into a plan
Strategy connects your goal to your context: a clear objective, the targets who can deliver it, the tactics that move them, and a theory of change that links them (Section Seven).
Most advocacy fails not from weak passion but from skipping strategy — rushing to a march before deciding who it is meant to move, and how.
Strategy questionA weak answer sounds like
What exactly do we want?"More attention to the issue"
Who can grant it?"The government"
What moves them?"Raising awareness"
Why would this work?"Because it is the right thing"
How will we know?"When the policy changes"
Every weak answer is vague about an actor. "The government" cannot say yes; a named official in a named department, with the authority and the budget line, can.
If you cannot name the person who signs, you do not yet have a strategy. That single test eliminates most of what gets called advocacy planning.

Problem vs Issue
A problem is broad; an issue is actionable
Problem
Big, complex, structural — 'child malnutrition'. Important, but too large to campaign on directly.
Issue
A specific, solvable slice with a clear decision-maker — 'the district must fill 200 vacant anganwadi-worker posts'.
You advocate on issues, not problems. The craft of framing is cutting a winnable issue out of an overwhelming problem.

Framing
The choice of which aspect of an issue to emphasise — the values, metaphors and storyline through which people understand it. The same facts, framed differently, mobilise different audiences.

From need to right

Caution

Framing chooses emphasis; it must never distort facts. Exaggeration that is later exposed destroys the credibility that is an advocate's core asset — and it is hard to rebuild.

Adoption is not implementation

The Message
Craft a clear, repeatable message
  • Problem: what is wrong, in one line
  • Solution: the specific change you want
  • Action: exactly what the audience should do

Why It's Hard
Advocacy is hard to measure — honestly
  • Long & non-linear: change can take years and arrive in leaps
  • Many actors: you are one of many pushing the same way
  • Attribution: who can claim a policy that 'everyone' wanted?
  • Counterfactual: would it have changed anyway?
These are real difficulties, not excuses. They mean advocacy needs different measurement tools — not none.
DifficultyHonest response
Long and non-linearTrack intermediate markers, not just the win
Many actors pushing the same wayClaim contribution, never sole credit
Attribution is impossibleUse contribution analysis or process tracing
No counterfactualDocument the causal chain instead of a comparison
These are real methodological difficulties, not excuses. Advocacy cannot be evaluated with the tools built for service delivery, and pretending otherwise produces either false claims or nothing.
The absence of a counterfactual is genuine: you cannot run a control country. What you can do is trace the mechanism — show the chain, dated, with evidence at each link.

Shift the Question
Contribution, not attribution
Attribution
'We caused this change.' Almost never provable in advocacy — too many hands on the lever.
Contribution
'We plausibly helped, and here is the evidence of how.' Honest, defensible, and far more useful.
Credible advocacy evaluation claims a contribution to change, traced through a plausible story — not sole credit.
ClaimDefensible?
"We caused this policy change"Almost never
"We contributed, and here is how"Yes, with documented markers
