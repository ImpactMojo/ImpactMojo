What ‘Post-Truth’ Means
CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 · ImpactMojo 101 Series03www.impactmojo.in

Word of the Year
'Post-truth' is the word of an era
In 2016, in the wake of Brexit and the US election, Oxford Dictionaries named post-truth its Word of the Year — reporting a roughly twenty-fold spike in usage over the previous year.
Post-truth
Relating to circumstances in which objective facts are less influential in shaping public opinion than appeals to emotion and personal belief. (Oxford Dictionaries Word of the Year, 2016.)
'Post' here does not mean facts have vanished. It means they no longer settle the argument.
What the definition rules inWhat it rules out
Facts still exist and are still checkableThe claim that truth has become impossible
They have stopped deciding the argumentThe idea that people are simply ignorant
Emotion and belonging do the decidingBlaming any single platform or party
A condition of the public sphereA description of individual character
The word is contested, and reasonably so. Critics point out that elites have manipulated publics for as long as there have been publics, and that “post-truth” can flatter the present with a false sense of novelty. Keep the objection in view: what follows argues that the infrastructure is new, not the impulse.
Watch how the term itself gets used. Like “fake news” before it, “post-truth” is now sometimes deployed to dismiss an opponent rather than to describe a condition. A word that can be used as an insult stops being useful as a diagnosis.

The Shift
When feelings outrank facts
Post-truth is not simply lying — lying is as old as speech. What is new is a public sphere where how a claim makes you feel, and whose side it signals, matter more than whether it is true.
Old contest
Rival claims judged against shared evidence; being caught wrong carried a cost.
Post-truth contest
Claims judged by identity and emotion; a fact-check can even harden belief.
QuestionOld contestPost-truth contest
What settles it?Evidence both sides acceptWhich side the claim flatters
Cost of being caught wrongReputational, and realOften none; sometimes a gain
Who referees?Press, courts, expert bodiesNobody both sides trust
Effect of a correctionUsually closes the questionCan be read as an attack by the other team
The second row is the load-bearing one. Lying is old; what changes a system is whether being caught carries a price. When a falsehood signals loyalty, exposure can raise a speaker’s standing with their own side rather than lower it.
Practical implication for anyone communicating: the shared referee is the thing to rebuild, and it cannot be rebuilt by winning an argument. It is rebuilt by being the source that was right when it was inconvenient.

Not New, but Newly Powerful
Old impulse, new infrastructure
Rumour, propaganda and demagoguery are ancient. What amplifies them now is infrastructure: smartphones in billions of pockets, algorithmic feeds, group chats and an economy that pays for attention.

Read the right-hand column as the argument of this whole deck. Every barrier that once slowed a falsehood was a cost — of printing, of licensing, of labour. Each has fallen to roughly nothing, while the cost of verifying a claim has stayed where it was: a person, doing work, taking time.
That asymmetry is the whole problem in one line. Production got free; verification did not. Any response that relies on out-producing falsehood is arguing against arithmetic.

Truth Decay
Four symptoms of a post-truth public sphere
  • Disagreement about facts, not just values — even on the verifiable
  • A blurred line between opinion and fact in how news is consumed
  • Rising volume and influence of opinion over reporting
  • Declining trust in once-respected sources of information
The RAND Corporation calls this cluster 'Truth Decay'. None of the four requires anyone to tell an outright lie.
SymptomWhat it looks like in practice
Disagreement about factsTwo sides dispute a published number, not what to do about it
Blurred opinion and factA panel show and a report land in the same feed, in the same format
Volume of opinion risingCommentary is cheap to produce; reporting is not
Declining trust in sources“They all have an agenda” becomes the default posture
None of the four requires a lie, which is what makes the diagnosis useful. A public sphere can arrive at truth decay through ordinary commercial and technological pressure, with every individual actor behaving legally and most of them honestly.
The third symptom is the cheapest to fix and the least attempted: mark opinion as opinion, visibly, including your own organisation’s. Most institutional communication blurs the line as much as any platform does.

Why It Matters Here
South Asia is a front line, not a footnote
With hundreds of millions of new, mobile-first internet users, dozens of languages and dense kinship and faith networks, South Asia is where post-truth dynamics play out at their largest human scale — often with life-and-death stakes.
Mobile-first
Most users came online on a phone, via cheap data, into closed messaging groups
Many languages
Falsehood travels in tongues most fact-checking tools barely cover
ConditionWhy it raises the stakes here
Mobile-first, cheap dataHundreds of millions arrived online through a phone and a group chat, without a decade of desktop web habits
Closed messagingThe dominant channel is encrypted and invisible to outside researchers and fact-checkers alike
Many languagesModeration and detection tooling is thinnest exactly where the population is largest
Dense kinship networksA forward carries the credibility of the person, not the source
Existing fault linesCaste, religion and region give disinformation ready-made targets
The consequence is not abstract. Section 10 covers documented cases where a forwarded rumour ended in a lynching. Treat this slide as the reason the rest of the deck is operational rather than academic.
The fourth row is the one outsiders miss. Interventions designed for open platforms assume a visible post that can be labelled. In a family group there is nothing to label and nobody outside to label it.

Precision Helps
Not all falsehood is the same
Lumping everything together as 'fake news' hides what matters: was it meant to deceive, and was it meant to harm? Those two questions sort the field into three useful categories.
The term 'fake news' is now so weaponised — used to dismiss inconvenient reporting — that researchers prefer the sharper words that follow.
Why “fake news” is a bad working term
It collapses an honest mistake and a paid influence operation into one word
It says nothing about intent, which is what decides the response
It has been captured as an insult for reporting somebody dislikes
It implies the problem is “news”, when most falsehood is not in news form at all
The two questions that do the sorting are simply: is it false, and was harm intended? Everything in the next four slides follows from those. They are worth memorising, because they can be applied in the moment to a message you have just received.
Note the asymmetry in how the term travels. Researchers have largely abandoned “fake news”; politicians have not. That divergence is itself a small case study in a word being more useful as a weapon than as a description.

The Core Distinction
Mis-, dis- and mal-information
TypeFalse?Intent to harm?Example
MisinformationYesNoSharing a wrong cure, believing it true
DisinformationYesYesA fabricated rumour seeded to incite a riot
MalinformationNo (mostly true)YesLeaking private data to harm someone
Framework: Wardle & Derakhshan (Council of Europe, 2017). The axes are falseness and intent to harm.
Same content, three categoriesWhich one, and why
An aunt forwards a false cure she believesMisinformation — false, no intent to harm
The same text, written by someone selling the cureDisinformation — false, and knowingly so
A real leaked medical record posted to shame someoneMalinformation — true, and deployed to harm
Notice that the first two rows are the same words. The category is a property of the actor and the moment, not of the text, which is why you cannot classify a message by reading it alone — and why automated classification of “disinformation” is a much harder problem than it sounds.
In a chain of forwards, the category changes hands. What began as disinformation becomes misinformation the moment it reaches someone who believes it. That person is a target, not an author, and treating them as an author is how corrections fail.

Misinformation
False, but shared in good faith
Misinformation
False or misleading information spread without intent to deceive. The sharer believes it is true — an anxious relative forwarding a bogus health tip.
Most of what floods family group chats is misinformation, not disinformation. It is sincere — and that sincerity is exactly what makes it travel.
Correcting a relative — what worksWhat backfires
In private, not in the groupPublic correction, which makes it about face
Credit the motive: they were trying to help“How could you believe that?”
Give a replacement fact, not just a denial“That’s fake” and nothing else
Show them the check, so they can do it next timeDoing the check for them, forever
Sincerity is the propellant. A forward from an anxious relative arrives wrapped in evident care, and care is far more persuasive than argument. This is why the same false message travels further in a family group than on an open platform.
The last row is the one that compounds. Teaching one person in a group to reverse-search an image converts a spreader into a check — and family groups have exactly the density that makes one such person effective.

Disinformation
False, and weaponised on purpose
Disinformation
False information created and spread deliberately to deceive, manipulate or harm — for political, financial or social gain.
Disinformation is engineered. It is seeded by actors who know it is false and design it to exploit a fault line — a communal tension, an election, a fear.
Marks of an engineered campaignWhat to look for
TimingAppears on polling eve, or hours after an incident, before facts exist
CoordinationIdentical wording across accounts that share no other interest
TargetingAimed at a fault line rather than at a topic
ProductionBetter made than the surrounding organic content
BeneficiaryAsk who gains if this is believed — and who gains if it is merely doubted
The last row is the most useful and the least used. “Who benefits?” is not proof of anything and is an excellent first question, because engineered falsehood almost always has a beneficiary while an honest mistake usually does not.
Do not over-apply it. The same question, used carelessly, produces conspiracy theory: a beneficiary can always be found for any claim. It is a prompt for checking, not a substitute for it.

Malinformation
True, but deployed to wound
Malinformation
Genuine information taken out of context, or private information exposed, in order to cause harm — doxxing, leaks, selective truths.
Malinformation is the trickiest, because the facts check out. The deception is in the framing, the timing and the omission.
FormExample
Private information exposedHome address of a journalist published to invite harassment
Real quote, missing contextA clause lifted from a speech that reverses its meaning
True but selectiveOne accurate statistic chosen because the other four do not fit
True, and timedA genuine old case surfaced the week it will do most damage
Malinformation defeats fact-checking by design. Every individual claim survives verification, so a checker working claim by claim will rate it true. The deception lives in selection and framing, which no per-claim check examines.
It is also where legitimate journalism and malinformation get closest. Publishing a true, damaging fact about a powerful person is accountability; publishing a true, damaging fact about a private one to silence them is not. The distinction is public interest, and it has to be argued rather than detected.

The Family of Falsehood
Rumour, propaganda, hoax, satire
FormWhat it is
RumourUnverified claim passed on, often filling an information vacuum
PropagandaInformation shaped to promote a cause, true or false
HoaxA deliberate fabrication designed to be believed
Satire / parodyFalse on purpose, but meant to be understood as a joke
ClickbaitMisleading framing engineered to harvest clicks
Conspiracy theoryAn all-explaining secret-plot narrative, resistant to evidence
FormWhat it needs to work
RumourAn information vacuum — it fills a gap officials left open
PropagandaRepetition and reach; truth is optional, consistency is not
HoaxPlausible detail and a believable messenger
SatireAn audience that shares the joke’s context
ClickbaitA gap between the headline and the article that pays

The first row is the most actionable finding in this section. Rumour fills vacuums, so the fastest way to reduce it after an incident is to say something true and specific early, even if it is only “here is what we know and when we will know more”. Silence is not neutral; it is space.
The last row explains why arguing rarely works. A theory built to absorb disconfirmation treats your evidence as recruitment. What sometimes moves a person is a relationship, over time, not a better fact.

Satire's Edge Case
When a joke stops being read as a joke
Satire is legitimate — until it is screenshotted, stripped of its source and reshared as fact. Then a parody headline becomes, in effect, misinformation by accident.
Context collapse: when content jumps platforms and audiences, the cues that signalled 'this is a joke' fall away.
Cue that marked it as a jokeWhat happens when it travels
The publication’s name and reputationCropped out of the screenshot
The surrounding page and its other storiesGone; only the headline survives
An audience that knows the formatReplaced by one that does not
Absurdity as the signalReads as plausible to someone primed to expect it
Context collapse is the general mechanism, and satire is only its clearest case. Anything written for one audience — an internal memo, a joke among colleagues, a simplification for a class — can be lifted into an audience it was never written for, where its cues mean nothing.
The response is not censorship. It is restoring the missing context: linking the original, naming the publication, showing the crop. A satirical piece that has been stripped needs its frame put back, not the piece taken down.

Why Labels Matter
The right name shapes the right response
  • Misinformation → correct gently; the sharer is not the enemy
  • Disinformation → expose the source and the motive, not just the claim
  • Malinformation → challenge the framing and protect the harmed
  • Satire → restore the missing context, don't censor the joke
Diagnosis before treatment. The category tells you whether to educate, to debunk or to defend.
DiagnosisResponseWhat to avoid
MisinformationCorrect privately, credit the motive, supply a replacementTreating the sharer as an opponent
DisinformationExpose the operation and the beneficiaryRepeating the claim while debunking it
MalinformationChallenge the framing; protect the person targetedArguing the facts, which are not in dispute
Satire misreadRestore the source and contextDemanding removal, which creates a grievance
The commonest operational error is treating row one as row two — responding to a worried relative as though they were running an influence operation. It loses the relationship, which was the only channel you had.
The second commonest is the reverse: patiently fact-checking a coordinated campaign claim by claim, as though it were a misunderstanding. That is the response the campaign is designed to absorb.

Old Wisdom
'A lie travels halfway round the world…'
A lie can travel halfway around the world while the truth is still putting on its shoes.
— widely attributed to Mark Twain (and to Swift before him); the attribution itself is disputed
Fittingly, the quote's own origin is a small case study in how a tidy story outruns the messy truth.
Attributed toThe record
Mark TwainNo contemporary source; the attribution appears long after his death
Jonathan Swift (1710)Wrote something close in The Examiner: falsehood flies, and truth comes limping after
Various politiciansRepeated so often that repetition became the evidence — see Section 5
Use this as the deck’s worked example. A neat, quotable line attached to a famous name outran a duller and more complicated history, and it did so among exactly the people who spend their days worrying about misattribution. Nobody was lying. That is the point.
A useful habit follows: when a quotation is unusually well-phrased for the point you want to make, that is the moment to check it, not the moment to use it.

The Surprise Premium
Why falsehood out-runs fact
The same study found false stories were markedly more novel than true ones, and inspired more surprise and disgust. Truth is often dull; lies can be tailored to be thrilling.
  • Novelty grabs attention and feels worth sharing
  • Strong emotion — fear, anger, awe — drives the forward button
  • The truth must compete while bound by being true
What the 2018 study foundWhy it matters
False stories reached more people, faster, and deeper into networksThe gap was largest for political stories
False stories were measurably more novelNovelty is what makes a share feel worth making
They provoked surprise and disgust; true ones provoked sadness and trustThe winning emotions are the shareable ones
Humans, not bots, did most of the spreadingRemoving bots would not have closed the gap
The last row is the finding people skip. It is more comfortable to locate the problem in automated accounts than in ordinary users forwarding things, and the study points the other way. Any intervention aimed only at bots is aimed at the smaller half.

The Closed-Loop Problem
Encrypted chats: trusted, untraceable
Why it spreads
  • Forwarded by people you trust
  • No visible like-count or fact-check label
  • End-to-end encryption hides the origin
Why it's hard to fight
  • Outsiders can't see what's circulating
  • Corrections rarely reach the same group
  • Speed outpaces any moderator
Forward-limit features (capping how many chats a message can be relayed to) slow, but do not stop, viral falsehood.
Open platformClosed group
Content is visible to researchersNobody outside can see what is circulating

The third row is why closed-network falsehood is harder, not easier, to counter. The message arrives pre-endorsed by someone the recipient trusts about everything else. No platform label can compete with that, which is why the interventions that work here are social rather than technical.
What does work: forward limits, which reduce velocity without needing to see content; and one competent person inside the group who checks things and is liked enough to be listened to. The second is not a policy, which is why it is under-resourced.

Bots
Automated accounts at scale
Bot
An automated account that posts, likes or reshares without a human behind each action — used to manufacture the illusion of popularity or consensus.
Bots rarely persuade you directly. Their job is to amplify — to make a fringe view trend, so that real humans assume it is mainstream and pass it on.
What bots are good atWhat they are bad at
Manufacturing the appearance of volumeChanging a specific person’s mind
Pushing a term into trendingSustaining a conversation under scrutiny
Drowning a hashtag so its real users leavePassing as human at close range
Making a fringe view look mainstreamOperating in closed groups, where entry needs a person
Bots work on you through other humans. The mechanism is social proof: seeing a view apparently held by many raises the odds that a real person adopts and repeats it. The bot’s target is not the reader but the reader’s estimate of what everyone thinks.
Beware casual bot accusations. “That account is a bot” has become a way of dismissing a person who disagrees, and detection by eye is unreliable. Assert it only with evidence of coordination, not on the basis of an opinion you find implausible.

Coordinated Inauthentic Behaviour
The troll farm and the IT cell
Coordinated inauthentic behaviour (CIB)
Groups of accounts working together to mislead about who they are and what they are doing — the platform term for organised influence operations.
Whether called a 'troll farm', an 'IT cell' or a 'PR operation', the pattern is the same: paid or volunteer networks manufacturing consensus and drowning out dissent.
Signal of coordinationWhy it is hard to fake away
Identical or near-identical text across accountsCheap to fix now with generative tools — so weaker than it was
Synchronised timing in burstsRequires deliberate staggering to hide
Accounts created in the same windowAgeing an account costs time, which campaigns rarely have
Shared infrastructure — links, images, phone prefixesOperational convenience leaves traces
Note what the first row concedes. The classic tell — copy-pasted wording — is exactly what generative text removes. A thousand distinct, fluent, on-message posts now cost about what one used to, so detection is shifting from content to behaviour and infrastructure.
The term is deliberately about behaviour, not opinion. Platforms act on the deception about who is speaking, not on the view expressed — a distinction worth keeping, because the alternative is a platform adjudicating opinions.

The Coined Term
Eli Pariser and the 'filter bubble'
Filter bubble
The intellectual isolation that results when algorithms selectively show you content they predict you'll like — quietly editing out the rest. Coined by activist Eli Pariser (2011).
Pariser's worry: personalisation creates a unique universe of information for each of us, and we rarely notice what has been filtered away.
What personalisation optimisesWhat it quietly removes
Predicted engagement with each itemThe item you would have argued with
Time spent, returns per dayThe boring but important story
Similarity to what you clicked beforeThe view held by people unlike you
Nothing about accuracy, either wayAny signal that you are seeing a selection at all
The last row is Pariser’s actual worry, and it is subtler than “the algorithm shows me bad things”. It is that the editing is invisible: a feed presents itself as a window rather than as a selection, and no version of it announces what it dropped.
Test it on yourself. Open the same platform in a private window, logged out, and compare. The difference between the two is the bubble, made briefly visible.

Bubble vs Chamber
Two related, distinct ideas
Filter bubble

We collect evidence for our side
Confirmation bias
The tendency to search for, interpret and recall information in a way that confirms what we already believe — and to discount what doesn't.

Motivated Reasoning
Reasoning toward the answer we want

Using our reasoning powers not to find the truth, but to justify a conclusion we are already emotionally committed to.

The finding that simply repeating a statement increases the likelihood that people will judge it true — even when they know better, and even for implausible claims.

The tendency for misinformation to keep shaping our reasoning even after we have heard, and accepted, a correction.

A pattern in which people with low ability in a domain tend to overestimate that ability — they lack the very skill needed to see their own gaps.

The New Terrain
When seeing is no longer believing
For a century, a photograph or recording was strong evidence. Synthetic media — content created or altered by software — is dissolving that trust, at falling cost and rising quality.
The deepest danger is not any single fake. It is the erosion of the shared assumption that recordings show something real.

Prebunking

Prebunking
Forewarning and refuting a manipulation technique before people meet it — building mental resistance, like a vaccine builds immunity.
Inoculation theory traces to psychologist William McGuire in the 1960s and has been revived for the digital age by Sander van der Linden and colleagues.
DebunkingPrebunking
After exposureBefore exposure
Addresses one claimAddresses a technique, so it generalises
Competes with a belief already heldArrives first, which is most of the advantage
Effect fades and must be repeated per claimEffect fades too, and covers claims not yet invented
Inoculation theory goes back to William McGuire in the 1960s and has been revived and tested at scale in the digital era, including through short videos shown before content. The results are consistent and modest — real effects that decay over weeks and need boosters, exactly like the vaccine metaphor implies.
Do not oversell it. Prebunking shifts the ability to spot manipulation techniques; it does not immunise anyone against a determined campaign aimed at an identity they hold. It is the best available demand-side tool, not a solution.

If You Must Debunk
How to correct without backfiring
  • Lead with the truth, not the myth
  • Warn briefly before repeating the falsehood (if you must repeat it at all)
  • Explain why it's wrong and the motive behind it
  • Give a clear replacement explanation to fill the gap
The 'truth sandwich': truth → brief myth → truth again. Don't let the lie be the loudest thing you said.
StepThe rule
OpenState the truth, in a full sentence, as the headline
Warn“A false claim is circulating…” before any repetition
Repeat minimallyOnce, briefly, and never in the headline or the image
ExplainWhy it is wrong, and who benefits from it
ReplaceSupply the true account that fills the gap
CloseRestate the truth; that is what gets remembered
Most institutional corrections break rule three. The myth appears in the headline, the graphic and the first line, because it is the interesting part. What the reader retains, a week later, is the vivid claim rather than the correction attached to it.
Check any correction you write against one question: if someone read only the headline and the image, what would they come away believing? That is what most of your audience will do.

Media & Information Literacy
The long game: build the skill in everyone
The most durable defence is widespread media and information literacy (MIL) — teaching people, from school age up, to source, verify, contextualise and question what they consume and share.
  • Who made this, and why?
  • What's the evidence, and where's it from?
  • What's missing or being left out?
  • How does it want me to feel — and act?
QuestionWhat it surfaces
Who made this, and what do they gain?Motive, which is usually visible once asked
What is the evidence, and where is it from?Whether there is a source at all beneath the claim
What is missing?Selection and framing — the malinformation case
How does it want me to feel?Emotional design, which is the reliable tell
What does it want me to do?Forward, vote, buy, fear, or simply give up
Media literacy is the long game because it compounds. A cohort taught these five questions at fourteen carries them into forty years of information they will encounter, most of which does not exist yet — which no debunking programme can claim.
One caution the field has learned: taught badly, media literacy produces blanket cynicism — students who dismiss everything, including good evidence. The aim is calibrated trust, which means teaching how to recognise a reliable source as well as an unreliable one.
