Section One
What a Theory of Change Is
CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 · ImpactMojo 101 Series03www.impactmojo.in
ImpactMojoTheory of Change 101www.impactmojo.in
Definition
A theory of change is a causal map
A theory of change (ToC) is an explicit, testable account of how and why a set of activities is expected to lead to long-term change. It connects what you do to the change you seek — and names the assumptions that have to hold for that chain to work.
Theory of change
A comprehensive description of how and why a desired change is expected to happen in a particular context — mapping the pathway from activities through outcomes to a long-term goal, with the assumptions made explicit.
It is not a wish list. It is an argument about cause and effect that you can examine, test and revise.
A ToC must beOr it is
ExplicitStill in someone’s head
CausalA list of things you will do
TestableA statement of intent
Assumption-bearingA diagram of hopes
The word doing most work is testable. A theory that no observation could contradict is not a theory, and a great many published ToCs are in that condition.
The practical test: name one finding from monitoring that would make you redraw the map. If you cannot, the map is decoration.

What It Is Not
A ToC is not just a list of activities
Not this
  • A list of things the project will do
  • A budget or a workplan
  • A logframe filled in after the fact
  • A diagram drawn to please a donor
But this
  • A causal account of how change happens
  • An explicit set of assumptions
  • A shared, testable hypothesis
  • A living tool you revisit and revise
If your ToC could be redrawn from the activity list alone, it is not yet a theory of change — it is a plan.
SymptomWhat it really is
Boxes are verbsA workplan
No arrows carry a becauseA results chain
Written after the designA justification
Only one pathwayA results chain again
Never revisedA proposal annex
Every one of these produces a document that looks like a theory of change and does none of its work. The tell is usually the absence of anything anyone disagreed about.
A ToC that no-one argued over during drafting has not surfaced the differing theories in the team, which is the main thing it exists to do.

Origins
Where the idea comes from
The ToC approach grew out of programme evaluation. Carol Weiss argued in 1995 that complex community initiatives kept failing evaluation because their underlying theories — the assumptions about how and why they would work — were left unstated and untested.
The concept of grounding evaluation in theories of change takes for granted that social programs are based on explicit or implicit theories about how and why the program will work.
— Carol H. Weiss, 1995
Weiss’s argumentConsequence for evaluation
Initiatives fail evaluation for lack of theoryYou cannot say why it failed
The theory is usually implicitNobody agreed what was being tested
Make it explicit firstEvaluation becomes an inquiry, not a verdict
Weiss was writing about comprehensive community initiatives, which resisted experimental evaluation because they had many components and long horizons — a description that fits most development programmes.
Her point was not that experiments are wrong. It was that without a stated theory, a null result tells you nothing about which part failed.

Two Faces
A diagram and a narrative
The diagram
An outcomes map — boxes for changes, arrows for causal links, working back from the goal.
The narrative
A written story that explains each pathway, spells out the assumptions, and justifies the logic with evidence and rationale.
You need both. A diagram without a narrative is decoration; a narrative without a diagram is hard to test.
The diagramThe narrative
ShowsStructure and pathwaysReasoning and evidence
Good forA shared picture in a roomJustifying a link
Fails atNuance, conditions, caveatsBeing seen at a glance
LengthOne pageTwo to five pages
Programmes that produce only the diagram lose the reasoning within a staffing cycle. The arrows survive; the argument behind them does not.
Write the narrative even when the funder asks only for the picture. It is what a new team member needs in order to use the map rather than admire it.

Context
Change happens in a context, not a vacuum
A ToC is always anchored in a specific context — the place, the people, the politics, the existing services. The same activity can produce different outcomes in different settings, so a ToC that travels well names the context it assumes.
Ask early: what about this district, this community, this moment makes our causal story plausible — or fragile?
Context elementChanges the theory by
Existing servicesWhat the marginal contribution is
Political settlementWhether policy links can hold
Social normsWhether behaviour change is available
Market conditionsWhether income links pay
Other actorsWhether you are the binding constraint
The last row is the one design teams skip. If another organisation is already supplying the missing piece, your activity may add nothing; if nobody is, your pathway has a gap.
State the context conditions your theory depends on. That is what lets someone in a different district judge whether it will travel.

Why Bother
Making the implicit explicit
Every programme already has a theory of change — in someone's head. The discipline of a ToC is to drag it into the open where the whole team can see it, argue with it, and improve it before the money is spent.
Theory of change is essentially a comprehensive description of how and why a desired change is expected to happen in a particular context.
— widely used working definition (after ActKnowledge / Aspen)
Implicit theory lives inSurfaced by
The proposal writer’s headAsking them to draw the arrows
Field staff experienceBuilding the map with them
The funder’s expectationsReading their strategy aloud
Community understandingAsking whose theory this is
These four rarely agree, and the disagreement is cheap to resolve at design and expensive at evaluation, when it becomes an argument about what was promised.
The value of the exercise is largely in the disagreement it produces. A workshop where everyone nods has surfaced nothing.

Alignment
One picture the whole team can argue with
Programme staff, finance, M&E, partners and the community often hold different, unspoken theories of how the work will help. A shared ToC surfaces those differences early — while they are cheap to resolve.
The argument you have over a draft ToC in a workshop is far cheaper than the argument you have over a failed evaluation.
Who holds a different theoryTypically believes
Programme staffDelivery quality is the binding constraint
M&EThe outcome is not measurable as stated
FinanceThe activity mix cannot be delivered on this budget
PartnersThe local barrier is something else entirely
CommunityThe problem is not the one being solved
Each of these is a testable claim, and each becomes visible the moment the group is asked to place boxes and draw arrows rather than approve a document.
Resolving them costs a day at design. Discovering them in year two costs a redesign, and discovering them at evaluation costs the whole result.

Evaluation
It tells the evaluator what to measure
A ToC is the backbone of theory-based evaluation. Each outcome on the map is something to measure; each assumption is something to test. Evaluation stops being a verdict and becomes an inquiry into which links held and which broke.
When a programme underperforms, a ToC lets you ask the useful question: did we fail to deliver, or was the theory wrong?
ToC elementBecomes, in evaluation
Each outcome boxSomething to measure
Each arrowA hypothesis to test
Each assumptionA question for monitoring
Each pathwayAn alternative explanation to rule out
This is what theory-based evaluation means in practice: the ToC supplies the evaluation questions, so the evaluator is not inventing a framework after the fact.
It also changes what a null result means. Without a theory, "it did not work" is the finding; with one, you can say which link broke.

Learning
Surprises become evidence
Without a ToC
An unexpected result is just bad news to explain away or bury in the annual report.
With a ToC
An unexpected result tells you precisely which assumption was wrong — and where to adapt next.
A ToC turns ‘it didn't work’ into ‘here is the link that broke, and here is what we now believe instead.’
SurpriseWith a ToC you can ask
Outcome did not moveWhich link failed?
Outcome moved more than expectedWas another pathway operating?
One district differedWhich assumption held there and not here?
Adoption fell after year oneWas the sustaining condition ever true?
Each question is answerable with monitoring data you would already have if the assumptions had been made monitorable at design.
That is the argument for spending time on the assumptions column: it is what converts a disappointing result into a usable finding.

Accountability
Honest about what you can claim
A ToC clarifies your sphere of control (activities and outputs), your sphere of influence (outcomes you contribute to), and the sphere of interest (impact shaped by many actors). It keeps you from over-claiming credit.
Control
Activities & outputs you own
Influence
Outcomes you contribute to
Interest
Impact shaped by many
SphereClaim languageEvidence needed
Control"We delivered"Programme records
Influence"We contributed to"Comparison or contribution analysis
Interest"This sits within"Context data, not a claim
The three spheres come from Outcome Mapping and are the cleanest way to explain to a funder why the impact row carries no target.
Over-claiming is usually accidental: an impact-level sentence written for a proposal becomes the standard the project is evaluated against three years later.

Three Cousins
Related tools, different jobs
ToC, the logframe and the results chain are cousins, not rivals. They share the activities–to–impact logic but differ in form, richness and what they make visible.
Results chain
The linear backbone: inputs → impact
Logframe
A 4×4 matrix for management & M&E
Theory of change
A richer causal model with assumptions
ToolAnswersLoses
Results chainWhat follows whatWhy, and what else
LogframeWhat we commit to and how we verifyThe reasoning between rows
Theory of changeHow and why change happensCompactness; ease of reporting
Use all three. Build the ToC, draw the spine out of it as a results chain, then summarise it as a logframe for the funder.
Confusion usually starts when a team is asked for one and produces another. Be explicit about which document you are handing over and what it does not contain.

The Logframe
A logframe is a 4×4 matrix
The logical framework arranges a programme into a four-by-four grid: four levels of result down the side, four columns across.
LevelIndicatorMeans of verificationAssumptions
Impact / goal………
Outcome / purpose………
Outputs………
Activities………
Compact and management-friendly — but the grid hides the causal arrows between the rows.
Logframe columnToC equivalent
LevelA box on the map
IndicatorHow you would know that box is real
Means of verificationWhere the evidence comes from
AssumptionsOne per arrow, not one per level
Mapping the two formats onto each other makes translation mechanical, which matters when a funder wants a matrix and your thinking lives in a diagram.
The mismatch is in the last row and it is not cosmetic: a matrix has four assumption cells, and a map of fifteen arrows has fifteen assumptions.

Richer Model
How a ToC is richer
  • Branching pathways, not a single straight line
  • Explicit assumptions on every causal arrow
  • A rationale — evidence for why each link should hold
  • Room for context, actors and feedback loops
  • A narrative alongside the diagram
A logframe answers ‘what will we track?’. A ToC answers ‘why do we believe this will work?’
ToC addsWhich lets you
Branching pathwaysShow more than one route to the goal
Assumptions per arrowLocate exactly where a theory could break
A rationaleCite the evidence for each link
Context and actorsSay where the theory holds
A narrativeExplain what a diagram cannot
Each addition costs space, which is why the ToC cannot replace the logframe for reporting. They are different resolutions of the same argument.
The rationale row is the one most often empty and the most valuable. An arrow backed by a cited evaluation is a different claim from one backed by intuition.

Common Confusion
‘We have a logframe, so we have a ToC’
A filled-in logframe is not a theory of change. The matrix can be complete while the causal reasoning — the ‘because’ arrows and the assumptions that connect the rows — remains entirely unstated.
If you cannot explain, out loud, why each logframe row should produce the row above it, you have a matrix but not a theory.
Ask of a logframeIf the answer is no
Why does this output produce this outcome?The theory is unstated
What must hold for it to?The assumptions are cosmetic
What evidence supports the link?There is no rationale
What else could produce this outcome?No alternative pathway considered
Four questions. If a team cannot answer them from the matrix, they have a logframe and not a theory of change, whatever the proposal says.
This is not an argument against logframes. It is an argument for not mistaking a summary for the thing it summarises.

Outcome Mapping
A note on related methods
Outcome Mapping (IDRC) and Outcome Harvesting are kindred approaches that focus on changes in the behaviour of actors a programme influences. They pair well with a ToC, especially for advocacy and systems change where impact is hard to attribute.
Different vocabulary, same spirit: be honest about influence rather than control, and track behaviour change in others.
MethodFocusPairs with ToC for
Outcome MappingBehaviour of boundary partnersAdvocacy, systems change
Outcome HarvestingOutcomes found retrospectivelyUnpredictable environments
Contribution analysisTesting the pathwayEvaluation without a control group
Process tracingCausal mechanism in a casePolicy influence claims
These are the methods to reach for when your goal runs through other actors’ decisions rather than through direct service delivery.
All four take the ToC as their starting point, which is a good argument for building it properly even when the funder only asked for a matrix.

Inputs
Inputs: what you put in
Inputs
The financial, human and material resources a programme uses — budget, staff time, training materials, vehicles, partner relationships.
Inputs are the easiest thing to count and the least interesting to a funder. Spending money is an activity, not a result.
InputFrequently uncosted
Staff timePartner and government staff time
MaterialsReplacement and maintenance
RelationshipsThe years of trust a partner brings
Participants’ timeThe largest hidden cost to the poorest
Inputs are the least interesting row to a funder and the most important for judging whether a result can be replicated elsewhere at a similar cost.
Spending is not achievement, and burn rate is not progress. Both get reported as though they were, because they are available monthly and outcomes are not.

Activities & Outputs
Outputs are what you deliver
Activities
The actions you take: run trainings, build toilets, hold meetings, distribute kits. Verbs you control.
Outputs
The direct, countable products: 200 teachers trained, 50 toilets built, 1,000 kits delivered. Still within your control.
Outputs are necessary but never sufficient. Delivering them proves effort, not change.
Output quality questionBecause
Was it usable?A training nobody understood is still a training
Did it reach the right people?Coverage is not targeting
Was it at the right time?Seeds after sowing are not an input
Did it arrive complete?Half a kit is not a kit
Outputs are usually reported as counts, and counts are silent about all four of these. That silence is where the output-to-outcome link quietly breaks.
Attach one quality indicator to each output. It is the cheapest way to distinguish "delivered" from "delivered well enough to work".

The Big Leap
Outcomes: changes in others
Outcome
A change in the knowledge, attitudes, behaviour, condition or status of the people, institutions or systems a programme works with. Outcomes happen in others — which is why they are outside your direct control.
The single most common ToC error is calling an output an outcome. ‘Teachers trained’ is an output; ‘teachers teach differently’ is an outcome.
Outcome domainExampleMeasured by
KnowledgeMothers know the danger signsTest or structured question
AttitudeTeachers believe the method worksScale, or observed choice
BehaviourFarmers irrigate with dripObservation or recall
ConditionChildren are less anaemicMeasurement
SystemThe panchayat allocates a budget lineRecords
Naming the domain forces a measurement decision. Knowledge and attitude are cheap to measure and weakly related to behaviour, which is why so many programmes stop at them.
If your outcome sits in the first two rows, be explicit that behaviour change is a further link, and put it in the map.

Whose theory is this?It is usually the funder’s or the writer’s
What do people here say causes this?Their causal account may differ entirely
What have they already tried?Failed local attempts are evidence
Who benefits from things staying as they are?The barrier may be interest, not capacity
The last question is rarely in a ToC and often explains the whole pattern. Where someone gains from the status quo, a capacity-building pathway will not shift it.
Involving the people the change is for improves the theory as analysis, not only as participation. They hold information the design process otherwise cannot reach.

Iterate
First draft, not final word
A ToC is never finished. Draft it quickly, use it, and revise as you learn. An over-polished first version signals false confidence; a rough, living one invites the questioning that makes it strong.
A theory of change is a work in progress, not a one-off product — its value lies in the thinking it provokes, not the diagram it produces.
— after Isabel Vogel, ToC review for DFID, 2012
Revise whenWhat changes
Baseline landsTargets, and sometimes the problem statement
An assumption failsThe pathway, or the design
An unexpected outcome appearsA pathway you had not drawn
Context shiftsExternal conditions, and possibly the goal
Date every version and note what changed and why. An undated ToC in circulation is indistinguishable from a superseded one.
An over-polished first draft signals false confidence. Draw it roughly, use it, and let the revisions be the evidence that anyone is paying attention.

Keep It Legible
Resist the urge to draw everything
A map with sixty boxes and a hundred arrows communicates nothing. The discipline is to show the main pathways clearly and push the detail into the narrative and the indicator table.
If a colleague cannot trace one path from activity to goal in under a minute, the diagram is too crowded. Simplify.
Legibility ruleTest
Main pathways only on the mapA colleague can trace one in a minute
Detail in the narrativeThe diagram fits on a page
Indicators in a tableNot on the boxes
Assumptions numberedReferable from the narrative
Sixty boxes and a hundred arrows is not more rigorous than fifteen and twenty. It is usually muddled thinking rendered at high resolution.
The one-minute trace is a good acceptance test. If nobody can follow a pathway from entry point to goal unaided, the map has failed its main job.

Pitfall 1
Activity-itis: doing mistaken for changing
Activity-itis is the most common disease of ToCs: the map fills with things the programme will do — train, build, convene — and never reaches the changes those activities are meant to cause.
The cure: for every activity, ask ‘so that what?’ until you reach a genuine change in someone's life or behaviour.
Activity boxThe change it should become
Train teachersTeachers use the method
Build toiletsHouseholds use and maintain toilets
Convene meetingsThe forum takes a decision
Distribute kitsKits are used correctly
The cure is mechanical: for every activity box, ask "so that what?" until the answer is a change in someone else. Then keep the change and demote the activity.
Activities still belong on the map, at the bottom, as entry points. The error is letting them occupy the middle where outcomes should be.

Pitfall 3
Missing assumptions: silent arrows
An arrow with no stated assumption is a claim nobody has examined. The link ‘women earn income → women are empowered’ hides a huge assumption: that they control how the income is spent.
Audit every arrow. If it has no ‘this works because… and only if…’ note, you have found a blind spot.
Silent arrowThe assumption it hides
Women earn income → women are empoweredShe controls how it is spent
Clinic built → people use itDistance, cost, trust, staffing
Information given → behaviour changesThey can act on it
Committee formed → accountability improvesIt has power and information
These four are among the best-documented broken links in development practice, and all four look self-evident when written as arrows.
Audit every arrow at the end of drafting. Where you cannot state a because, either you have a leap or you have found the assumption that matters most.

Pitfall 4
Leaps of logic: the missing middle
A leap of logic skips the outcomes in between. Fill the gap with the real intermediate changes — or admit the link is hope, not theory.
The missing middleWhat belongs there
After trainingWorkers practise correctly

ThenSick children are treated
Only thenDeaths fall
Each missing box is also a missing measurement point. A programme that maps only the two ends can report activity and mortality, and nothing that would explain either.
The middle boxes are usually the ones a programme can actually influence and observe, which makes skipping them doubly costly.

Pitfall 5
Over-complexity: the spaghetti diagram
The opposite failure: a map so dense with boxes and crossing arrows that no one can follow a single pathway. Complexity is not rigour — often it hides muddled thinking behind a busy picture.
If you cannot explain your ToC in two minutes with the diagram in hand, it is too complex to be useful. Prune it.
Complexity is warranted whenIt is not when
Several genuine pathways existEvery stakeholder wanted a box
Feedback loops are realArrows were added to look thorough
Different groups follow different routesThe thinking was never resolved
The distinguishing test is whether you can say what each arrow claims. Where you cannot, the density is hiding an unresolved argument rather than representing one.
If a map must be complex, split it: one overview showing pathways, and a detailed map per pathway. Two readable pages beat one unreadable one.

More Traps
Three quieter mistakes
  • Reverse-engineering: writing the ToC after deciding the activities, to justify them
  • Single-pathway tunnel vision: ignoring the other ways the goal could be reached — or blocked
  • Set-and-forget: drawing it once for the proposal and never looking at it again
Each of these turns a living theory into a dead document. The fix is the same: keep using it.
Quieter trapWhat it costs
Reverse-engineeringThe design is never tested
Single-pathway tunnel visionAlternative routes and blockers unseen
Set-and-forgetNo learning; a stale map
No negative pathwayUnintended harm unanticipated
The fourth is worth adding to your own practice: draw the pathway by which the programme could make things worse, and put a monitoring check on it.
Unintended effects are rarely unforeseeable. They are usually foreseeable and unasked-about, which a five-minute question at design would fix.

In Design
Designing with a ToC
At design, the ToC stress-tests the programme before money is spent: are the pathways plausible? Are activities matched to the change sought? Where are the riskiest assumptions, and can the design strengthen them?
A ToC can save a programme by revealing, on paper, that the activities could never reach the goal — the cheapest failure you will ever have.
Design question the ToC answersBefore spending
Is the pathway plausible?Yes, or redesign
Are activities matched to the change?Or are they what we already do?
Where is the riskiest assumption?Can the design strengthen it?
What are we not doing that is necessary?Partner, or accept the gap
The third question is where a ToC most often changes a design: an assumption identified as load-bearing gets converted into an activity, and the programme gets stronger.
That conversion — assumption into output — is the single most useful move the method produces. It closes a gap rather than documenting it.

Contribution
Contribution analysis: did the theory hold?
Where you cannot run a controlled experiment, contribution analysis uses the ToC as its spine: gather evidence on each link, check whether the pathway played out as theorised, and rule out alternative explanations.
The verdict is not ‘did it work?’ but ‘is it reasonable to conclude the programme made a difference, given how the theory held up?’
Contribution analysis stepWhat it produces
Set out the ToC and its risksThe claim to be tested
Gather evidence on each linkWhere the chain held
Consider alternative explanationsWhat else could have caused this
Assemble the contribution storyA reasoned, evidenced claim
Seek out disconfirming evidenceCredibility
Mayne’s formulation is the standard reference, and the step people skip is the third: ruling out other explanations is what turns a narrative into an argument.
It is the practical method where a control group is impossible, which describes most policy influence, systems and advocacy work.

In Reporting
Telling an honest results story
A ToC structures reporting around change, not activity. Instead of ‘we trained 500 people’, you can say ‘here is the change we contributed to, here is the evidence, and here is where the theory did and did not hold.’
Funders increasingly trust the programme that reports honestly on a broken link over the one that reports only success.
Instead of reportingReport
“We trained 500 people”“Practice changed for X%, and here is how we know”

“The programme was successful”“We contributed to this, alongside these actors”
Only what workedWhat did not, and what it taught
Reporting the broken link is a stronger document than reporting only success, because it demonstrates that the programme was actually testing something.
Most funders now say they want this. Whether their systems reward it is a separate question, and worth asking before the annual report is due.

Living Document
Keep it on the wall, not in the drawer

  • Date each version and note what changed and why
  • Pin the current map where the team can see it
  • Use it to onboard new staff and partners quickly

  • Map backwards from the long-term goal, not forwards from what you do
  • Outputs ≠ outcomes ≠ impact — know which is which
  • Make assumptions explicit — that is where risk and learning live
  • Keep it living — use it across design, MEL, adaptation and reporting
