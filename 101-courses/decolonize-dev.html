Section One
What ‘Decolonising Development’ Means
CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 · ImpactMojo 101 Series03www.impactmojo.in
ImpactMojoDecolonial Development 101www.impactmojo.in
Starting Point
Beyond a buzzword
‘Decolonising’ is everywhere — in funding calls, conference titles and job descriptions. Used loosely it becomes a slogan that changes nothing. This course treats it as a serious analytical project: naming how colonial power still shapes who develops whom, with whose knowledge, in whose interest.
Decolonising development is not a rebrand. It asks who holds power over the very idea of ‘development’ — and whether that idea can be remade or must be left behind.
Used as a buzzwordUsed as an analysis
A section in a strategy documentA question about who decides
Diversifying a photo libraryChanging who holds the budget line
A workshop on unconscious biasMoving a decision from London to Patna
New vocabulary, same architectureNew architecture, whatever the vocabulary
The test throughout this deck is the right-hand column: after the change, who decides what counts as a problem, what counts as success, and where the money goes? Anything that leaves those three untouched has changed the presentation.
The term is contested, including by people sympathetic to it, and Section 11 takes those objections seriously rather than treating them as bad faith. A course that cannot state the strongest case against its own subject is advocacy.

Definition
Decolonising development, defined
Decolonising development
A critical and practical project that exposes and undoes the colonial relations of power, knowledge and economy embedded in development — challenging who sets agendas, whose knowledge counts, where resources flow, and whose vision of a good life prevails.
It works at three levels at once: knowledge (whose ideas are authoritative), power (who decides), and economy (who owns and profits).
DomainThe decolonial question
KnowledgeWhose theory counts as theory, and whose as folklore?
MoneyWho sets the terms, and who reports to whom?
AgendaWho decided this was the problem?
EvaluationWho defines success, and in whose language?
RepresentationWho tells the story, and who is paid for it?
Five questions, each with an observable answer. That is deliberate: an abstraction that cannot be checked against an organisation chart, a grant agreement or a logframe becomes a mood rather than an analysis.
Apply them to your own organisation before applying them to the sector. Most readers will find the third row is the one where their programme has least to say, and it is the one that determines everything downstream.

Three Registers
Decolonial, postcolonial, anti-colonial
  • Anti-colonial: the political struggle to end direct colonial rule (Fanon, the independence movements)
  • Postcolonial: analysing culture, identity and discourse after empire (Said, Spivak, Bhabha)
  • Decolonial: a Latin American school centring ‘coloniality’ — the lasting structures of power (Quijano, Mignolo)
Overlapping, not identical. This course draws on all three but leans on the decolonial idea that colonialism ended while coloniality lives on.
RegisterCentral claimAssociated with
Anti-colonialEnd colonial rule; national liberationFanon, Cabral, the independence movements
PostcolonialAnalyse the legacies in culture, text and identitySaid, Spivak, Bhabha
DecolonialColoniality outlived colonialism; delink from its structuresQuijano, Mignolo, the Latin American school
They are frequently used interchangeably and are not the same argument. Anti-colonial politics largely succeeded in its stated aim; the postcolonial and decolonial traditions both begin from the observation that this changed less than expected.
The decolonial school is the one this deck leans on, because its central concept — coloniality, in Section 5 — is what explains why formally independent states reproduce the hierarchies of the empires that left.

Why It Matters Here
Why this matters in South Asia
South Asia was both a laboratory of empire and a cradle of anti-colonial thought — from the drain of wealth debates to Gandhi’s swaraj. Yet much of its development sector still imports frameworks, metrics and ‘best practice’ designed elsewhere.
The question is sharp for practitioners: are we delivering an externally authored model of progress, or building one rooted in the histories and aspirations of the people we serve?
InheritanceStill visible in
Administrative structuresThe district, the collector, the revenue system
Legal codesStatutes drafted in the nineteenth century, still in force
Language hierarchyEnglish as the medium of authority and advancement
Census categoriesCaste and tribe classifications formalised under colonial rule
Economic orientationRaw material out, finished goods in
The fourth row is the sharpest example for this region. Colonial enumeration did not merely record caste; it fixed fluid, local identities into countable national categories, and those categories now structure both discrimination and its remedies.
Which is also why the analysis cannot stop at the colonial. Caste predates the British and was reshaped by them; treating every hierarchy here as a colonial import is historically wrong and politically convenient for those it advantages.

Section Two
The Colonial Roots of ‘Development’

Origins
‘Development’ has a history, not just a meaning
We talk of development as if it were a timeless, neutral goal. But the modern idea — some countries are ‘developed’, others must catch up — is a twentieth-century invention with deep colonial roots.
Development was the conceptual heir of the civilising mission: the same hierarchy of peoples, with the vocabulary changed.
— a recurring claim in critical development studies
PeriodHow the relationship was named
18th–19th centuryCivilising the barbarous
Late 19th centuryTrusteeship over “backward peoples”
InterwarColonial welfare and development acts
1949 onwardTechnical assistance to “underdeveloped areas”
NowPartnership, capacity building, aid effectiveness
Read down the right-hand column and the vocabulary softens at every step while the structure — one party assessing another’s deficiency and offering to correct it — persists. That continuity is the section’s argument, and it is a historical claim you can check rather than a rhetorical one.
Continuity is not identity. Aid is not colonial rule; nobody is being conquered. The claim is that the categories and the confidence carried over, not that nothing changed.

The Civilising Mission
From ‘civilising’ to ‘developing’
The civilising mission
The colonial justification that European rule uplifted ‘backward’ peoples — the mission civilisatrice, the ‘white man’s burden’ — casting domination as benevolence and tutelage.
Strip away the racial language and the structure survives: an advanced party guiding a deficient one toward a single, predefined destination. That structure migrated into ‘development’.

Macaulay
Educating the colonised in the coloniser’s image
Thomas Macaulay’s 1835 Minute on Indian Education openly aimed to form ‘a class… Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste, in opinions, in morals and in intellect’ — dismissing Indian and Arabic learning wholesale.
Here is coloniality of knowledge in plain view: native learning declared worthless, the coloniser’s curriculum installed as universal. Its institutional legacy long outlived the Raj.
The 1835 Minute proposedWhat followed
English as the medium of instructionAn English-educated administrative class
Indian learning as of little valueSystematic devaluation of existing scholarship
“A class Indian in blood and colour, English in taste”An intermediary stratum between rulers and ruled
Macaulay’s Minute is quoted so often that its actual argument gets lost. It was a budget decision about which kind of education to fund, argued on the explicit ground that European knowledge was superior — and it is worth reading, because the reasoning is stated without embarrassment.
The consequences are not simple. The same English education produced the nationalist leadership that ended colonial rule, and English remains a route out of caste hierarchy for many Dalit families. Section 7 returns to this; language politics here does not divide neatly.

Truman 1949
Point Four and the birth of ‘underdevelopment’
On 20 January 1949, in his inaugural address, US President Harry Truman announced Point Four: a programme of technical aid for ‘underdeveloped areas’. With that word, half the world was re-described overnight.
We must embark on a bold new program for… the improvement and growth of underdeveloped areas.
— Harry S. Truman, Inaugural Address, 20 Jan 1949 (Point Four)
What Point Four didConsequence
Named most of the world “underdeveloped”Two billion people acquired a deficiency
Made one trajectory the measureDifference became backwardness
Offered technical knowledge as the remedyThe problem became technical, not political
Arrived at the start of the Cold WarAid as an instrument of alignment
The third row is the one with the longest shadow. Framing poverty as a shortfall of technique rather than an outcome of arrangements is what makes it addressable by experts and projects — and what removes trade, debt and land from the conversation.
The fourth row is often left out of the story. Point Four was announced in January 1949 in a speech largely about containing communism, and the aid architecture that followed was shaped by alliance politics as much as by need.

The Invention
Naming a problem into existence
Arturo Escobar argues that ‘underdevelopment’ was not discovered but invented — the moment diverse societies were lumped together as a deficient mass, defined by what they lacked relative to the United States.

Before the categoryAfter it

The fourth row is the part that self-perpetuates. Once the category exists, institutions form around it, and those institutions have an interest in the category remaining meaningful — which is a structural observation rather than an accusation of bad faith.
Do not overstate the invention. Hunger, disease and premature death were not invented in 1949 and are not discursive. What was invented was a way of grouping and explaining them that made one path the remedy.

The Development Era
An age of plans, experts and aid
The decades after 1949 institutionalised development: the World Bank and IMF (Bretton Woods, 1944), bilateral aid agencies, five-year plans, the ‘Decade of Development’ (the 1960s) and an emerging class of international experts.
Decolonisation of territory and the rise of development overlapped almost exactly. As flags changed, a new, subtler form of external guidance moved in.

ImpactMojoDecolonial Development 101www.impactmojo.in
The Core Claim
Knowledge is never innocent of power
A central decolonial insight, drawn from Michel Foucault and radicalised by postcolonial thinkers: knowledge and power produce each other. To define and describe a people is already to exercise power over them.
Eurocentrism
Treating European history, categories and experience as the universal standard against which all societies are measured — so that other knowledges appear partial, local or primitive.
Knowledge decisions that carry powerExample
What counts as evidenceRandomised trial above practitioner experience
Which language it must be written inEnglish for a journal; the funder’s language for a report
Who may certify an expertDegrees from particular institutions
What gets classified as theoryEuropean thought as theory; other thought as culture
The fourth row is the sharpest and the least noticed. A European thinker is read as producing general theory; a South Asian or African thinker is read as producing regional knowledge — the same intellectual act, filed differently.
This is not an argument that evidence hierarchies are worthless. Trials answer certain questions well. The claim is narrower: that deciding which questions are worth answering, and which knowledge counts, is a use of power that presents itself as method.

Escobar 1995
Arturo Escobar’s Encountering Development
In Encountering Development (1995), Arturo Escobar applied a Foucauldian lens to development itself, treating it as a discourse: a whole system of knowledge, institutions and practices that produces its own objects — ‘the poor’, ‘the Third World’.
Development… created a space in which only certain things could be said and even imagined.
— Arturo Escobar, Encountering Development (1995)

Development as Discourse
What a ‘discourse’ does
Discourse
More than talk: an organised system of statements, categories and institutions that defines what counts as true, normal and possible — and thereby what can even be thought.
If development is a discourse, then tweaking projects inside it is not enough. The categories themselves — ‘poverty’, ‘the target population’ — carry the power.
A discourse doesExample in this sector
Defines what can be said sensiblyA proposal must name a measurable outcome
Creates the objects it describes“The rural poor” as a manageable population
Assigns who may speak with authorityThe consultant, not the farmer
Makes alternatives unsayable“We should not do a project here”
The fourth row is the most testable claim in the section. Try writing a funding application whose recommendation is that no intervention is warranted; the form does not accommodate it, and that is what “discourse” means in practice.
Escobar is criticised for leaving little room for agency, and the criticism has force: people inside these structures resist, subvert and redirect them constantly. A discourse constrains what is easy to say; it does not determine what people do.

Universals
The trouble with the ‘universal’
Markets, the rational individual, linear progress, the nuclear household, the modern state — presented as universal, these are in fact a particular European history dressed up as everyone’s future.
Decolonising knowledge means re-particularising the ‘universal’: asking where each supposedly neutral category actually came from, and whom it fits.
Presented as universalActually particular
The nuclear household as the unit of analysisJoint families, multiple earners, shared cooking
Individual land title as securityCommon land, customary and collective tenure
Wage employment as the measure of workUnpaid care, subsistence, family labour
Written contract as the form of agreementOral and relational arrangements
Each left-hand item is embedded in survey instruments and programme design, so the particularity is invisible: the questionnaire simply does not have a box for the arrangement people actually live in, and the resulting data reports its absence as a deficiency.
The counter-argument deserves stating. Some universals are worth defending — that no one should be beaten, that children should eat — and a critique of universalism that cannot distinguish those from the household unit has given up more than it meant to.

The Orthodoxy
Modernisation theory
The post-war orthodoxy, crystallised in W. W. Rostow’s Stages of Economic Growth (1960), held that all societies climb the same ladder — from ‘traditional’ to ‘high mass consumption’ — if they adopt Western institutions and values.
Modernisation theory
The view that development is a single, linear path; poor countries are simply at an earlier stage and must become more like the West to advance.
Modernisation theory holdsWhich implies
All societies pass through similar stagesSome are simply behind
The obstacle is internal — tradition, institutionsThe remedy is internal reform
Capital and technology transfer accelerate the passageAid as the accelerator
The endpoint resembles the industrial WestOne destination, known in advance
The second row is where the politics sits. If the obstacle is internal, then the international arrangements — trade terms, debt, capital flows — are not part of the explanation, and the entire question of external responsibility disappears by assumption.
It has not gone away. Explicit stage theory is out of fashion, and its logic survives in any programme whose theory of change locates every constraint inside the community it works in.

Hidden Assumptions
What modernisation theory smuggles in
  • One destination for all — the Western present
  • ‘Tradition’ as obstacle, not resource
  • The obstacle is internal — culture, attitudes, institutions
  • Therefore: history, colonialism and global structure can be ignored
Its deepest move is to locate the cause of poverty inside poor societies — conveniently absolving the colonial and global system that helped produce it.
AssumptionProblem
History has one directionDifferent societies have taken different paths
Tradition obstructs; modernity liberatesBoth contain hierarchy and both contain resource
Poor countries are early, not disadvantagedIgnores that some industrialised at others’ expense
The West is the future of the restA claim about time that is really about power
The third row is the dependency school’s opening. If Britain’s industrialisation and India’s deindustrialisation were connected events rather than sequential stages, then “behind” is the wrong word for the relationship.
The fourth row names the underlying move — what Chakrabarty called the “waiting room” of history. Placing societies on a timeline rather than in a relationship converts a political question into a chronological one.

The Counter-School
Dependency theory turns the lens around
From Latin America in the 1960s–70s, dependency theory argued the opposite: poor countries are not ‘behind’ but actively underdeveloped — their poverty produced by the same global system that enriched the ‘core’.
Dependency
A relationship in which the economies of peripheral countries are shaped by and subordinated to the needs of dominant ‘core’ economies — growth at the centre conditioned on extraction from the periphery.
Modernisation saysDependency says
Poverty is a starting conditionPoverty is a produced outcome
Integration into world markets helpsIntegration on unequal terms is the mechanism
The obstacle is internalThe obstacle is the structure of the relationship
More aid and investmentChanged terms, or delinking
The disagreement is about causation, not compassion. Both schools want poverty to end; they differ on whether the poverty of one region is connected to the wealth of another, and that difference produces entirely different policy.
It also produces different politics of aid. On the dependency account, aid can be a way of managing the consequences of an arrangement while leaving the arrangement in place — which is the argument Section 8 returns to.

Prebisch & Frank
Prebisch, Frank and ‘the development of underdevelopment’
Raúl Prebisch
Argued that the terms of trade move against commodity exporters over time — the periphery runs to stand still. Led ECLA and shaped UNCTAD.
Andre Gunder Frank
Coined ‘the development of underdevelopment’: the same process that develops the metropole underdevelops the satellite. Same coin, two sides.
The radical implication: integration into the world economy on existing terms may deepen, not cure, underdevelopment.
ThinkerContribution
Raúl PrebischTerms of trade decline for primary exporters; the case for industrialisation
Andre Gunder Frank“The development of underdevelopment” — one process, two outcomes
Samir AminDelinking from the world system as a strategy
Prebisch’s thesis is the most testable and the most consequential. If the price of what you export falls relative to what you import over the long run, then trading more of the same thing makes you poorer — which is the analytical case for industrial policy rather than comparative advantage.
The empirical record is mixed and the policy record is worse. The Question
Knowledge has a hierarchy
Not all knowledge is treated equally. A randomised trial counts as ‘evidence’; a farmer’s reading of the monsoon or an elder’s account of a forest is filed under ‘belief’, ‘anecdote’ or ‘folklore’.
Epistemic injustice
Harm done to someone specifically as a knower — when their testimony is unfairly doubted, or when the shared concepts needed to make sense of their experience are missing or suppressed.

The hierarchy is rarely stated and is enacted constantly. Watch which claims in a programme meeting require evidence and which are accepted on the speaker’s authority; the pattern usually tracks the rows above.

A speaker is given less credibility because of who they are — the illiterate, the rural, the woman, the Adivasi — regardless of what they know.

A whole group’s experience cannot be voiced because the dominant vocabulary has no words for it — the gap itself is an injustice.

The Radical Turn
What if development is the problem?
Post-development thinkers — Escobar, Wolfgang Sachs, Gustavo Esteva, Majid Rahnema — go further than reform. They argue the whole project should be abandoned, not improved: it homogenises diverse ways of living into a single, Western blueprint.
The idea of development stands… like a ruin in the intellectual landscape.
— Wolfgang Sachs (ed.), The Development Dictionary (1992)
Post-development claimsThe obvious objection
Development is a project to be abandoned, not fixedPeople want clinics, schools and electricity
Its promise has failed on its own termsPoverty and mortality have fallen substantially
Alternatives already exist locallySome are hierarchical in their own right
The category itself does the damageCategories can also mobilise resources
The second objection is the strongest and must be stated. Child mortality, literacy and life expectancy have improved enormously across South Asia over the development era, and a critique that cannot account for that is not describing the world.
The serious version of the argument is narrower: not that material improvement is bad, but that it need not require accepting one model of the good life, one measure of progress, and one set of experts to define both.

The Pluriverse
Escobar’s pluriverse
Pluriverse
Arturo Escobar’s vision of ‘a world where many worlds fit’ — against the one-world, one-future model of development; many coexisting ways of being, knowing and organising life.
The phrase comes from the Zapatistas. Its promise: not a better single path, but the dignity of many paths.

Buen Vivir
Buen vivir / sumak kawsay
From Andean Indigenous thought, buen vivir (sumak kawsay in Kichwa) means ‘good living’ or ‘living well together’ — a life in balance with community and nature, not the endless accumulation of more.
It is constitutional in Ecuador (2008) and Bolivia (2009), and grounds the idea of rights of nature — an alternative end of development, not just a different means.

Sufficiency rather than accumulationGrowth as the objective
Written into Ecuador’s and Bolivia’s constitutionsA live legal experiment, not only a philosophy
The fourth row is what makes it more than a slogan and also what tests it. Both states subsequently expanded extractive industry while the principle sat in their constitutions, which is the most instructive thing about the case.
Read that as evidence about implementation, not refutation. A constitutional principle without a fiscal alternative loses to the revenue that funds the state — which is the same problem any alternative model faces, and worth confronting rather than skipping.

Ubuntu
Ubuntu: I am because we are
From Southern Africa, ubuntu — ‘a person is a person through other persons’ — centres relationship, dignity and the collective over the isolated, maximising individual of mainstream economics.
Ubuntu reframes wellbeing as fundamentally relational: my flourishing is bound up with yours — a direct challenge to development’s individualist core.

This is the clearest case of the danger in Section 11. An indigenous alternative to a colonial model can carry an indigenous hierarchy, and a decolonial argument that cannot say so is defending one domination against another.

Degrowth
Degrowth: questioning growth itself
Degrowth, largely a Northern critique, argues that infinite economic growth on a finite planet is neither possible nor desirable — and that rich economies must shrink their material throughput so all can live well.
It converges with post-development on rejecting growth-as-goal — though decolonial voices insist degrowth must start in the over-consuming North, not be imposed on those still meeting basic needs.
Degrowth arguesThe South Asian question it raises

Rich economies should contractWhich is where the argument is aimed

Redistribution, not expansionBetween countries as well as within them
Degrowth is frequently misheard here as a proposal that poor countries should stay poor, and its serious proponents say the opposite: contraction in high-consuming economies precisely so that consumption can rise where it is inadequate.
The suspicion is nonetheless well earned. Environmental limits have repeatedly been invoked to constrain industrialisation in the South while consumption in the North continued, so the burden is on the argument to say explicitly who reduces first.

Romanticisation
The risk of romanticising the ‘local’
Celebrating indigenous and traditional ways can slide into romanticisation — ignoring that local structures, too, can be unequal: caste, patriarchy, gerontocracy, exclusion.
‘The community’ is not a harmonious whole. Decolonising must not become a cover for defending local injustice in the name of authenticity.

Essentialism
The essentialism trap
Essentialism
Treating ‘Western’ and ‘indigenous’ (or East and West) as fixed, pure, opposed essences — ironically reproducing the very binary that Orientalism built.
Knowledge has always travelled and mixed. Hybridity (Homi Bhabha) reminds us there is no untouched ‘authentic’ to return to — only living, entangled traditions.

Essentialising the colonised repeats the structure of Orientalism with the sign reversed. Said’s critique was of homogenising representation, and a homogenising representation produced sympathetically is still homogenising.

The Hardest Question
Can a colonial sector decolonise itself?
The pessimist
The aid system is the colonial relation. Asking it to decolonise is asking the master’s house to dismantle itself with the master’s tools.
The pragmatist
Until the system is gone, those inside can still shift real power and resources. Imperfect change beats principled paralysis.
Audre Lorde’s warning — ‘the master’s tools will never dismantle the master’s house’ — haunts every reform. Sit with the tension; do not resolve it cheaply.

The third position is the most under-discussed inside the sector, for an obvious reason: it implies that the most consequential decolonial work is not in aid reform at all but in trade rules, debt restructuring and climate finance, where the sector has little standing.
This deck does not resolve the question, and a course that pretended to would be doing the thing it warns against. The useful stance is to work the second position while keeping the first two objections in view.
