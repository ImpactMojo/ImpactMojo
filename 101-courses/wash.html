Definitions
Three services that block one route of infection
WASH stands for water, sanitation and hygiene. Water means drinking water that is close, available when it is needed and free of contamination. Sanitation means a toilet that separates excreta from human contact and a chain of services that keeps it separated until it is treated or safely buried. Hygiene means the practices that keep hands, food and water clean, above all washing hands with soap, and the facilities that make those practices possible, including for menstruation.
Why the three are taught together
Diarrhoea, worm infections and many enteric infections travel the same way, from one person's faeces to another person's mouth. Each service blocks a different stretch of that route. A toilet keeps faeces out of the environment, safe water keeps them out of the drink, and soap keeps them off the hands that prepare food and feed children.
Why they are run separately
In India the three sit in different programmes. Drinking water is Jal Jeevan Mission, rural sanitation is Swachh Bharat Mission (Grameen), urban sanitation is Swachh Bharat Mission (Urban), and hygiene is scattered across health, education and both missions. A district officer sees three dashboards for one route of infection.
This course follows the route of infection first and the programmes second, because a child is exposed through whichever barrier is weakest.

New host
Public health teaching draws transmission as the F-diagram. Pathogens leave an infected person in faeces and reach the next person through water, hands, flies, soil and the food that all of these touch. Barriers work at two points. Primary barriers stop faeces entering the environment at all. They include a toilet that is used, safe disposal of children's faeces and safe emptying of pits and tanks. Secondary barriers stop contamination already in the environment from reaching the mouth. They include handwashing, safe water storage and treatment, and food hygiene.
Primary barriers
A latrine that every member uses, safe disposal of small children's faeces, and a pit or tank that is emptied and treated without exposing workers or neighbours.
Secondary barriers
Handwashing with soap after defecation, after cleaning a child and before food. Covered storage and household treatment. The NSS 76th round found 65.3% of households did not treat drinking water, 72.8% in rural areas (NSS Report No. 584, 76th round (July-December 2018), NSO, 2019, Statement 8).

A public good
Sanitation protects the neighbours as much as the owner
A household that builds and uses a toilet lowers the exposure of every child nearby. A household that defecates in the open raises it, whatever the neighbours do. That spillover is why sanitation behaves like a public good and why health gains depend on how many people in a village stop open defecation, as well as on whether any single family has a latrine.
The district evidence
Spears, Ghosh and Cumming matched HUNGaMA 2011 stunting data from 112 districts with Census 2011 open defecation. After adjusting for wealth, maternal education and calorie availability, a 10 percentage point rise in open defecation was associated with a 0.7 point rise in stunting (PLoS ONE, 2013). The design is ecological, and the authors say so.
What that means for a programme
A target of a toilet in each house measures construction. A target that matters for health counts the share of people in a settlement who use one, and how faeces are handled afterwards. Section 5 shows how far those two kinds of number have drifted apart in India.
Coverage thresholds matter. A village at 60% use can still have enough faeces in its lanes and ponds to keep children sick.

Who carries the burden
Water collection, distance and caste shape who goes without
Distance and drudgery
In 2018, 58.2% of rural households and 80.7% of urban households had drinking water within their premises (NSS Report No. 584, 76th round (July-December 2018), NSO, 2019). Someone in the other households walks to fetch it, and that person is usually a woman or a girl. In the JJM Functionality Assessment 2022, 79% of households where women used to fetch water reported less drudgery after a tap was installed (JJM Functionality Assessment 2022, national report).
Caste and class
The same NSS round found 71.3% of rural households had access to a latrine, against 62.8% of Scheduled Caste and 63.3% of Scheduled Tribe rural households. Across rural consumption quintiles access rose from 53.5% in the poorest fifth to 86.7% in the richest (NSS 76th round, via MoSPI eSankhyiki).

SDG 6
Targets 6.1 and 6.2 and how the JMP measures them
The WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme (JMP) is the custodian of the global indicators for SDG targets 6.1 (drinking water) and 6.2 (sanitation and hygiene). It publishes an estimate for every country from national surveys, censuses and administrative records, using one set of definitions so that countries and years can be compared.
Safely managed drinking water
Drinking water from an improved water source that is accessible on premises, available when needed and free from faecal and priority chemical contamination (JMP).
Safely managed sanitation
Use of improved facilities that are not shared with other households and where excreta are safely disposed of in situ or removed and treated off-site (JMP).
Basic hygiene
Availability of a handwashing facility with soap and water at home (JMP).
Each definition stacks conditions. A household fails the top rung if any one condition fails, which is why safely managed figures fall well below access figures.

What counts as improved
These are sources that by design can deliver safe water, such as piped water, boreholes and tubewells, protected dug wells and springs, rainwater and packaged or delivered water. The NFHS-6 footnote lists piped water, public taps, tubewells, protected wells and springs, rainwater, tanker trucks, carts with small tanks, bottled water and community RO plants (NFHS-6 India fact sheet (provisional), IIPS, May 2026).
The 30-minute line
Time is measured as a round trip including the queue. A protected well an hour away is improved and still only a limited service. Surveys ask the time, so the line depends on the respondent's estimate.

Why the indicator is a facility
Behaviour is hard to observe in a survey visit, so enumerators ask to see the place where household members usually wash hands and record whether water and soap are there. A facility makes handwashing possible. It does not show that anyone washes at the moments that matter.
Menstrual health indicators
The JMP country file has columns for use of menstrual materials, enough materials, a private place to change, awareness before the first period, ability to reduce pain, comfort seeking help and participation in school, work and social activities. For India every one of these columns is empty (WHO/UNICEF JMP country file, India, updated August 2025).
India's JMP hygiene estimate in 2024 is 89.7% basic, 8.9% limited and 1.4% with no facility (WHO/UNICEF JMP country file, India, updated August 2025).

Method
How the JMP turns surveys into a yearly line
The JMP does not run its own survey. For India it lists inputs from the Census, NSS rounds, NFHS rounds, DLHS, IHDS, LASI, the 2023 Household Consumption Expenditure Survey and the 2023 Comprehensive Annual Modular Survey, alongside administrative records from Jal Jeevan Mission, Swachh Bharat Mission (Grameen and Urban), the Central Pollution Control Board and others. It fits trend lines through these points and reads off a value for each year.
The minimum rule
For safely managed drinking water the JMP takes the lowest of three proportions among people using improved sources, which are accessible on premises, available when needed and free from contamination. It reports the estimate only if quality data cover at least half the population and accessibility or availability data are also present (WHO/UNICEF JMP country file, India, updated August 2025, Charts SM notes).
What this means for the reader
A smooth JMP line between survey years is a fitted value. When the inputs disagree, the fit sits between them. Section 5 shows the rural open defecation points the JMP used for India, which range from under 1% to over 25% for nearby years.
Values the JMP chose not to use appear in square brackets in its data summary sheet. Open the country file before quoting a single year.

Improved sources
An improved source describes the technology at the source
The word improved carries a lot of weight in WASH statistics. It classifies the type of source or toilet, on the reasoning that some designs are more likely to deliver safe water or contain excreta. It does not record whether the water tested clean on the day, whether the tubewell runs dry in May, or whether the pit overflows into a drain.
Two households, one category
A household with a tap in the yard that runs two hours a day and a household with a handpump 25 minutes away whose water carries fluoride above the limit can both be counted as using an improved source. Their risks are very different.
What the quality data show
Among Indians using improved sources in 2024, the JMP estimates 91.3% have water free from contamination, 84.5% have it available when needed and 76.4% have it on premises (WHO/UNICEF JMP country file, India, updated August 2025). On-premises access is the lowest, so it sets the safely managed figure.

Reading ladders
Five habits for reading a ladder chart
  • Read the top rung and the bottom rung together. Progress at the top can hide people stuck at the bottom.
  • Check whether a figure is for people or for households. The JMP counts people, NFHS fact sheets mostly count people, NSS highlights count households.
  • Separate rural and urban before comparing states. A state average mixes two different service models.
  • Look for the word estimate. A JMP figure for 2024 is a fitted value. Nobody measured it in that year.
  • Find the oldest input behind the newest year. Several JMP series for India rest on 2021 to 2023 surveys.
A worked example
The JMP shows rural open defecation in India at 10.7% in 2024 (WHO/UNICEF JMP country file, India, updated August 2025). The most recent survey points behind it are the 2023 HCES (18.0%) and the 2023 CAMS (11.4%), both rural, as listed in the JMP data summary. The 2024 figure extends the fitted trend one year past the newest survey. It may turn out to be right, and it is a projection.
Ladders make progress visible. They also make it easy to quote the rung that flatters a programme. Quote the full ladder.

A missing row
The NFHS-6 India fact sheet carries no sanitation indicator
NFHS-5 reported the share of the population living in households that use an improved sanitation facility, 70.2% in 2019-21. The NFHS-6 India fact sheet released in May 2026 reports 101 key indicators and none of them concerns sanitation, toilets or handwashing. Its household profile keeps electricity, improved drinking water, iodised salt, insurance and bank accounts (NFHS-6 India fact sheet (provisional), IIPS, May 2026).
What is lost
NFHS was the only national survey that measured sanitation in the same households as child height, anaemia and diarrhoea. Without it, a district cannot set its stunting figure beside its sanitation figure from one sample. Researchers will need the unit-level data, if it is released, or other surveys.
Where to look instead
NSS rounds, including the 76th round of 2018 and the 2020-21 round, and the 2023 HCES and CAMS surveys, carry household latrine questions, and the JMP lists them as inputs. The ImpactMojo Deep Dive What NFHS-6 stopped counting collects the readings on this gap.
When a programme report cites NFHS-6 for sanitation, the citation is wrong. Ask which survey the figure came from.

SBM (Grameen)
Swachh Bharat Mission (Grameen): the aim and the design
Swachh Bharat Mission (Grameen) was launched on 2 October 2014 with the aim of eliminating open defecation and achieving ODF status by 2 October 2019, the 150th birth anniversary of Mahatma Gandhi. Phase II began on 1 April 2020 with a focus on keeping villages ODF and adding solid and liquid waste management. The programme has been extended to 2026-27 (DDWS note on SBM(G) Phase II, January 2026).
The household incentive
An incentive of ₹12,000 is paid for building an individual household latrine to BPL households and to identified APL households, namely SC and ST households, small and marginal farmers, landless labourers with homestead, persons with disabilities and women-headed households. States may add to it from their own share (DDWS Year End Review 2025, PIB).
Who pays
The Centre and states share costs 90:10 in the eight north-eastern states, Uttarakhand, Himachal Pradesh and Jammu and Kashmir, 60:40 in other states, and the Centre pays all of it in Union Territories. Phase II also draws on 15th Finance Commission grants to rural local bodies (DDWS Year End Review 2025, PIB).
A subsidy paid on construction gives every official an incentive to report construction. Use is nobody's line item unless the programme makes it one.

Three verbs
Declared, verified and measured mean different things
Declared ODF
The village or its panchayat states that no one defecates in the open, and the status is entered in the programme MIS.
Verified ODF
A later check of the declaration under the programme's own protocol. It is carried out within the government that runs the programme.
Measured open defecation
A sample survey asks households or individuals where they usually defecate, and estimates the share for a population with a known margin of error.
Why the gap is structural
A declaration is binary for a whole village. A survey measures a share of people. A village where 90% of people use toilets is not ODF by definition, yet a declaration can still be filed for it.
Incentive
Officials whose performance is judged on declarations have reason to file them early. Surveys run by statistical agencies have no such stake, which is why they are the right check on a declaration.

Caste and the pit
Why some owners avoid the latrine they have
Diane Coffey and Dean Spears argue in Where India Goes: Abandoned Toilets, Stunted Development and the Costs of Caste (HarperCollins India, 2017) that rural north Indians avoid affordable pit latrines because emptying a pit is associated with the work that caste assigned to Dalits. A household that will not empty its own pit, and will not be seen hiring someone to do it, prefers a large septic tank it cannot afford or no latrine at all.
What it predicts
Use is lower where latrines are small pits, and owners report fear of the pit filling quickly. Households build large tanks when they can. The work of emptying falls on Dalit workers, often by hand, which links Swachh Bharat to the law on manual scavenging in Section 9.
What a programme can do
Promote twin pits with the fact that a rested pit holds decomposed material (NSS defines a rest period of one and a half years), make mechanised emptying available and visible, and never assign emptying to caste-identified workers.
Treat the caste argument as a hypothesis to test in your district, with questions on pit size, fear of filling and who empties.

A reader's checklist
Questions to ask of any ODF claim
  • Is the figure a declaration, a verification or a survey estimate?
  • Who collected it, and does that agency run the programme?
  • Is the unit a village, a household or a person?
  • Was each household member asked, including children's faeces disposal?
  • When was fieldwork, and in which season?
  • Were scheme-benefit questions asked before facility questions?
  • When pits fill, who empties them, and how?
Using it in a district
Take the district's ODF Plus status from the SBM(G) dashboard. Then visit a random sample of hamlets at dawn, the time when open defecation is most visible, and ask each member of sampled households where they went yesterday. Record the state of each latrine. A one-week exercise of this kind tells a programme more about use than any declaration.

Pollution law
The Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974
The Water Act, 1974 set up the Central Pollution Control Board and the State Pollution Control Boards. Together with the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 it is the main law against discharge of untreated effluent and sewage into rivers, lakes and groundwater. Sewage and septage dumped without treatment contaminate the sources that drinking water schemes draw on. The SBM-U ODF++ and Water+ certifications are attempts to make cities treat what they collect, a duty the 1974 Act already implied for discharges.

Pits and tanks
Twin pits, single pits and septic tanks, as the NSS defines them
Twin leach pit
Excreta go to one of two subsurface pits at a time. When one is full, flow is diverted to the second. The filled pit can be emptied after a rest period of one and a half years, during which pathogens are inactivated and the organic matter decomposed (NSS Report No. 584, 76th round (July-December 2018), NSO, 2019, Concepts and Definitions).
Single pit
Desludging is needed as soon as the pit fills, which involves handling fresh, undigested excreta. The NSS calls this hazardous to health and says single pits suit only places with a mechanical vacuum tanker or where the pit is abandoned when full.
Septic tank
A watertight underground settling tank. Its treated effluent usually seeps into the ground through a leaching pit. Sludge accumulates and must be removed and treated.
The twin pit is the design that makes manual contact with fresh excreta unnecessary. A programme that builds single pits is building future hazardous work.

SuperAmma
Changing behaviour without talking about germs
The SuperAmma trial ran in 14 villages of Chittoor district, Andhra Pradesh, between May 2011 and September 2012. It tested whether a village-level campaign built on emotional drivers of behaviour, with knowledge of disease left out of the message, could change handwashing. The campaign used an animated film, skits and public pledging in community and school events (Biran et al., Lancet Global Health, 2014).
What it found
Handwashing with soap at key events rose from 1% to 37% at six months in intervention villages, against 6% in control villages. At twelve months, after controls received a shorter version, both groups were at 29%.
What to take from it
Baseline handwashing with soap was close to zero by observation. The NSS in 2018 recorded 74.1% of households as using soap after defecation. The two differ in method, place and year, and the gap is a warning about self-report. Messages built on emotion moved behaviour. Fourteen villages is a small trial, and the effect at twelve months was measured after the control group was treated.
If your programme reports handwashing from a questionnaire alone, it is reporting what people say. Budget for some structured observation.

Reading null results
Why the trials found so little, and what it does not mean
Coverage too low
In Odisha latrine coverage reached 63% of households. If health gains require most of a community to stop open defecation, 63% may be below the threshold. The authors concluded that the expected health outcome cannot be assumed from coverage (Clasen et al., 2014).
Interventions too elementary
The SHINE authors concluded that household-level elementary WASH interventions in rural low-income settings are unlikely to reduce stunting or anaemia. A latrine and a handwashing station leave animal faeces, contaminated soil and neighbours' faeces untouched.
Adherence fades
In Kenya handwashing adherence fell below 25% by year two and water treatment below 45% in year one, so the trial tested an offer that most households stopped using (Null et al., 2018).
What the null does not mean
Bangladesh found lower diarrhoea in the sanitation and handwashing arms. Karachi found large effects of soap. The trials show that small household packages do not change growth. They do not show that safe water and sanitation for whole communities are without health value.

Reading the two surveys
The NSS 76th round found 87.6% of rural and 90.9% of urban households had sufficient drinking water from their principal source throughout the year (NSS Report No. 584, 76th round (July-December 2018), NSO, 2019). The 2022 JJM assessment found 43% of households with a tap had faced a shortage in piped supply at some time. In Ladakh, Mizoram, Rajasthan, Chhattisgarh, Himachal Pradesh, Jammu and Kashmir, Jharkhand, Odisha and Meghalaya almost six in ten households reported shortages, and the report found seasonal scarcity was being managed by households with limited community action (JJM Functionality Assessment 2022, national report).
A dry-season visit and a post-monsoon visit to the same village can give two different answers to the question of whether a scheme works. Plan for both.

Water stress
Irrigation, groundwater and the projected gap
What NITI Aayog reported (2019)
The Composite Water Management Index reported that about 74% of the area under wheat and 65% of the area under rice faces significant water scarcity, that groundwater accounts for 62% of irrigation water, and that best estimates indicate demand will exceed supply by a factor of two by 2030 (NITI Aayog, CWMI, August 2019).
How to use a projection
The doubling figure is a projection made from 2008 demand estimates and assumptions about growth. Attribute it to NITI Aayog and say it is a projection. The 2025 groundwater account, with 730 over-exploited units, is a measurement and carries more weight in a district plan.

Source sustainability
A pipe network is only as reliable as its source
The December 2025 review describes Jal Jeevan Mission moving from building assets to sustaining service, and names source sustainability planning among the topics in reviews with district collectors (DDWS Year End Review 2025, PIB). The 2022 assessment recorded which villages had groundwater sources and recharge structures, and recommended adding a scheme-level measure of seasonal shortage to future assessments (JJM Functionality Assessment 2022, national report).
Recharge and storage
Jal Shakti Abhiyan has run each year since 2019. Mission Amrit Sarovar built or rejuvenated nearly 69,000 ponds, which store water and recharge groundwater (PIB, 4 December 2025).
Questions for a scheme
Is the source a borewell in an over-exploited block? Has its yield been measured in May? Is there a recharge structure upstream, and is it maintained? Is there a fallback source, and who pays for tankers when the main one fails?
Source sustainability is the climate adaptation measure that a drinking water programme controls directly.

Sanitation and climate
Floods and droughts change how toilets behave
Floods and high water tables
Pits and septic tanks that flood can release excreta into the lanes and into shallow wells nearby. Raised plinths, sealed tanks and planned emptying before the monsoon reduce the risk. DDWS's 2025 toilet repair campaign included awareness on its Climate Resilient Sanitation and Service Delivery Protocol (DDWS note on SBM(G) Phase II, January 2026).
Drought
A pour-flush toilet needs water. When water must be carried, households ration it for drinking and cooking first, and toilet use can fall. In 2018, 4.5% of rural households using a latrine reported no water in or around it (NSS Report No. 584, 76th round (July-December 2018), NSO, 2019, Highlights 15).
Heat
Heat raises water demand for drinking and bathing and can shorten the hours a stretched network supplies. Monitoring in summer catches failures that a winter survey misses.
Design responses
Twin pits sited away from drinking water sources, rainwater storage at schools and anganwadis, and handwashing stations that use little water. Each needs a named person responsible for upkeep.

Measuring behaviour
Self-report, observation and spot checks
Self-report
Cheap and fast. Inflated for socially approved behaviour such as handwashing and toilet use. Ask about the last occasion, since usual practice invites the approved answer. Ask each person separately, and put programme-benefit questions at the end, as the NSS's own warning suggests.
Structured observation
An observer records behaviour at key events over several hours. SuperAmma observed 20-25 households per village and told them the study was about domestic water use to reduce bias (Biran et al., 2014). Expensive, and people behave differently when watched.
Spot checks
Look for signs such as a wet pan in the morning, a worn path to the toilet, soap at the handwashing place, faeces in the lanes at dawn. Spot checks are cheaper than observation and harder to fake than a questionnaire.
Combining them
Use self-report for scale, spot checks for a sample, and observation for a sub-sample, and report the gap between them. The gap is itself a finding about how far reports can be trusted in your area.

Water quality
Monitoring drinking water quality in practice
The 2022 JJM assessment tested twelve critical parameters in district labs. They were turbidity, total hardness, total alkalinity, chloride, ammonia, iron, nitrate, sulphate, total dissolved solids, bacteriological contamination, arsenic and fluoride. pH and residual chlorine were measured on site (JJM Functionality Assessment 2022, national report).
Field test kits
Village women trained to use field test kits tested over 37 lakh samples in 2025-26 up to 29 December 2025 (DDWS Year End Review 2025, PIB). Kits screen for common problems and should trigger a lab test when they show a problem.
Lab testing
Labs returned results for 83% of samples in 2022 and took over 48 hours. A bacteriological test only describes the moment of sampling. Repeat tests at the source, the storage tank and the household tap, in more than one season (JJM Functionality Assessment 2022, national report).
Residual chlorine at the tap is the quickest daily check that a piped supply has been treated. In 2022 it was within limits in about a quarter of households sampled.
