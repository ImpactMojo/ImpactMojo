Section One
What Community Development Is
CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 · ImpactMojo 101 Series03www.impactmojo.in
ImpactMojoCommunity Development 101www.impactmojo.in
Definition
Development done with, not to, people
Community development is a process by which people who share a place or condition act together to shape the decisions that affect their lives — building their collective capacity, organisation and voice along the way. The point is not only the road or the well; it is the capability to act that the community keeps afterwards.
Community development
A process and an outcome: people working collectively to identify their own needs and act on them, strengthening relationships, leadership and self-reliance — not simply delivering goods to passive recipients.
Test of good practice: when the project ends, is the community more able to act on its own future than before? If not, something was delivered, but little was developed.
The definition requiresWhich rules out
People acting togetherIndividual beneficiary transfers
Shaping decisions that affect themBeing consulted on a fixed design
Building collective capacityDelivery that leaves nothing behind
Their own prioritiesA donor theme applied locally
Every clause excludes something common. Most work described as community development fails at least one of these tests, usually the second.
That is not an accusation. Service delivery is legitimate and necessary; the error is calling it community development and expecting its results.

The Contested Word
'Community' is not as simple as it sounds
We speak of 'the community' as if it were one thing with one voice. In reality a village is layered by caste, class, gender, religion, age and faction — with unequal power and conflicting interests.
  • There is rarely a single 'community interest' — there are interests
  • Who claims to speak for the community is itself a question of power
  • Romanticising 'the community' can hide who is being excluded within it
Practitioner habit: never say 'the community wants…' without asking which part, and who was in the room when it was decided.
"The community wants" usually meansCheck by asking
The dominant caste wantsWho was in the room?
The men wantWas there a separate women’s discussion?
The articulate wantWho spoke, and who did not?
The sarpanch wantsChosen how, accountable to whom?
A village is not a unit with one interest. Treating it as one is not simply imprecise — it systematically registers the preferences of whoever is already dominant.
Separate group discussions by gender and social group are the standard corrective, and they are cheap. Mixed groups reliably produce the dominant account.

Many Meanings
Three senses of 'community'
Place
People sharing a location — a village, ward, basti or hamlet
Identity
People sharing a trait — caste, faith, occupation, disability, gender
Interest
People sharing a concern — water users, forest dwellers, weavers
These overlap and cut across each other. A single village holds several identity and interest communities at once — which is exactly why 'the community' can never be assumed to be unified.
Sense of communityBoundaryRisk if assumed
PlaceGeographicIgnores internal division
IdentityCaste, faith, occupationAssumes shared interest
InterestA shared concernExcludes those not affected
The three overlap and conflict. Water users in a village are a community of interest that cuts across caste; the caste groups within it are communities of identity that do not share water interests.
Be explicit about which sense you mean when you write "community" in a design document. Most confusion in this field comes from sliding between them.

Two Directions
Endogenous vs exogenous change
Endogenous (from within)
Change driven by the community's own priorities, knowledge and resources. The outsider supports; the community leads. Slower, but owned — and durable.
Exogenous (from outside)
Change introduced by an external agency, scheme or expert. Faster and well-resourced — but easily resented, poorly fitted, and prone to collapse when funding leaves.
Most real work blends the two. The craft is to bring outside resources in service of an inside agenda — not to substitute the agency's plan for the community's.
EndogenousExogenous
Priorities set byThe communityThe outsider
SpeedSlowerFaster
OwnershipHighLow unless built
Durability after exitHigherDepends on the asset
FitsInstitution buildingEmergency, technical delivery
Neither is always right. A cholera outbreak calls for exogenous action; a village water committee calls for the endogenous route, and confusing them wastes both.
The failure mode is doing exogenous work and reporting it as endogenous, which produces the ownership expectations of one and the results of the other.

The Shift
From 'beneficiary' to 'citizen'
The best way to help people is to help them help themselves.
— a long-standing principle of community development practice
The language matters. A beneficiary receives; a citizen claims rights and holds the state to account. Community development at its best turns recipients of schemes into claimants of entitlements — and authors of their own plans.
Beneficiary framingCitizen framing
Receives what is givenClaims what is owed
Gratitude is the expected responseAccountability is
Excluded by a targeting errorHas a grievance route
Depends on the programmeDepends on the state
The framing changes what a programme builds. A citizen framing requires knowing the entitlement, the office, and the route when it fails — which is deliberate work.
It is also more durable. The programme ends; the entitlement and the panchayat do not.

The Outsider's Place
You are a guest in someone else's life
Whoever you are — NGO worker, official, researcher — you enter the community as an outsider. The community will live with the consequences of every decision long after you have moved on to the next project.
That asymmetry should breed humility, not authority. The people you work with are the experts on their own lives; your expertise is in process, connection and leverage — not in their reality.
As an outsider you carryWhich means
ResourcesYour attention creates incentives
StatusPeople will agree with you
A timetableYours, not theirs
An exitThey live with the consequences
The last row is the asymmetry that matters. Every decision you influence has a longer horizon for the people in the village than it does for you.
Courtesy bias follows from the second row: people tell visiting agencies what they think will keep the resources coming, and this is rational rather than dishonest.

Principle 1
Participation
Participation
People affected by a decision taking an active part in making it — not merely being informed or consulted, but genuinely influencing the outcome.
Participation is the bedrock principle, but it is also the most abused word in the sector. Calling a meeting is not participation if the decisions were already made. We return to how real it is in Section 3.
Called participationActually
An awareness campInforming
A feedback formConsultation
A seat with no votePlacation
A community-managed budgetDelegated power
The word covers everything from being told to deciding, which is why it guarantees nothing on its own and why Arnstein’s ladder is still in use fifty years on.
State the rung you are aiming at in the design document. It is a more honest commitment than the word participation, and it can be checked.

Principle 2
Empowerment
Empowerment
The process by which people who lack power gain greater control over the decisions, resources and institutions that affect their lives — individually and collectively.
Empowerment is not something an agency gives. Power is taken, organised and exercised. The outsider can open space and remove barriers — but cannot hand over agency like a sack of rice.
Empowerment isEmpowerment is not
A process people go throughA benefit an agency delivers
Contested, because power isComfortable
Visible in what people now controlMeasured by attendance
Sustained by organisationProduced by a workshop
Because power is relational, someone gains it only as someone else’s hold weakens. That is why genuine empowerment work produces friction and awareness-raising alone does not.
If a programme claims empowerment and has produced no friction anywhere, it is worth asking what actually changed.

Principle 3
Self-determination
Communities have the right to set their own priorities — even when those priorities differ from the agency's logframe, the donor's theme, or the expert's view of what they 'should' want.
The hard test: are you willing to support a community decision you disagree with? If the answer is always no, you are delivering your agenda, not enabling theirs.
The hard test arises whenThe honest options
They want a temple, you fund WASHSay what you can fund, and why
They want cash, you offer trainingAsk why; consider changing
They rank a road above a schoolSupport it, or explain the constraint
They want nothing from youAccept it
Self-determination is meaningless if it operates only within the agency’s existing menu. The test is whether a community preference can move your allocation.
Where it cannot, say so plainly at the start. A consultation held inside fixed constraints that are not disclosed is the classic form of tokenism.

Principle 4
Equity & inclusion
A village 'consensus' often reflects the voice of dominant caste, class and gender. Equity means deliberately reaching the people a normal process leaves out — Dalits, Adivasis, women, persons with disabilities, the landless.
  • Equality treats everyone the same; equity gives more support where need is greater
  • Hold separate spaces so marginalised voices form before the big meeting
  • Ask of any outcome: who gained, who was absent, who paid?
Consensus in a village meetingUsually reflects
Who could attendTiming, distance, permission
Who could speakCaste, gender, age, language
Who was heardStatus, not argument
Who was asked to agreePublic assent under observation
A unanimous gram sabha resolution is weak evidence of agreement. It is strong evidence about who was present and what could be said aloud.
Separate meetings, secret ballots for sensitive choices, and deliberate outreach to the excluded are the standard correctives, and each costs a little more time.

Sustainability
Sustainability is not only environmental. It asks whether the institution, the skills and the will survive after the project and its money depart.
Social

Financial

Ecological

Working Values
How these values show up in conduct
  • Respect: treat local knowledge as expertise, not ignorance
  • Humility: the outsider is a guest, not the hero of the story
  • Transparency: share budgets, plans and decisions openly
  • Accountability: answer to the community, not only the donor
  • Do no harm: never leave a community worse or more divided
ValueWhat it looks like in a meeting
RespectAsking before telling
HumilityNot sitting at the front
TransparencyThe budget on the wall, in the local language
AccountabilityA grievance route people actually use
Displaying the budget publicly, in the local language, is among the cheapest and most consequential things an agency can do — and among the least common.
Values are legible in conduct, not in documents. Communities read the conduct accurately and quickly.

A Caution
Values can become slogans
Participation has been used both to enable people and to manipulate them. The word alone guarantees nothing.
— a recurring critique in participatory practice
Every principle in this section can be performed without being practised. The chapters that follow are about telling the difference — in the room, not on the poster.
Value performedValue practised
A participation section in the proposalThe community changed the design
An inclusion policyThe meeting was held in the Dalit hamlet
A transparency commitmentThe budget is on the wall
An empowerment objectiveSomeone now holds a decision they did not
Every principle in this section can be performed without being practised, and the performed version produces exactly the evidence a report requires.
That is why this course keeps returning to behavioural tests. A value that cannot be checked by watching a meeting is not yet operational.

The Question
Participation is a matter of degree
Not all 'participation' is equal. Being told about a decision is participation of a kind — but so is making the decision. The gap between them is the gap between tokenism and power.
The classic map of this gap is Sherry Arnstein's Ladder of Citizen Participation (1969) — eight rungs grouped into three levels.
Degree of participationWho decides
Being informedThe agency
Being consultedThe agency, after listening
PartnershipJointly, with negotiation
Delegated powerThe community, within limits
Citizen controlThe community
The right-hand column is the whole content of the ladder. Everything else — meetings, forms, committees — is compatible with any row.
Ask of any participatory process: at the end, who signs? That question sorts the rungs faster than any typology.

The Three Levels
Non-participation, tokenism, citizen power
Non-participation
Manipulation & Therapy. Not participation at all — the aim is to 'educate' or pacify people, not to share power.
Degrees of tokenism
Informing, Consultation, Placation. People hear and are heard — but lack the power to ensure their views change anything.
Degrees of citizen power
Partnership, Delegated power, Citizen control. Real negotiating power and, at the top, genuine control over decisions and resources.
LevelRungsWhat the community gets
Non-participation1-2Education or therapy
Tokenism3-5A voice, no power
Citizen power6-8A share of, or all, the decision
Arnstein wrote in 1969 about American urban renewal programmes, and the ladder has survived because the pattern she named recurs everywhere participation is required by a funder.
Her own framing was blunt: participation without redistribution of power is an empty ritual for the powerless. The middle band is where that ritual lives.

The Trap
Tokenism wears participation's clothes
The dangerous rungs are the middle ones. Consultation and placation look and feel participatory — meetings, feedback forms, a token seat on a committee — while power stays exactly where it was.
Arnstein's sharp point: there is a critical difference between going through the empty ritual of participation and having the real power to affect the outcome.
Tokenism looks likeBecause it has
A well-run consultationMeetings, minutes, attendance
A representative on a committeeA named seat
A feedback mechanismForms and a register
A community planA document with signatures
Every one of these produces exactly the evidence a donor report asks for, which is why tokenism is stable: it is rewarded by the monitoring system.
The distinguishing question is not what was produced but what changed as a result. If nothing in the design moved, the rung was three or four.

Hart's Ladder
Participation for children & youth
Roger Hart adapted Arnstein's ladder for children and young people (UNICEF, 1992). Its lower three rungs name forms of false participation that are especially common with youth.
  • Manipulation: children used to carry adults' messages
  • Decoration: children present for show, not voice
  • Tokenism: a child on the panel who has no real say
Hart's higher rungs run from adult-initiated but shared with children, up to child-initiated, shared decisions with adults — genuine youth agency.
Hart’s false-participation rungsRecognisable as
ManipulationChildren used to carry adults’ message
DecorationChildren present for effect
TokenismChildren speak, without preparation or choice
Hart’s adaptation (UNICEF, 1992) is the standard reference for youth participation, and its lower rungs describe most of what is photographed at events.
His upper rungs include child-initiated projects with shared decisions, which is a far higher bar than most youth engagement is designed to reach.

Beyond the Ladder
Spaces, not just rungs
Later thinkers noted a ladder implies more is always better. Sometimes a community rationally chooses to be merely informed. What matters is whether the level was chosen by the community or imposed on it.
Ask not only 'how high on the ladder?' but 'who decided how high to go, and who opened or closed the space?'
AskRather than only
Who chose the level?Which rung is it?
Was the space invited or claimed?Was there a space?
Can the community leave?Did they attend?
Is there a route to a higher rung?Is this rung good?
Cornwall’s distinction between invited and claimed spaces is the useful addition: a space the community created carries different power from one they were invited into.
A community that deliberately chooses to be informed rather than to run something is exercising power, not lacking it. The imposition is what matters.

A Flip
Start from strengths, not deficits
Most planning starts with a needs assessment: a list of what is wrong, missing and broken. Asset-Based Community Development (ABCD) flips this — it begins by mapping what a community already has.
Pioneered by John Kretzmann and John McKnight (Northwestern University, 1993) in Building Communities from the Inside Out.
Needs assessment producesAsset mapping produces
A list of what is absentAn inventory of what is present
A case for outside resourcesA case for local action
The agency as the answerThe community as the actor
A clientA partner
Kretzmann and McKnight developed ABCD from work in Chicago neighbourhoods that had been described in deficit terms for decades with no improvement.
The critique of ABCD — that it can let the state off the hook — is real and is answered by doing both: map assets, then claim entitlements.

Why It Matters
The deficit lens does quiet harm
Needs-based lens
  • Sees a map of problems and gaps
  • Makes the community a client
  • Hands the agency the power
  • Deepens dependency
Asset-based lens
  • Sees a map of capacities
  • Makes the community the agent
  • Starts with local power
  • Builds self-reliance
A community told only what it lacks learns to wait for outsiders. A community shown what it holds learns to act.
Deficit lens effectObservable
Community internalises the framingPeople list what they lack when asked what they have
Agency becomes the source of solutionsEvery problem is brought to the office
Local capacity is invisibleSkills are not used
Dependency deepensNothing happens without convening
The first row is the one practitioners notice: ask a community what resources it has and the first answers are frequently about what it does not have.
That is a learned response to years of being surveyed for needs, and it takes deliberate facilitation to move past.

Not Either / Or
Assets first — but needs are real
ABCD is sometimes caricatured as ignoring genuine poverty and letting the state off the hook. Done well, it does neither: it starts from strengths and then claims the rights and services a community is owed.
Watch-out: 'use your own assets' must never become an excuse for the state to withdraw. Asset-based does not mean self-financed.
ABCD critiqueThe honest answer
It ignores real povertyIt starts from assets; it does not deny needs
It lets the state off the hookOnly if you stop before claiming entitlements
It romanticises communityPower analysis is still required
It suits austerity politicsWhich is why the rights half matters
The fourth row is the serious version of the objection: self-reliance language can be used to justify withdrawing public provision, and has been.
Do both in sequence and the objection loses force: map what exists, then use the organised community to claim what the state owes.

The SHG
What a Self-Help Group is
Self-Help Group (SHG)
A small, usually women-only group (typically 10–20 members) who save regularly into a common fund, lend to one another, and over time link to banks and act collectively on shared concerns.
The SHG is South Asia's most widespread vehicle of community-level collective action — financial at its root, but social and political in its reach.

The Commons
Managing shared resources together
Much of what poor communities depend on is held in common — grazing land, forests, fisheries, irrigation water, groundwater. The old fear was that shared resources are inevitably overused: the 'tragedy of the commons'.

Ostrom
Elinor Ostrom and governing the commons
Elinor Ostrom won the 2009 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics for showing how communities sustainably manage common resources. Her design principles describe what makes collective governance work.
  • Clear boundaries — who may use the resource, and what
  • Rules that fit local conditions, made by the users themselves
  • Collective choice: those affected help set the rules
  • Monitoring, graduated sanctions & cheap conflict resolution
  • Recognised right to organise; nested layers for larger systems
Ostrom design principlePractical question
Clear boundariesWho is a member, and who is not?
Rules fit local conditionsWere they made here?
Collective-choice arrangementsCan users change the rules?
MonitoringWho watches, and are they accountable to users?
Graduated sanctionsIs the first offence treated as the fifth?
Conflict resolutionIs there a fast, cheap forum?
Ostrom’s point against Hardin was empirical: communities do sustainably manage commons, and the cases that work share these features.
Graduated sanctions is the principle most often missing in designed institutions, which tend to have either no enforcement or a single severe penalty nobody applies.

Read the Room
Power is always in the room
Every community decision happens inside structures of power — caste, class, gender, age, party. Ignoring power does not make it neutral; it just lets the powerful win quietly.

The Core Risk
Elite capture
Elite capture
When the benefits of a programme — resources, decisions, representation — are siphoned by locally powerful individuals or groups, at the expense of the people it was meant to reach.
Participatory and decentralised programmes are especially vulnerable: handing power 'to the community' can simply hand it to whoever already dominates the community.

Bringing power to the surface creates conflict. That is not a failure of community development — it is often a sign that real interests, long suppressed, are finally being voiced.

Suppressing conflict to keep a false peace — which simply preserves the existing imbalance of power.
Do
Surface it, name interests, mediate fairly, and seek outcomes the least powerful can also accept.

The Endgame
Plan the exit from day one
The measure of community development is what survives after the agency leaves. Yet exit is usually an afterthought — a rushed handover when the grant runs out. It should be designed from the very beginning.
Ask on day one: 'What will the community own, run and fund without us in five years?' Build backwards from that answer.
Ask on day oneBecause on the last day
Who runs this afterwards?It is too late to build them
What funds it?The grant has ended
Who holds the records?They walk out with the staff
What is the legal form?Registration takes months
Exit design is cheap at the start and impossible at the end. Every item here takes months to arrange and none can be done in the final quarter.
Write the exit plan into the proposal. Funders rarely ask for one and rarely refuse it, and its absence is the best predictor of what happens after closure.

The Trap
Dependency: the silent failure
Dependency
When a community comes to rely on an external agency for activity, decisions or funds — losing, rather than gaining, the capacity to act on its own.
Generous, well-meaning, sustained support can create dependency just as surely as neglect causes harm. Doing things for people too long teaches them to wait.

Solving each problem brought to youBeing competent
Staying too long in the lead roleStaying too long
The distinction matters: resources do not create dependency, but performing the community’s functions does, however generous the intention.
The commonest route is the most sympathetic one — solving a problem quickly because the community would take longer and might fail.

Ownership
Ownership cannot be transferred at the end
Ownership is not a handover ceremony; it is who decided, who contributed and who controlled resources throughout. A community that did not build it will not defend it.
Low ownership
Agency designed, built and ran it; community 'received' it. Falls into disrepair within a year of exit.
High ownership
Community chose, co-invested and managed it from the start. Maintains and adapts it long after.
Ownership is built byNot by
DecidingBeing consulted
ContributingReceiving
Controlling resourcesBeing informed of the budget
Selecting leadersHaving leaders nominated
A community that did not build something will not defend it. That is why handover ceremonies do not transfer ownership: it accumulates through the process or not at all.
Contribution need not be cash. Labour, land, time and materials all create the stake, and a nominal contribution requested from the start works better than a large one at the end.

Signals

  • Every problem is brought to the agency, not to the local institution
  • The committee cannot name what it will do once funds stop
  • Contributions (cash, labour, time) come only from the project, never the community

Sustaining Finance
Where the money comes from after exit
Financial sustainability is the hardest kind. An institution that depends entirely on the agency's grant has no future once the grant ends. Durable community institutions build their own resource base.
  • Member contributions — savings, fees, a corpus the group owns
  • Linkage to mainstream finance — banks, government schemes
  • Earned income — a service or enterprise the institution runs
  • Claimed entitlements — convergence with public budgets (MGNREGA, NRLM)
The strongest exits leave a community not just willing but able to fund what matters — from its own resources and the public resources it has learned to claim.
Revenue source after exitRealistic when
Member contributions and savingsThe institution serves members directly
A service feeThere is a service people will pay for
Public funds via the panchayatA working relationship exists
Corpus or revolving fundIt was built during the project
A new grantNot a plan
The corpus row has to be decided early: a fund built from year one is substantial by closure, and one started in the final year is not.
The panchayat route is the most underused. A community institution recognised by local government has a route to public money that no grant can replace.

The Frame
Local self-government as the arena
In South Asia, community development largely plays out through local self-government — the panchayat in India, the union council in Pakistan and Bangladesh, the local body in Nepal and Sri Lanka. Knowing this machinery is part of the craft.
Community institutions (SHGs, user groups) and statutory bodies (panchayats) work best when they reinforce, rather than bypass, one another.

73rd Amendment
India's 73rd Constitutional Amendment, 1992
The 73rd Constitutional Amendment (1992) gave constitutional status to panchayati raj — a three-tier system of elected village, block and district councils — as institutions of local self-government.
  • Three tiers: gram (village), intermediate (block), zila (district)
  • Regular elections every five years
  • Reservation of seats for SC, ST and women
  • A Gram Sabha — the assembly of all village voters

The Amendment created the structure and left the substance to the states, which is why panchayat power varies enormously between Kerala and most of north India.

Gram Sabha
The Gram Sabha: direct democracy in the village
Gram Sabha
The assembly of all adult voters in a village — the only directly democratic, face-to-face body in the panchayati raj system, meant to approve plans, select beneficiaries and hold the panchayat to account.
On paper, the Gram Sabha is where community development and the state meet. In practice, attendance and real power vary widely — reviving it is itself a community-development task.

It is the only face-to-face democratic body in the system and therefore the most valuable and the most easily hollowed out.
Strengthening it is concrete work: notice in advance, timing that suits women and workers, an agenda circulated, minutes read aloud, quorum enforced.

Reservation
Reservation: who gets to lead
Reserving panchayat seats and chairs for women and for Scheduled Castes and Tribes has brought more than a million women and lakhs of Dalit and Adivasi citizens into elected office — a structural intervention in who holds local power.
It is not automatic: 'proxy' leadership (the sarpanch-pati husband) persists. But evidence shows real shifts in voice and priorities where reserved leaders are genuinely supported.

Both are true. A quota delivers presence; converting presence into authority takes training, protection and a second term.

NRLM
The rural livelihoods mission at work
DAY-NRLM threads community development through the state: women's SHGs and federations link to banks, run social-audit processes, and increasingly interface with the panchayat — turning savings groups into agents of local governance.

The Gap
Devolution on paper vs in practice
The law devolved functions, funds and functionaries (the '3 Fs') to panchayats. In practice, real powers and budgets often remain with higher tiers and line departments — the unfinished business of decentralisation.
A common practitioner role: helping communities claim the powers the law already grants them — the gap between the statute and the street is where much of the work lives.

FunctionsListed in the Eleventh SchedulePartially
FundsVia finance commissionsOften tied and small
FunctionariesMeant to report to panchayatsUsually report to departments
The third row is the binding constraint. A panchayat that cannot direct the staff working in its area cannot deliver what the law assigns to it.
This is why community organisations often work with line departments directly. It is pragmatic and it also entrenches the gap.

The Core Skill
Facilitation, not instruction
The master skill of community development is facilitation — helping a group think, decide and act for itself, without taking over. The facilitator manages the process so the community owns the content.
  • Ask more than you tell; question more than you answer
  • Draw out the quiet; gently check the dominant
  • Make the process visible — agree it openly with the group
  • Be comfortable with silence, and with not being in control
Facilitator moveInstead of
Ask a questionGive the answer
Wait through the silenceFilling it
Invite the quiet participantTaking the first answer
Summarise what was saidSummarising what you wanted
Let the group decideSteering to your option
Waiting through silence is the hardest of these and the most productive. The first answer in a group is usually the safest one; the second and third are the useful ones.
The facilitator owns the process, the community owns the content. Where that boundary blurs, the output is yours and the ownership will be too.

Do No Harm
First, do no harm
Intervention is never neutral. Bringing resources, attention and new rules into a community shifts its balance of power — sometimes for the better, sometimes not. The do-no-harm lens makes you check the side-effects.
  • Could this deepen existing divisions of caste, class or gender?
  • Could benefits be captured by the already-powerful?
  • Could we be raising expectations we cannot meet?
  • Could we be undermining a local institution that already works?
Ask before interveningBecause
Who gains status from this?Resources shift power
Who loses?Someone usually does
Could this deepen a division?Targeting one group can
Could someone face retaliation?Especially those who speak up
What if we stop suddenly?Withdrawal is also an intervention
The fourth question is the one with real consequences: people who take a visible role in a participatory process can face costs after you leave.
The last is regularly forgotten. Ending a programme abruptly redistributes power just as starting one does, and usually against the same people.

The Long View
Community development takes time
Trust, organisation and leadership grow at the speed of relationships — not project cycles. The work is slow, non-linear and often invisible until, quite suddenly, a community acts on its own.
Beware the funding mismatch: three-year grants chasing changes that take a decade. Manage expectations — yours, the donor's and the community's — honestly.
Takes aboutRealistically
Forming a functioning SHGA year of regular meetings
A federation with bargaining powerThree to five years
Local leaders who can negotiateSeveral years
A community institution surviving exitA decade of support
These timeframes are not pessimism; they are what the successful models in Section 10 actually took, and they are rarely stated alongside the models.
A three-year project cannot build a durable community institution from nothing. It can start one, strengthen an existing one, or achieve something else and say so.
