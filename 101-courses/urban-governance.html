Two Kinds of Town
India has two kinds of town, and only one of them has a municipality
Statutory town
Every administrative unit that a state law defines as urban, such as a municipal corporation, municipality, cantonment board, notified town area committee, town panchayat or nagar palika. The Registrar General treats all of them as urban without applying any test.
A statutory town exists because a state government issued a notification under its municipal law. That notification brings an urban local body, a municipal budget, property tax under a municipal act and eligibility for urban programmes. Most of this course is about statutory towns, because they are the places with an elected urban government.
Census town
A place with no municipal status that meets three tests together: at least 5,000 people, at least 75 per cent of male main workers outside agriculture, and a density of at least 400 persons per square kilometre.
A census town is a statistical category. The Census counts its people as urban, while the state continues to govern it as a village through a gram panchayat under the 73rd Amendment. The same settlement can therefore be urban in national statistics and rural in its budget, its schemes and its building rules.

Where the City Ends
How the Census draws a city's edge with outgrowths and agglomerations
An outgrowth is a village, a hamlet or an enumeration block made of them, with clear boundaries, that has grown up next to a statutory town but outside its legal limits. The Census gives railway colonies, university campuses, port areas and military camps as examples. To count as an outgrowth, the place must have urban infrastructure (paved roads, electricity, taps, drainage, schools, post offices, clinics, banks) and must touch the core town.
An urban agglomeration joins a town and its outgrowths, or two or more contiguous towns, into one unit for counting. It must contain at least one statutory town. This is the closest the Census comes to the city that people commute across every day, and it is a unit that no single council governs.
Why the edge matters for governance
A household in an outgrowth may be counted in a city's population while its water, roads and building permissions come from a gram panchayat or a district office. A city plan prepared by the municipality covers only the municipal area, so the fastest-growing edges are often outside it. Articles 243ZD and 243ZE (section 03) were written to plan across this boundary through district and metropolitan planning committees.

Fieldwork
Census towns are urban in the Census and rural in governance
A World Bank study led by Partha Mukhopadhyay and Marie-Hélène Zérah, with a research team coordinated by the Centre for Policy Research, studied census towns in Bihar, West Bengal, Odisha and Jharkhand. It found an everyday bazaar economy serving surrounding villages, small town-specific trades often rooted in caste occupations, and five new activities common to every site, namely para-transit, construction, private education, private health care and mobile phone services.
Controlling for size, the study found little evident difference in service provision between census towns and statutory towns. Built-up growth spilled outside settlement boundaries everywhere, helped by the absence of building regulations and lower taxes outside towns.
Who wants urban status
The push for municipal status came mostly from local elites and traders. Poorer groups resisted it. In Erein, Odisha, Dalit, Muslim and tribal residents were apprehensive about losing rural welfare schemes. In Bihar, where access to those schemes was poor, the argument for staying rural was weaker.
The report's policy conclusion is to plan at the panchayat, tehsil or district level, because the rural and urban split that structures all public programmes does not match how these places work. It names more active district planning committees as one option.
World Bank Report AUS7449 (2015), Executive Summary, paragraphs 5 to 17.

International
By the international yardstick India is less urban than its neighbours report
Urban population as per cent of total, 2024
World Bank, World Development Indicators, SP.URB.TOTL.IN.ZS, accessed 14 October 2026
Each country reports urban population on its own definition, and the World Bank passes those definitions through. Brazil counts the urban and suburban zones of every municipal and district centre, Mexico any locality of 2,500 people, Argentina any centre of 2,000. India asks a census town for 5,000 people, a density test and a workforce test together, which is demanding by comparison.
The HPEC report made the point with a counterfactual. If Indian villages of more than 10,000 people were classed as urban, the country would have been over 35 per cent urban in 2010 instead of about 30, still well below China at 45 per cent and Indonesia at 54 per cent in that year.

Part IXA
The 74th Amendment gave municipalities a place in the Constitution
Before 1992, municipalities existed only under state laws, which alone set their term, their powers and their taxes. The Constitution (Seventy-fourth Amendment) Act, 1992 inserted Part IXA, Articles 243P to 243ZG, and the Twelfth Schedule. Together they require every state to constitute elected municipalities, hold their elections on a fixed cycle through a State Election Commission, reserve seats, set up a State Finance Commission and create planning committees for districts and metropolitan areas.
The companion 73rd Amendment did the same for rural panchayats in Part IX. The two parts were written in parallel, which is why Article 243Y gives the panchayat finance commission of Article 243-I the job of reviewing municipal finances as well.
What the Amendment fixed and what it left to states
It fixed the existence of municipalities, their five-year term, direct election of councillors, reservation, the election and finance commissions and the planning committees. It left to state legislatures the decisive matters, such as which functions and taxes a municipality gets, how its mayor is chosen, who appoints its staff and how much the state transfers. Most of this section is about that division.

Elections
Councils are elected for five years, through a State Election Commission
Article 243R requires every seat in a municipality to be filled by direct election from territorial wards. State law may add non-voting experts in municipal administration, and voting members such as the area's MPs and MLAs and the chairpersons of wards committees. The manner of electing the chairperson or mayor is left entirely to state law.
Article 243U fixes the term at five years from the first meeting. A council can be dissolved early only after a reasonable opportunity of being heard, and a fresh election must then be completed within six months. Elections must be completed before the term ends.
Article 243ZA
The superintendence, direction and control of electoral rolls and of all municipal elections is vested in the State Election Commission set up under Article 243K. The Election Commission of India has no role in municipal polls. In many states the state government delimits wards and rotates reserved seats, and this is where delays begin (section 04).
Article 243ZG bars courts from interfering in municipal electoral matters except through an election petition under state law.

Planning Committees
The planning committees are the least used part of the Amendment
Article 243ZD requires a District Planning Committee in every state to consolidate panchayat and municipal plans into a draft development plan for the district. At least four-fifths of its members must be elected from among district panchayat members and municipal councillors, in proportion to rural and urban population.
Article 243ZE requires a Metropolitan Planning Committee for every metropolitan area, with at least two-thirds of members elected from councillors and panchayat chairpersons. Its plan must consider spatial planning, sharing of water, integrated infrastructure and environmental conservation, and the chairperson forwards it to the state.

Staff
One municipal post in three is vacant
Municipalities rarely decide their own staffing. The CAG's 2024 audits found staff requirements assessed by state governments, frequent underestimation, and one in three sanctioned posts vacant across the 18 states. Shimla Municipal Corporation needed 720 personnel and was sanctioned 20 new posts.

Case: Bengaluru
Bengaluru replaced one corporation with an authority and up to seven corporations
The Greater Bengaluru Governance Bill was introduced on 23 July 2024, referred to a select committee and passed by both houses in March 2025. It replaces the BBMP Act, 2020, and came into effect on 15 May 2025. The Greater Bengaluru Authority it creates is chaired by the Chief Minister.
  • The Authority is chaired by the Chief Minister, with an appointed Chief Commissioner as member secretary and MPs, MLAs, mayors and agency heads as members.
  • Each corporation needs more than 10 lakh people, more than 5,000 people per sq km and over ₹300 crore of local revenue.
  • Each corporation has up to 150 wards, a mayor and deputy mayor for 30 months, and a commissioner appointed for two years.
  • Each ward committee is chaired by the councillor with 14 other members.

Article 243T
Reservation puts women, Dalits and Adivasis on councils and in mayors' chairs
  • Seats are reserved for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in every municipality in proportion to their population in the municipal area, and may rotate between wards (243T(1)).
  • Not less than one-third of SC and ST seats are reserved for women of those communities (243T(2)).
  • Not less than one-third of all directly elected seats, counting those SC and ST women's seats, are reserved for women (243T(3)).
  • Chairperson offices are reserved for SCs, STs and women in the manner state law provides (243T(4)).
  • States may reserve seats and chairpersonships for backward classes (243T(6)).
Article 243T(5) ends the SC and ST reservations at the same time as reservations in Parliament and assemblies under Article 334. The reservation of chairpersonships for women is outside that sunset clause.
Rotation means a ward reserved for a woman this term may be general next term, so a sitting councillor may be unable to contest the same ward again. The Constitution sets one-third as a floor, and a state law may reserve more.
Backward class reservation is not required by Part IXA. Article 243T(6) only says that nothing in the Part prevents a state from providing it, so its extent differs by state.

User Charges
Water and waste services are paid for mostly from other money
Non-tax revenue was about 30 per cent of municipal corporation revenue in the RBI's 2022 report, dominated by fees and user charges. Property tax and grants pay for most of what remains of service costs.
AMRUT 2.0 made this an explicit reform. States are to meet 20 per cent of water demand from recycled water, reduce non-revenue water to under 20 per cent and reform property tax, user charges and credit worthiness, with incentives for municipalities that do. JNNURM had earlier asked for full recovery of operation and maintenance costs of water supply and solid waste management through user charges.
Non-revenue water
Water that a utility produces but does not bill, through leakage, theft, unmetered public use and billing errors. AMRUT 2.0 set a target of under 20 per cent.
The equity question
A charge that recovers costs raises bills for households that already have a connection, while those without one gain nothing until they are connected. Lifeline tariffs, a low price for the first block of consumption, are one answer. Connecting households comes first, which is what AMRUT 2.0's 2.68 crore tap target is about.

Accounts and Audit
Without published accounts, no money from the Finance Commission
Article 243Z lets state law provide for the maintenance and audit of municipal accounts. JNNURM's mandatory reforms required accrual-based double-entry accounting and annual balance sheets, because cash books record receipts and payments but not what a town owns, owes or is owed.
The 15th Finance Commission made the publication of provisional and audited accounts in the public domain an entry condition for local body grants. The 16th Finance Commission repeats it as one of three entry conditions for every local body grant from 2026-27.

A lender or a credit rating agency needs audited accounts that show what a municipality owns, owes and collects. Pune's 2017 bond carried AA+ ratings from CARE and India Ratings and pledged property tax for repayment.
For a researcher, the published annual accounts and budget books of a municipality are the first documents to collect. Ask for actual figures as well as budget estimates.

Definitions
What a slum is in the schemes, and what a municipality is asked to do about it
Slum (PMAY-U 2.0)
A compact area of at least 300 population or about 60 to 70 households of poorly built congested tenements, in unhygienic environment usually with inadequate infrastructure and lacking in proper sanitary and drinking water facilities.
The definition sets a minimum size and describes conditions. It says nothing about legal status, land ownership or how long residents have lived there, which are settled by state slum laws and by each town's own lists.
Tenable and untenable
PMAY-U 2.0 distinguishes tenable slums, which can be redeveloped where they stand, from untenable ones, such as those on hazardous land, which may be resettled. In-situ redevelopment of tenable slums on public land runs through the AHP vertical. A tenable slum whose households build under BLC must be delisted after its infrastructure deficit is met by the state or town.
'Slum improvement and upgradation' and 'urban poverty alleviation' are items 10 and 11 of the Twelfth Schedule.

Evidence: Relocation
A housing lottery in an Indian city brought better houses and no economic gain
Sharon Barnhardt, Erica Field and Rohini Pande studied a lottery that offered slum residents of an Indian city improved housing on the city's periphery. Because the houses were allotted by lottery, winners and losers can be compared as in an experiment.

Why Slums Persist
Economists who study slums describe a poverty trap
Benjamin Marx, Thomas Stoker and Tavneet Suri reviewed the economics of slums in the developing world in 2013. They argue that poverty in contemporary slums resembles a poverty trap. Three mechanisms keep residents from using the economic opportunities of being near a city. They are thresholds in human capital, inertia in investment, and a 'policy trap'.
They also test the competing view that slums are a transitory feature of fast-growing economies, by examining economic growth, urban growth and slum growth, and whether living standards improve within slums and across generations. They discuss why standard policy approaches have often failed to slow the growth of slums.
What this means for municipal practice
  • Service connections (water, toilets, lights) can be extended without first deciding title, as upgrading programmes do.
  • Ask what tenure a scheme confers. The relocation study found no gain in tenure security even for lottery winners.
  • Resettlement that breaks networks can cancel the gain from a better house (previous slide).
  • Count renters as well as owners when listing households for any scheme.

Implementation
Why the Street Vendors Act is hard to implement
The Act makes the survey the gate to every protection. Until the Town Vending Committee has surveyed and certified vendors, section 3(3) bars eviction, but vendors also hold no certificate to show a police officer. A town that never completes the survey leaves both the protection and the regulation unused.
When the Bill was before Parliament, PRS noted that street vending was then regulated under state municipal laws, and that Parliament's power to legislate depended on whether the Bill was read as about vendors' rights (Concurrent List) or municipal zoning (State List).
Gaps PRS identified in the Bill
  • No principles for issuing certificates, allocating vending zones or deciding the number of vendors per zone.
  • No requirement to consult stakeholders on the street vending plan, which the local authority prepares every five years.
  • The Standing Committee asked for railway land to be covered, for scheme provisions to be written into the law and for the Town Vending Committee to be consulted on the plan.

The Rural Guarantee
The change to the rural guarantee alters the urban debate
The Viksit Bharat Guarantee for Rozgar and Ajeevika Mission (Gramin) Act, 2025 came into force on 1 July 2026, and section 37 repealed the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act, 2005 from that date. Ashwini Kulkarni's reading of the Bill identifies four changes that matter for towns and their edges.
  • Workdays per household rise from 100 to 125 a year.
  • Work is suspended for 60 days during sowing and harvest, on dates the state sets.
  • The Centre funds 60 per cent of total cost and the state 40 per cent, or 90 and 10 in the North Eastern and Himalayan States (section 22). Under the 2005 Act the Centre paid all unskilled wages.
  • The Act applies only to rural areas that the Centre notifies.
Why urban practitioners should care
Census towns and outgrowths are rural in law. Whether they are notified under the new Act decides whether their residents have any statutory work guarantee. Peri-urban villages merged into municipalities, which the 16th Finance Commission's urbanisation premium encourages, leave the rural guarantee altogether and enter towns that have none unless the state provides one.

The May 2010 heatwave in Ahmedabad killed 1,344 more people than usual
All-cause deaths in Ahmedabad, 1 to 31 May

Heat Action Plan
Ahmedabad's Heat Action Plan, the first in India
After the 2010 heatwave, the Ahmedabad Municipal Corporation and partners prepared India's first Heat Action Plan in 2013 and update it periodically. The plan works through four strategies.
  • Public awareness and outreach from March to June, by text message, radio, social media and person-to-person contact with vulnerable groups.
  • An early warning system that passes IMD forecasts to agencies, hospitals, emergency responders, community groups and media.
  • Training of primary medical officers, paramedics and community health staff to recognise and treat heat illness.
  • Reducing exposure, including a city-wide cool roofs programme with mandatory, voluntary and low-income housing measures.

Chennai 2015
Chennai, December 2015: the CAG called the floods a man-made disaster
The CAG's report, 'Flood management and response in Chennai and its suburban areas', was submitted to the Tamil Nadu government in March 2016 and tabled in the Assembly in July 2018. The state's Water Resources Department drew most of its criticism.
  • On 1 December 2015 at 2 pm, with Chembarambakkam tank holding 3.377 tmc and room for 0.268 tmc more, discharge was raised from 12,000 to 20,960 cusecs. The CAG said 12,000 could have been held for six more hours.
  • Discharge of 29,000 cusecs ran for 21 hours on 1 and 2 December into the Adyar river.
  • The CAG found the increase was made to save patta land that had been allowed in the tank's foreshore area from submergence.
  • The tank's operating rules had not been revised for 23 years, and the Adyar had not been desilted.
The lesson for urban governance
The decisions the CAG examined in Chennai were taken by a state department that manages a reservoir, about land in a tank's foreshore. Flood planning that stops at municipal limits, or at municipal departments, will miss where much of the risk is created.

Ward Committees
Wards committees and area sabhas give residents a voice below the council
Article 243S requires wards committees in every municipality of three lakh people or more. A committee may cover one ward or several, the councillor of each ward in it is a member, and where it covers several wards the members elect one of those councillors as chairperson. The state law decides everything else.
JNNURM required states to enact a Community Participation Law and a Public Disclosure Law as mandatory reforms. Under such laws, area sabhas bring together every voter of a polling area. Bengaluru had them under the 1976 and 2020 Acts, where area sabhas proposed development priorities and identified beneficiaries for schemes.
What the 2024 Bengaluru law kept and dropped
Each ward committee is chaired by the councillor with 14 other members. Its functions include preparing ward development plans, ensuring tax collection and maintaining services such as solid waste, sewerage, water supply and street lighting. The area sabhas were removed by the select committee, although the Bill as introduced had kept them.

Practice
A checklist for working with an Indian municipality
Status and power
  • Is the place a statutory town, a census town or an outgrowth? Check against the Census 2027 frozen boundaries.
  • Is there an elected council, or an administrator? When is the next election due under Article 243U?
  • For your issue, does the municipality, a parastatal, a state department or an SPV hold the function?
  • Does the commissioner, the mayor-in-council or the state sign contracts?
  • Is there a wards committee, and does it meet?
Money and evidence
  • Get the latest budget book and audited accounts, and compare budget estimates with actuals.
  • Find the property tax register's coverage and collection rate.
  • Check whether the state meets the 16th Finance Commission's entry conditions.
  • Find the town's AMRUT 2.0 water action plan and its PMAY-U progress by vertical.
  • Ask whether a street vendor survey has been completed and certificates issued.
  • Ask for the heat action plan and the flood contingency plan, and who is named in each.
Every item on this list can be answered from a public document or a right to information request.
