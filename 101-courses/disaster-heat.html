Definition
A disaster is what happens when a hazard meets people who cannot cope
Disaster (DM Act 2005, s. 2(d))
A catastrophe, mishap, calamity or grave occurrence arising from natural or man made causes, or by accident or negligence, which results in substantial loss of life or human suffering or damage to property or the environment, and is of such a nature or magnitude as to be beyond the coping capacity of the community of the affected area.
The last clause carries the idea that runs through this course. A cyclone over an empty sea is weather. The same cyclone over villages of thatched houses, with no shelter and no warning, is a disaster. The wind is the same in both cases. What differs is who is in its path and what they have to protect themselves with. The 2025 amendment added an Explanation to s. 2(d) saying that man made causes do not include law and order situations.
Disaster risk practice therefore separates three things that are often run together in news reports and in project documents. The hazard is the physical event. Exposure is the people and assets in the place where it happens. Vulnerability is the set of conditions that decide how badly those people and assets are hurt. Each one can be changed by a different kind of action, and a programme that does not say which one it is working on usually cannot show that it worked.
Practitioners often call these natural disasters. The Act's definition, and this course, treat the hazard as natural and the disaster as the result of where people live, what they own and how they are governed.

The risk equation
Why the three parts are multiplied together
Risk analysts write disaster risk as a product: risk = hazard × exposure × vulnerability. The Sixteenth Finance Commission built its Disaster Risk Index for allocating money to States on exactly this rule, and explained why. Disaster risk only exists when hazard, exposure and vulnerability intersect. A severe hazard over an empty place carries no risk to people. A large, poor population in a place no hazard reaches carries none either.
The product form has a practical consequence. If any one term falls towards zero, risk falls towards zero, whatever the other two are doing. Moving a village out of a floodplain cuts exposure. Building a cyclone shelter and warning people in time cuts vulnerability for the hours that matter. Neither changes the hazard.
Source: Report of the Sixteenth Finance Commission, Vol. I, ch. 11 (November 2025), paras 11.48 to 11.51.

Illustration
Two villages, one cyclone
Village A (Illustrative)
Pucca houses, a multipurpose cyclone shelter 800 metres away, a trained village task force, a siren tower, a fair price shop stocked before landfall, and fishing boats hauled up two days earlier on official advice. Most families have a mobile phone and received the warning in Odia. The same wind blows the roofs off two cattle sheds and floods the fields for a week.
Village B (Illustrative)
Kutcha houses on a low sandbar, the nearest shelter 6 km away across a creek, landless households who work for daily wages and have no savings, older people living alone, and a hamlet of migrant salt-pan workers who are not on the panchayat list. The warning arrives, but nobody comes to move the people who cannot walk.
The hazard is identical. Exposure is higher in B because of where the houses stand. Vulnerability is higher in B because of what the houses are made of, who lives in them, and who the warning and transport systems were designed for. The loss in B will be measured in lives. The loss in A will be measured in crops and roofs.
  • Every disaster assessment that reports one district-level death toll averages A and B together and hides the difference.
  • Every preparedness plan has to say how it will reach the people in B who are least visible to it: the migrants, the disabled, older people alone, and households outside the official lists.
  • Section 10 returns to this with documented cases from the 2004 tsunami and Cyclone Fani.

Slow and fast
Slow-onset hazards are disasters too, and they are financed differently
A cyclone has a landfall time. A drought has no single day on which it begins, and a heatwave builds over a week. Public systems are better at responding to events with a date. Rescue teams, relief camps and ex gratia payments are designed for the cyclone. Drought and heat harm people through lost income, debt, migration and illness spread over months, and that harm is easier to miss and harder to count.
The financing shows the bias. Of what States spent on notified disasters from 2019-20 to 2023-24, floods took 55.09 per cent and drought 17.26 per cent. Heat was not on the national list of notified disasters at all. It was a State-specific disaster in eleven States, paid for out of a 10 per cent window in each State's response fund (Report of the Sixteenth Finance Commission, Vol. I, ch. 11 (November 2025), paras 11.44, 11.65, 11.66).

Compounding
Hazards arrive together, and one makes the next worse
Disaster risk practice used to plan for one hazard at a time. Recent events in South Asia rarely arrive alone. Pakistan's 2022 floods combined torrential rain, riverine, urban and flash flooding, accelerated glacial melt and landslides. About 33 million people were affected, one in seven Pakistanis, and nearly 8 million were displaced (Government of Pakistan et al., Pakistan Floods 2022 Post-Disaster Needs Assessment).
Heat and drought reinforce each other: hot days dry soil faster, and dry soil heats faster. A cyclone in the middle of a pandemic forces shelters to choose between crowding and distancing. A flood that destroys a primary health centre removes the place where heatstroke cases would have been treated the following summer.

For practitioners
Questions to ask of any disaster risk programme
  • Which hazard is it about, and how was the hazard mapped? Whose data, at what resolution, for which years?
  • Who is exposed? Has the programme counted people who are not on household lists: migrants, people in informal settlements, nomadic and pastoral groups?
  • What makes people here vulnerable, and how was that established? Was there a vulnerability assessment, or a list copied from a guideline?
  • Which of the three terms does the intervention change? A shelter, an embankment, a warning system and a cash transfer act on different terms.
  • What happens to the person who cannot read the warning, cannot walk to the shelter, or will lose a day's wage by going?
These questions come before any discussion of law or money, because the law and the money follow from the answers. A district plan that cannot answer them is a list of departments and phone numbers. A plan that can answer them tells an officer where to send the boat first.

Origins
From Hyogo to Sendai
The Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction 2015-2030 was adopted on 18 March 2015 at the Third UN World Conference on Disaster Risk Reduction in Sendai, Japan, and endorsed by the UN General Assembly. It replaced the Hyogo Framework for Action 2005-2015, and was the outcome of consultations that began in March 2012 and intergovernmental negotiations from July 2014 to March 2015.
It is a voluntary agreement that creates no binding obligations. Countries report against 38 global indicators through the online Sendai Framework Monitor, and UNDRR is charged with supporting implementation, follow-up and review. It sits alongside the Paris Agreement, the Addis Ababa Action Agenda, the New Urban Agenda and the Sustainable Development Goals.

Goal
One outcome and one goal
The substantial reduction of disaster risk and losses in lives, livelihoods and health and in the economic, physical, social, cultural and environmental assets of persons, businesses, communities and countries.
Sendai Framework, para 16, expected outcome
Prevent new and reduce existing disaster risk through the implementation of integrated and inclusive economic, structural, legal, social, health, cultural, educational, environmental, technological, political and institutional measures that prevent and reduce hazard exposure and vulnerability to disaster, increase preparedness for response and recovery, and thus strengthen resilience.
Sendai Framework, para 17, goal
Read the goal as three instructions. Preventing new risk means not building the next school on the floodplain. Reducing existing risk means retrofitting the school that is already there. Strengthening resilience means preparing to respond and recover when the remaining risk becomes a disaster.
The first instruction is the cheapest and the most often ignored. Most risk in fast-growing South Asian cities is being created now, by where housing, roads and industry are built. A framework that only funds response will keep paying for risk that planning decisions made yesterday.
The goal names exposure and vulnerability explicitly. It says nothing about stopping hazards, because most cannot be stopped.

Four priorities
Four priorities for action
PriorityIn plain terms
1. Understanding disaster riskKnow the hazard, who is exposed, who is vulnerable and what capacity exists. Use the knowledge for prevention, mitigation, preparedness and response.
2. Strengthening disaster risk governanceLaws, institutions, plans and national platforms that assign responsibility at every level.
3. Investing in disaster risk reduction for resiliencePublic and private money for structural measures (shelters, embankments) and non-structural measures (land use rules, insurance, social protection).
4. Preparedness and Build Back BetterPrepare for response, act in anticipation of events, and use recovery to reduce future risk.
The fourth priority contains the phrase that became a slogan, Build Back Better. UNDRR describes recovery, rehabilitation and reconstruction as a critical opportunity to integrate risk reduction into development. In practice that means a house rebuilt after a cyclone should be able to withstand the next one, and a school rebuilt after a flood should be on a plinth.
The same priority asks countries to take action in anticipation of events. That sentence is the basis for the anticipatory action discussed in section 09.
The priorities are a checklist for reading a State disaster management plan. A plan that lists response equipment in detail and says nothing about priority 1 has skipped the step that decides where the equipment should go.

Measuring it
How Sendai progress is measured
UNDRR's Sendai Framework Monitor captures progress that member states report themselves against 38 indicators. A sub-system, DesInventar Sendai, lets countries build disaster loss databases to collect the data for targets (a) to (d): deaths, affected people, economic loss and damage to infrastructure.
Self-reporting is the framework's weakness. A country that records few heat deaths will appear to be meeting target (a) on heat whatever is actually happening. Loss databases are only as complete as the death certificates, revenue records and damage surveys that feed them.

The district
The DDMA is where plans meet people
Section 25 requires every State to establish a DDMA for every district. Its members are the Collector as chair, the elected representative of the local authority as co-chair, the chief executive officer of the DDMA, the Superintendent of Police, the Chief Medical Officer and up to two other members. Since 2025 those two may be district officers, disaster management experts or people from civil society organisations (s. 25(2)(f), as substituted). Where a zila parishad exists, its chairperson is co-chair. In Sixth Schedule tribal areas the Chief Executive Member of the autonomous district council is co-chair.
The DDMA prepares the district disaster management plan, coordinates departments, and monitors whether local authorities comply. The Sixteenth Finance Commission calls DDMAs the critical hubs for planning, coordination and implementation, and notes that local authorities, including panchayats, municipalities and autonomous district councils, are responsible for their own plans and for relief and reconstruction in their areas (Report of the Sixteenth Finance Commission, Vol. I, ch. 11 (November 2025), paras 11.7, 11.8, 11.14).
A criticism worth knowing
Jos Chathukulam argues in EPW that a DDMA headed by a bureaucrat, with the elected local functionary only as co-chair, is a sign of centralisation. He notes that in Kerala district panchayat presidents are DDMA co-chairs, but that during COVID-19 the Collectors ran DDMA meetings and the presidents' role was rare. He also argues that neither the 2005 Act nor the 2025 amendment provides financial allocations to the lower levels of administration.
Source: Jos Chathukulam, Disaster Management (Amendment) Act, 2025: Old Wine in a New Bottle?, Economic and Political Weekly, 26 July 2025, Vol. 60, No. 30.
For a practitioner, the DDMA's plan and its meeting minutes are public documents in most States. Read them before designing a district programme.

Cities and forces
Urban authorities and State response forces
Urban Disaster Management Authority, s. 41A
A State may, by notification, constitute a separate authority for its capital and for every city with a Municipal Corporation, except the National Capital Territory of Delhi and the Union territory of Chandigarh. The Municipal Commissioner chairs it and the District Collector is vice chair. It prepares and implements an Urban Plan for disaster management.
Cities had been planned for through district plans drawn for mostly rural districts. Urban flooding, heat islands, building collapse and fire behave differently, and the municipal corporation controls drains, building permits and street-level services. Placing the authority with the Municipal Commissioner puts it with the body that controls those levers. The Act says a State may create these authorities. It does not require them.
State Disaster Response Force, s. 44A
A State may constitute a State Disaster Response Force for specialist response to a threatening disaster situation or disaster, with functions and service conditions set by the State. The 2005 Act provided only the National Disaster Response Force (s. 44).
Many States had already raised such forces before the amendment gave them a legal basis. Odisha created the Odisha Disaster Rapid Action Force after the 1999 super cyclone and had 10 units by 2019 (Government of Odisha, Cyclone Fani Damage, Loss and Needs Assessment (2019), ch. 15). The Sixteenth Finance Commission describes State forces as an important first line of defence alongside the NDRF (Report of the Sixteenth Finance Commission, Vol. I, ch. 11 (November 2025), para 11.15).

Rights in the Act
What the Act promises to people affected by disaster
Minimum standards of relief, now s. 6(2)(w)
The NDMA recommends guidelines for minimum standards of relief, which may include the minimum requirements in relief camps for shelter, food, drinking water, medical cover and sanitation, special provision for widows and orphans, ex gratia for loss of life, and assistance for damaged houses and for restoring livelihoods. Each SDMA lays down State standards, which may not be lower than the national minimum (s. 18(2)(o)).
Prohibition against discrimination, s. 61
While providing relief to the victims of disaster, there shall be no discrimination on the ground of sex, caste, community, descent or religion.
In the 2005 Act the minimum standards sat in s. 12, which said the guidelines shall include those items. The 2025 amendment omitted ss. 12, 13 and 19 and moved the standards into the functions of the NDMA and SDMAs, where the list may include them. Section 13, also omitted, had let the NDMA recommend relief in loan repayment.
The amendment also deleted the words compensation and from s. 61. Before 2025 the ban on discrimination covered compensation and relief. It now names relief only. The list of grounds does not include disability, age or migrant status, so those groups rely on the general law and on the plans.

A practitioner documenting exclusion from relief should cite s. 61 as it now stands, and should not assume it reaches compensation.

Reading the law
What the amendment did not do
Jos Chathukulam's EPW commentary argues that most of the 2025 amendment strengthens a centralised, top-down approach. His points are that the new staffing and regulation powers of the NDMA, the statutory status of the NCMC and HLC, and the removal of the specified purposes for which the National Disaster Response Fund may be used all concentrate authority, that local governments are given no larger role, and that the amendment contains no comprehensive provisions for pandemics or for the particular vulnerability of women, persons with disabilities and marginalised groups.
Source: Jos Chathukulam, Disaster Management (Amendment) Act, 2025: Old Wine in a New Bottle?, Economic and Political Weekly, 26 July 2025, Vol. 60, No. 30.
The government's case for the amendment rests on clarity and data: definitions aligned with Sendai, plans prepared by the Authorities themselves, a national and State disaster database, preparedness assessments and post-disaster audits. Both readings can be true at once. A database and an audit power are useful tools, and who controls them decides whom they serve.
  • Ask whether your State has notified an Urban Disaster Management Authority for its cities, and who sits on it.
  • Ask whether the State has published its disaster database, and whether it records deaths by sex, age and social group.
  • Ask whether panchayats have their own disaster plans and any money to act on them.

FC-16 formula
How the Sixteenth Finance Commission shares money among States
Each State's allocation is 70 per cent based on its average past expenditure on disasters from 2011-12 to 2023-24, leaving out the COVID-19 years 2020-21 and 2021-22, and 30 per cent based on its score on the Disaster Risk Index. The base year allocation rises by 5 per cent a year.

Windows
What the money can be spent on
Under the Fifteenth Commission, the response fund had three sub-windows: Response and Relief (40 per cent), Recovery and Reconstruction (30 per cent) and Preparedness and Capacity Building (10 per cent), with the remaining 20 per cent going to the mitigation fund. The Economic Survey 2021-22 summarised the 80:20 division between response and mitigation for both State and national funds.
The Sixteenth Commission keeps 80:20 but changes the inside. States get full flexibility between Response and Relief and Recovery and Reconstruction, and Preparedness and Capacity Building moves to the mitigation funds, on the reasoning that preparedness happens before a disaster and response after it. Money may not be shifted between the response and mitigation funds because they are separate funds under the Act.

The coast
Why Odisha is the place to study cyclone preparedness
The Fani assessment, led by the Government of Odisha with the UN, World Bank and Asian Development Bank, records that of 1,019 cyclonic disturbances in the Indian subcontinent in the last century, 890 were on the eastern coast, and 260 of those made landfall on the Odisha coast. Odisha has ten major river systems that flood regularly, and is also exposed to flash floods, landslides, earthquakes and tsunami.
The state is poor and rural. In 2011, 83 per cent of its 4.2 crore people lived in rural areas, Scheduled Tribes were 22.8 per cent and Scheduled Castes 17.1 per cent of the population, and poverty, though it fell from about 57 per cent in 2004-05 to about 33 per cent in 2011-12, remained well above the national average of about 22 per cent. That combination of high hazard and high vulnerability is what makes its fall in cyclone deaths instructive.

1999 and after
What Odisha built after the 1999 super cyclone
The super cyclone of October 1999 killed nearly 10,000 people in Odisha, in the figure the state's own assessment uses. In its immediate aftermath the government set up the Odisha State Disaster Management Authority as an autonomous organisation, before the national Act existed. It also raised the Odisha Disaster Rapid Action Force, a trained and equipped state rescue force.
Over the next two decades Odisha built multipurpose cyclone shelters with community task forces linked to each one, an Early Warning Dissemination System with five layers of communication and sirens on the coast, District Emergency Operation Centres in all 30 districts, a GIS cell, a drought monitoring cell and a lightning detection system. The statutory SDMA under the 2005 Act was constituted in 2010, with the Chief Minister as chair and the Special Relief Commissioner's office as its secretariat. Through the national Cyclone Risk Mitigation Project, Odisha strengthened its last-mile warning system and built and maintained multipurpose cyclone shelters.

Who was still hurt
What the Fani assessment found about exclusion
Relief tied to ration cards
The immediate cash transfer of ₹2,000 and 50 kg of rice was given only to ration card holders. The assessment records that this excluded many vulnerable families, including migrants and brick kiln workers who were non-residents and were equally affected. The 50 kg was the same for every family whatever its size. It recommended giving cash and grain to all affected people without proof of residency, and distributing grain by family size.
Segregation inside shelters
In the days after the cyclone, details emerged of segregated arrangements for food and use of facilities by a dominant social class, which forced socially vulnerable groups to shelter in other buildings such as anganwadi centres. The assessment recommended placing new cyclone shelters closer to Dalit hamlets.
These are the state government's own findings, in a report it led with the UN, the World Bank and the ADB. They show that a system which saves lives can still distribute shelter and relief unequally. Women, people with disabilities and other socially and economically vulnerable groups were, in the report's words, severely affected despite the evacuation.
  • Recovery recommendations included housing help for women-led households, cash for work, advance payment of social security entitlements, and assistive devices for persons with disabilities.
  • It asked for the Odisha Relief Code and the SDRF and NDRF norms to be reviewed so that relief conditions do not exclude affected people.
  • It recommended including low income communities in the bodies that decide on reconstruction.

Floods
Floods take most of India's disaster money
Floods accounted for 55.09 per cent of what States spent on notified disasters from 2019-20 to 2023-24, far more than any other hazard (Report of the Sixteenth Finance Commission, Vol. I, ch. 11 (November 2025), Figure 11.5). They are frequent, they cover large areas, and most of their cost is in lost crops, damaged houses and broken roads. Deaths are a smaller part.
Flood risk has four sources that call for different responses. Riverine floods build over days and can be forecast from upstream gauges. Flash floods and cloudbursts in the hills give minutes of warning. Urban floods happen when drains cannot carry heavy rain, and are made worse by building over lakes and channels. Coastal floods come with cyclone surge.

Embankments
Embankments protect some places and endanger others
An embankment keeps a river out of the land behind it up to its design height. When the river exceeds that height, or the embankment breaches, the water arrives suddenly into places that people had come to believe were safe. Embankments also stop silt from spreading over the floodplain, so the river bed rises between the walls over the years and the river sits higher than the land around it.
This is why flood planners in India distinguish structural measures (embankments, dams, channels) from non-structural ones (zoning, warning, insurance, raised houses, flood-tolerant crops). The Sixteenth Commission records that States have used SDMF for flood protection walls, dam restoration, drains and groundwater recharge (Report of the Sixteenth Finance Commission, Vol. I, ch. 11 (November 2025), para 11.38).

Pakistan 2022
The largest recent flood disaster in South Asia
Between June and August 2022, torrential rain and a combination of riverine, urban and flash flooding caused what the Pakistan Post-Disaster Needs Assessment calls an unprecedented disaster. About 33 million people, one in seven Pakistanis, were affected and nearly 8 million were displaced. More than 1,700 people died, a third of them children. By 11 October, 94 districts had been declared calamity hit, and 19 of the country's 25 poorest districts were among them.
The assessment projected that the floods would raise the national poverty rate by 3.7 to 4.0 percentage points, pushing between 8.4 and 9.1 million people into poverty. Damage was estimated at USD 14.9 billion, about 4.8 per cent of FY22 GDP, losses at USD 15.2 billion and recovery needs at USD 16.3 billion.

Landslides
Landslides: small share of spending, large share of fear in the hills
Landslides took 2.59 per cent of State disaster spending from 2019-20 to 2023-24, and cloudbursts 0.32 per cent (Report of the Sixteenth Finance Commission, Vol. I, ch. 11 (November 2025), Figure 11.5). Those shares are small because the hill States are small. For the people of the Himalayan and north-eastern States and the Western Ghats, landslides cut roads for weeks and can bury a settlement in minutes.
Landslide risk is raised by rain, by cutting slopes for roads and buildings, by removing vegetation, and by blocking natural drainage. Most landslide warning in practice uses rainfall thresholds: when accumulated rain passes a level known to trigger slides on a slope, people below are moved.

Lightning
Lightning kills more Indians than any other force of nature
NCRB data, as quoted by the Sixteenth Finance Commission, show lightning as the leading cause of death from forces of nature in India. In 2022 it accounted for 2,887 of 8,060 deaths, or 35.8 per cent. The Annual Lightning Report prepared with IMD for 2020-21 recorded about 18.5 million lightning strikes, a 34 per cent rise on the previous year.
The Commission notes who dies. Agricultural labourers working in open fields, with no shelter and near water, face the highest risk, and people living in mud houses are more exposed to ground strikes. Sixteen States had notified lightning as a State-specific disaster when the Commission reported, and it recommended adding lightning to the national list (Report of the Sixteenth Finance Commission, Vol. I, ch. 11 (November 2025), paras 11.69 to 11.73).

For practitioners
Working on floods, landslides and lightning in a district
  • Get the district hazard map and the list of villages flooded in each of the last ten years. The pattern usually repeats.
  • Find out where the embankments are, when they were last repaired, and which villages sit between them and the river.
  • For hill districts, ask whether the district has rainfall thresholds for landslide alerts and who acts on them.
  • For lightning, ask whether the warning reaches people working in fields, and whether schools and panchayat buildings have lightning conductors.
  • Check whether the district has proposed projects to the State mitigation fund. Under-used mitigation money is common.
The same five questions work for a programme officer in an NGO and for a block development officer. They turn a general concern about floods into a short list of records to ask for and people to visit.

The categories matter because they arrive in sequence. Rain fails first. Soil dries and crops fail weeks later. Wells and tanks fail months later. People migrate, sell animals and borrow throughout. A declaration based only on rainfall comes early and may miss a district where rain was near normal but badly timed. Drought harms people mainly through lost work and income, which is why the response that matters most is often employment and food over months. A single relief payment does not replace lost work.

Work in a drought

Declaring drought
What a declaration decides, and who is left out
A drought declaration by a State government opens access to relief norms, central assistance and scheme relaxations. The timing and the unit of declaration, usually a block or tehsil, decide who gets help. A village in a block that is not declared gets nothing, however badly its own crops failed.
Declarations rely on a mix of rainfall, crop sowing, vegetation, soil moisture and water indicators, followed by field verification. The Union Agriculture Ministry publishes the procedure that States are expected to follow. Practitioners should read the current version of that procedure before advising a State, because its indicators and thresholds have been revised over time.

Warm night (IMD)
Considered only when the maximum stays at 40°C or more. A warm night is a minimum temperature 4.5°C to 6.4°C above normal. A very warm night is more than 6.4°C above normal.

Heat in India
Hotter days, and nights that do not cool
The Sixteenth Finance Commission records that the frequency and intensity of hot days in India increased significantly over 1981 to 2022, with major heatwaves in 2013, 2016, 2019, 2022 and 2024, and a marked rise in very warm nights in the last decade. It explains why nights matter: warm nights stop the body recovering from daytime heat, which raises the risk of heat illness such as heatstroke (Report of the Sixteenth Finance Commission, Vol. I, ch. 11 (November 2025), paras 11.66, 11.67).
CPR's review of heat action plans adds that three-day runs of concurrent hot days and hot nights increased significantly over 1951 to 2016, and are projected to increase two to four times by 2050 under moderate and high emission scenarios (Pillai and Dalal, How is India Adapting to Heatwaves?, CPR (March 2023), citing published projections).

Humid heat
Wet-bulb temperature, and why humidity changes the danger
Wet-bulb temperature
The lowest temperature air can reach by evaporating water into it. It combines heat and humidity. The body cools itself mainly by evaporating sweat, so as the wet-bulb temperature approaches skin temperature, sweating stops working.
Sherwood and Huber argued in 2010 that a wet-bulb temperature above 35°C for an extended period would cause hyperthermia in humans, because metabolic heat could no longer be lost, and noted that observed peak wet-bulb temperatures then did not exceed 31°C (PNAS 107(21):9552, 2010).
Vecellio and colleagues tested the idea on young, healthy volunteers in a chamber. In humid conditions the critical wet-bulb temperature at which heat stress became uncompensable averaged 30.55°C, well below 35°C, and no subject reached 35°C. In hot, dry conditions it was lower still (J Appl Physiol 132(2):340, 2022).

Ahmedabad 2010
The heatwave that started South Asia's first heat action plan
In May 2010 Ahmedabad's temperature reached 46.8°C. Azhar and colleagues compared all-cause deaths in the city for 1 to 31 May 2010 with the average of the same period in 2009 and 2011. There were 4,462 deaths in May 2010 against a reference of 3,118, an excess of 1,344 deaths, or 43.1 per cent. During the most intense week, 19 to 25 May, mortality rate ratios were 1.76 to 2.12 depending on the reference period.
The excess deaths were not recorded as heat deaths. They appeared in the city's death registrations as deaths from all causes, which is why the method matters. Counting deaths certified as heatstroke would have found a small fraction of the 1,344.

The plan

Did it work
Evaluating the Ahmedabad plan
Hess and colleagues compared summer all-cause mortality in 2014-2015, after the plan, with a 2007-2010 baseline. Before the plan, the mortality rate ratio at 47°C was 2.34 (95% CI 1.98 to 2.76). After it, the ratio at 47°C was 1.25 (1.02 to 1.53). They estimated 1,190 deaths avoided a year on average (95% CI 162 to 2,218).
Read the uncertainty as carefully as the headline. The confidence interval on avoided deaths runs from 162 to 2,218. The incidence rate ratios for days above 40°C and above 45°C were 0.95 (0.73 to 1.22) and 0.73 (0.29 to 1.81), both consistent with no change. The study is a before and after comparison in one city, so other changes over the same years, such as air conditioning, could account for part of the fall. The authors call it a pilot evaluation.

Counting the dead
Why recorded heat deaths are a small part of the true toll
The Sixteenth Finance Commission quotes NCRB data showing 3,798 deaths from heat or sunstroke in the whole of India from 2018 to 2022, about 760 a year (Report of the Sixteenth Finance Commission, Vol. I, ch. 11 (November 2025), para 11.67). In one month of 2010, in one city, Azhar and colleagues counted 1,344 excess deaths from all causes during a heatwave (Azhar et al., Heat-related mortality in India: excess all-cause mortality associated with the 2010 Ahmedabad heat wave, PLoS One 9(3):e91831, 2014).
The two numbers measure different things, and the gap between them is the point. NCRB counts deaths that police record as caused by heat or sunstroke. Most heat deaths are certified as heart attacks, strokes, kidney failure or simply old age, often at home, and never reach that category. Excess mortality, comparing all deaths in a hot period with deaths expected in a normal one, captures them without needing a cause on the certificate.

The ILO notes that the Factories Act 1948 and the Building and Other Construction Workers Act 1996 were the statutes relevant to such work, and that neither made clear how the occupational safety and health of brick workers was to be protected (ILO, Working on a Warmer Planet (2019), Table 6.2, Southern Asia, 1995 and 2030 (projections), Box 6.1). Labour law has changed since the report, so check which occupational safety law and rules now apply in your State.
Practical measures that heat plans recommend for outdoor work include shifting hours away from the afternoon, water and shade at the work site, rest breaks, and protecting the wages of workers who stop in extreme heat. Illustrative.

Trust
False alarms, missed alarms and trust
Every warning system trades two errors. A low threshold gives more false alarms: people leave homes, lose wages and come back to find nothing happened. A high threshold gives more missed events: people stay home and the hazard arrives. After a few false alarms people stop leaving, so the second error starts to follow from the first.
Fani's forecast track was revised nine times while the storm meandered over the Bay of Bengal, and Odisha was expected to be spared in the early days (Government of Odisha, Cyclone Fani Damage, Loss and Needs Assessment (2019), ch. 15). The response was still timely because evacuation began only once landfall in Odisha was confirmed, with enough lead time left to move people.
  • Explain uncertainty plainly: say how likely the event is and when it will be known for sure.
  • Compensate people for the cost of acting: food in shelters, wage protection for workers who stop in heat.
  • After every event, publish what was forecast, what happened and what was done.
  • Keep warning language the same across seasons so people recognise it.
  • Involve the people warned in setting the thresholds that affect them.

Relief is fast, visible and mostly paid by the State response fund. Recovery is slow, harder to see and easy to underfund. Under the Fifteenth Commission's national fund, the Response and Relief window had released ₹14,855 crore of its ₹27,385 crore by 31 July 2025, while the Recovery and Reconstruction window had released ₹818.74 crore of ₹20,539 crore (Report of the Sixteenth Finance Commission, Vol. I, ch. 11 (November 2025), Table 11.1).
Recovery decides whether a household returns to where it was. A family that has sold its livestock, taken a high-interest loan and pulled a child out of school may be counted as relieved and still be poorer for a decade.
The definition of recovery in the Act includes improvement as well as restoration. That is the legal hook for Build Back Better.

Caste
The 2004 tsunami: Dalits blocked from relief
The December 2004 tsunami killed at least 10,273 people in India, 8,010 of them in Tamil Nadu, and displaced at least 6,47,556 into emergency shelters. Human Rights Watch reported consistent accounts of caste discrimination in the distribution of aid. Fishing communities, who regard themselves as a higher caste, often blocked relief from reaching Dalits.
In one case a fishing community refused to share water provided by UNICEF and other relief organisations, claiming the Dalits would pollute it. A Dalit rights activist told HRW that relief materials could not be taken to Dalit areas because the fishing community refused to let them pass. Coastal farm labourers, sharecroppers and tenants, many of them Dalits, lost their crops and wages to seawater but were ignored in the first relief because they had suffered fewer deaths.
Source: Human Rights Watch, After the Deluge: India's Reconstruction Following the 2004 Tsunami (May 2005).
We had problems earlier as relief materials could not be taken to Dalit areas because the fishing community refused to let us pass.

The government changed some policies when told of discrimination, and later assessed damage in Dalit communities and distributed rations to them. HRW's conclusion was that the greatest source of trouble was discrimination against Dalits by other victims of the tsunami, and that rooting it out required proactive measures from the government.
Fifteen years later, the Fani assessment recorded segregated food and facilities in cyclone shelters and recommended placing new shelters closer to Dalit hamlets (Government of Odisha, Cyclone Fani Damage, Loss and Needs Assessment (2019)).

Women
When relief is paid to the head of household
After the tsunami, Human Rights Watch found that women's access to aid was brokered through husbands, fathers or other male relatives, because ration cards, the main proof of identity and residence, were issued in the name of the male head of the household. Women complained that relief and compensation were distributed through male heads, and press reports described cash relief that did not reach families. Unmarried, widowed and divorced women were often absorbed into the households of relatives instead of being counted as independent households.
The government did not treat women from fishing communities as workers, although most cleaned, sold and processed fish before the tsunami. Vocational offers of basket weaving or sewing gave low, irregular income and reinforced stereotypes about women's work (Human Rights Watch, After the Deluge: India's Reconstruction Following the 2004 Tsunami (May 2005)).

Migrants
People who are not on the list

Disability and age
People who cannot reach the shelter
Human Rights Watch noted after the tsunami that disasters hurt persons with existing disabilities disproportionately and create new disabilities among the injured. People lose assistive devices such as crutches, artificial limbs and hearing or visual aids, and their usual caregivers may be killed, injured or diverted to other tasks. It recommended that shelters, health centres, housing and schools rebuilt after the disaster be accessible (Human Rights Watch, After the Deluge: India's Reconstruction Following the 2004 Tsunami (May 2005)).
The Fani assessment found that people with disabilities were among the most affected despite the evacuation, and recommended supplying assistive devices and giving self-help groups of persons with disabilities better market links (Government of Odisha, Cyclone Fani Damage, Loss and Needs Assessment (2019)).

  • Make at least one shelter per cluster accessible: ramps, accessible toilets, space for wheelchairs.
  • Include organisations of persons with disabilities in drawing up the plan, as Sendai para 36(a)(iii) asks.
  • Stock replacement assistive devices and medicines for chronic illness.
  • For heat, check on older people living alone during every alert.

Risk layering
Different layers of risk need different money
Disaster risk finance divides losses into layers by how often they happen and how large they are. Small, frequent losses are cheapest to absorb from savings, budgets and social protection. Medium losses are what State response funds are for. Rare, very large losses are where insurance, reinsurance and catastrophe bonds can move risk off the public budget.
India relies mostly on public funds. The Sixteenth Finance Commission found that insurance as a supplement to public disaster finance remains at a nascent stage, except for crop insurance, because of few products, low penetration, low awareness and high cost, and because governments prefer post-disaster payouts to buying cover in advance (Report of the Sixteenth Finance Commission, Vol. I, ch. 11 (November 2025), para 11.88).

Parametric cover
Insurance that pays when a measurement crosses a threshold
Parametric insurance
Cover that pays a pre-agreed amount when a measured index crosses a threshold, such as rainfall at a weather station, wind speed or temperature, without a survey of each policyholder's loss. It pays fast. Its weakness is basis risk. A household can suffer a loss when the index does not trigger, or receive money when it suffered none.
Nagaland is the Sixteenth Commission's example. Its Disaster Risk Transfer Parametric Insurance Solution is described as the first initiative in India to insure an entire State against extreme rainfall, using automated weather stations to set triggers, in a partnership of the Nagaland SDMA, SBI General Insurance, Munich Re and GIC Re. The Commission calls it a success that remains isolated and limited in scale (Report of the Sixteenth Finance Commission, Vol. I, ch. 11 (November 2025), para 11.90).

Social protection

Shock-responsive social protection uses programmes that already exist to deliver help when a disaster strikes. A programme can pay more to people already enrolled (vertical expansion), add affected people who were not enrolled (horizontal expansion), relax its rules for a time, or lend its payment system and lists to a relief operation.
