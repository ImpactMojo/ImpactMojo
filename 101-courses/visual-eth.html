What Is Visual Ethnography?
CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 · ImpactMojo 101 Series03www.impactmojo.in

Definition
Seeing culture, not just describing it
Ethnography is the close study of a culture or social world from the inside. Visual ethnography does that work with and through images — photographs, video, objects, drawings, the whole visual fabric of everyday life — both as data and as a way of knowing.
Visual ethnography
The study of culture and social life in which images are central to producing, analysing and communicating knowledge — not mere illustration of words, but a method in their own right.
The camera is not a neutral recorder. It is a research instrument — and a social act — that you must learn to use with the same care as an interview guide.
Visual ethnography isIt is not
A method of producing knowledgeIllustration for a written report
Images treated as evidenceImages chosen for appeal
A relationship with those depictedA collection activity
Analysed systematicallyPresented and left to speak
The last row is the commonest failure in development practice. Images are gathered, captioned and published without ever being read as data — which makes them decoration with a methods section attached.
The test is whether an image could change your conclusion. If no photograph you might have taken would have altered the finding, the images are not evidence in the study.

Why Images
What images do that words cannot
Images capture
  • The texture of a place — light, density, wear
  • Bodies, gesture, dress, spatial arrangement
  • What people take for granted and never say
  • Material culture: tools, objects, thresholds
Words capture
  • Meaning, motive, interpretation
  • Sequence, cause, history
  • Abstraction and generalisation
  • What an image cannot show on its own
Visual ethnography works best when images and words travel together — the photograph raises questions the interview then answers.
Images carryWords struggle with
Texture — light, density, wearDescribing without evaluating
Spatial arrangementWho stood where, relative to whom
Gesture and postureBearing, which people rarely narrate
The taken-for-grantedWhat nobody thinks to mention
The last row is the strongest argument for the method. A photograph records the ordinary arrangement of a room, which no interview would produce because nobody considers it worth saying.
Images are also weak where words are strong: they cannot state a cause, a sequence, or a counterfactual. That is why the method pairs them with talk rather than replacing it.

Not This
What visual ethnography is not
  • Not stock photos dropped into a report to fill space
  • Not 'a nice picture' chosen for beauty over meaning
  • Not surveillance — watching people without their knowledge
  • Not a shortcut that skips consent, context or analysis
The discipline is in the method: systematic, reflexive, consented, and analysed — not just photogenic.
Not visual ethnographyWhy

South Asia
A deeply visual region
South Asia is saturated with images — cinema hoardings, calendar gods, wedding photography, wall murals, WhatsApp forwards, NREGA muster boards, ration-shop signage. The visual is where much social meaning already lives.
For a development researcher this is an opportunity: you are studying people who are already skilled makers and readers of images. Visual methods meet them on familiar ground.
South Asian visual formWhat it can be read for
Calendar art and framed godsHousehold religion, aspiration
Wedding photographyKinship, status, gendered display
Wall murals and signagePolitics, public health messaging, caste
NREGA muster boardsThe state made visible, or not
WhatsApp forwardsRumour, mobilisation, what circulates
The fourth row is a research site hiding in plain sight. Whether a muster board is displayed, updated and legible is an observable fact about transparency that requires no interview.
A region saturated with images is one where people are already fluent readers of them. Participants generally have sharper visual literacy than the researcher assumes.

Origins
Photography and anthropology grew up together
Photography (1839) and modern anthropology emerged in the same century, and were entangled from the start. Early ethnographers carried cameras into the field believing the photograph offered objective, scientific record.
That belief — that the camera simply captures truth — is exactly what the rest of this course will dismantle. The image is always made by someone, for someone.
Early ethnographic photographyAssumption
Photographed physical typesThat culture was visible on the body
Used measuring rods and gridsThat people could be catalogued
Posed subjects frontallyThat the specimen convention applied
Rarely named the personThat the individual did not matter
The camera arrived with anthropology and was shaped by it. These were not aesthetic choices but methodological ones, expressing a theory about what people are and what could be known from looking.
The conventions survive in unexpected places. A frontal, unnamed portrait of a "beneficiary" in a donor report repeats the specimen grammar without intending to.

The Colonial Gaze
Photography as an instrument of empire
In colonial South Asia, the camera was used to classify, measure and control. Projects like The People of India (1868–75) catalogued communities as 'types' — bodies arranged for the imperial eye.
  • People posed as specimens, stripped of name and voice
  • Anthropometric photographs to rank and racialise
  • Images made about people, never with them
  • The subject had no say in how they were seen or used
The People of India (1868–75)Function
Photographed castes and tribesClassification
Compiled after 1857Knowing a population in order to govern it
Captioned by type, not nameThe person as an instance of a category
Circulated to administratorsMaking the categories official
Photographic classification helped make colonial categories real. Recording people as instances of a caste or tribal type contributed to the fixity those categories acquired in administration.
This is the history behind recognition justice: a photographic archive that told people who they were, in a form the state could act on. It is worth knowing before you photograph anyone as a representative of anything.

Salvage
The 'salvage' paradigm
Salvage ethnography
The early-20th-century drive to photograph and record 'vanishing' cultures before they disappeared — freezing living peoples as relics of a dying past.
The salvage gaze treated communities as already-dead museums rather than living, changing societies. It denied people a present and a future — a politics hiding inside an apparently caring impulse.
The salvage claimWhat it obscured
"This culture is vanishing"It was being dismantled, often by the same administration funding the camera
"Record it before it goes"Recording substituted for asking what people wanted
"Authentic" dress and toolsStaged; everyday clothing was edited out of frame
"A people without history"Denied change, so denied politics
Salvage logic did not end in 1930. A grant proposal promising to document a "disappearing craft" or a "last generation of speakers" is using the same frame — and it still tends to photograph the practice rather than the pressure destroying it.
The test: does your project have a place to record what people say they want to keep, change or abandon? If not, you are salvaging.

Turning Point
Bateson and Mead: the systematic turn
Gregory Bateson and Margaret Mead's Balinese Character (1942) used 759 photographs analysed systematically — an early attempt to treat images as rigorous data rather than decorative illustration.
A landmark, but still firmly in the observational tradition: the anthropologists made and arranged the images; the Balinese were studied, not collaborators.
Balinese Character (1942)Innovation
759 photographs, systematically analysedImages as data, not illustration
Sequences of the same actComparison across instances
Paired with written analysisNeither medium alone
Explicit selection criteriaAnalysis that could be checked
Bateson and Mead’s contribution was treating a photographic corpus as analysable rather than as evidence that the fieldwork happened. That is the founding move of the method.
The work is also criticised on exactly the grounds this deck develops: the subjects were observed rather than collaborating, and the analytic categories were entirely the researchers’.

Founding Texts
Collier and the method takes shape
John Collier Jr.'s Visual Anthropology: Photography as a Research Method (1967) made the case that the camera could be a disciplined research tool — and introduced photo-elicitation, using photographs to prompt interviews.
Collier moved the field from 'photographs of people' towards 'photographs as a way of talking with people' — the seed of today's participatory methods.
Collier's claimWhat it licensed
The camera records more than the eye retainsSystematic photographic survey of a site
Photographs are data, not illustrationImages admitted to the analysis, not just the report
An image in an interview opens memoryPhoto-elicitation as a named technique
The researcher must read the frameAnalysis of what the photograph itself is doing
Collier wrote from within a documentary tradition that took the camera's objectivity largely for granted. The critique that followed — Sontag, Berger, and the reflexive turn in anthropology — did not overturn the method so much as strip it of that innocence.
Read Collier for the craft and the discipline of looking. Read the critics for what the craft cannot see about itself.

The Critique
Sontag and Berger name the power
Susan Sontag
On Photography (1977): to photograph is to appropriate — an act with the logic of acquisition and even aggression. The camera does something to its subject.
John Berger
Ways of Seeing (1972): seeing is never innocent. How we look is shaped by power, gender and convention — the image carries an ideology.
Keep both voices in your head whenever you raise a camera in the field. Making an image is never a neutral act.
CriticClaim
Sontag, On Photography (1977)To photograph is to appropriate; the act has the logic of acquisition
Berger, Ways of Seeing (1972)Every image embeds a way of looking, shaped by power
BothThe camera is never a neutral instrument
Neither argued against photography; both argued against treating it as transparent. The photograph is a made object with an author and an interest, and reading it as a window is the error.
Berger’s point is the more useful one operationally: it directs you to ask whose way of seeing a given image encodes, which is a question with an answer.

The Inheritance
Why this history matters to you
When an NGO photographs 'beneficiaries' for a donor report, it can unknowingly repeat the colonial gaze — people posed, unnamed, used to tell someone else's story.
Knowing this history is not academic. It is how you avoid reproducing extraction with a modern camera and a development budget.
NGO photographRepeats the colonial grammar when
The subject is unnamedA type rather than a person
Posed for the cameraArranged to signify need
Captioned by the organisationTheir meaning, not hers
Used to raise money elsewhereThe audience is not the subject
Never returned to herThe exchange is one-way
Nobody intends this and the grammar is inherited. The conventions of the donor photograph were formed in the same century as the colonial archive, and they encode the same relation.
Every row is fixable at no cost: name the person with consent, let her say what the caption says, and give her the photograph. Section 10 makes this concrete.

Family 1
Researcher-produced images
The researcher holds the camera, choosing what to frame. Useful for systematic documentation — spatial layouts, work processes, material culture, change over time.
Strength: control and consistency. Weakness: the frame is yours — it encodes your assumptions about what matters. Whose eye is choosing, and what does that eye miss?
Researcher-produced images suitBecause
Spatial layoutsSystematic, comparable framing
Work processesSequence captured deliberately
Material cultureObjects recorded consistently
Change over timeRepeat photography from a fixed point
Repeat photography is the under-used technique here. The same frame at intervals — a season, a year, a decade — shows change that no single image and no recollection can establish.
The cost is that everything in the archive reflects your judgement about what mattered. Pair it with participant-produced work if you want to know what you failed to notice.

Cross-Cutting
Photo-elicitation: a method that uses any image
Photo-elicitation is not a fourth family but a technique — it takes images from any source and puts them at the centre of an interview to prompt deeper, richer talk. We unpack it next, in Section 4.
Likewise video cuts across all three families — researcher-shot, participant-shot, or found footage. Section 6 gives it its own treatment.
TechniqueImage sourceWhat it is doing
Photo-elicitationAny of the three familiesUsing an image to prompt talk
Auto-drivingPhotographs of the participant themselvesParticipant narrates their own depicted life
Archival elicitationOld photographs, albums, official recordsPrompting memory and historical account
Video elicitationFootage, sometimes of the participantTalk about action and sequence
Because elicitation is a technique rather than a source, its ethics travel with the image. An archival photograph shown in an interview carries whatever the archive did to the people in it, and the person you show it to may recognise a relative.
Decide before the interview what you will do if the response is distress rather than talk. That is a foreseeable outcome of showing people images of their own past, not an accident.

CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 · ImpactMojo 101 Series29www.impactmojo.in

Definition
Interviewing with images on the table
Photo-elicitation
An interview technique in which photographs are introduced into the conversation to evoke responses — the participant and researcher talk about and through the images together.
Introduced by John Collier Jr. in the 1950s–60s, it is now one of the most widely used visual methods — simple, powerful, and gentle on the participant.
In photo-elicitation, the image isNot
A promptThe data itself, necessarily
Something to talk aroundSomething to be interpreted by you alone
A shared object on the tableA test with a right answer
The participant’s to interpretYours to explain
The talk is the data, and the photograph is the instrument that produces it. Studies that analyse the images and discard the transcript have used the technique backwards.
Which is why a technically poor photograph works as well as a good one. What matters is that it is recognisable and relevant, not that it is well made.

Why It Works
Images unlock memory and emotion
A photograph in front of someone does something a bare question cannot. Collier observed that images sharpened memory and lengthened interviews while easing fatigue — people talk longer, and differently.
  • Triggers concrete, detailed memory ('that is the well we lost')
  • Opens emotion that abstract questions keep at bay
  • Gives shy participants something to look at, not just answer
  • Surfaces details the researcher did not know to ask about
With a photograph on the tableCompared with a bare question
Memory is prompted by specificsRecall is general and reconstructed
The participant leadsThe interviewer sets the agenda
Silence is comfortableSilence feels like failure
Disagreement is easyContradicting the interviewer is harder
The third row is underrated. A shared object gives both people somewhere to look, which removes the pressure of eye contact and makes long pauses tolerable — and pauses are where the material is.
Collier’s original observation was that image-prompted interviews ran longer and produced more specific detail. The mechanism is that the participant has something concrete to work from.

Two Modes
Whose photographs are on the table?
Researcher's images
You bring photographs — of the place, of historical scenes, of types of work — and ask people to react. Good for shared reference and comparison.
Participant's images
People discuss photographs they made or own — family albums or photovoice shots. The agenda is theirs; the talk goes where they take it.
Participant-led elicitation shifts authority to the interviewee — often the richer and more equitable choice.
Your imagesTheir images
Good forComparison across participantsTheir priorities and meanings
RiskYou plant the frameLess comparable across people
PreparationSelect carefully in advanceTraining and time to shoot
Who leadsYou, partlyThem
The risk column is the reason to use both where possible. Your images make responses comparable and constrain what can be said; theirs open the field and cannot be aggregated.
A common design runs their images first and yours second, so the participant’s own framing is established before your selection introduces categories.

Definition

Photovoice

Why Video
What moving images add
Video captures what a still cannot: time, movement, sound, speech, the unfolding of an event or a practice. It is uniquely suited to ritual, work processes, interaction and embodied skill.

Interpreting meaning — what the image signifies, what it connotes, what cultural codes it draws on. Deep reading of few images; answers what does it mean.
Content analysis is broad and countable; semiotic analysis is deep and interpretive. Many projects use both.

They answer different questions and are usually combined. Counting how many NGO images show women receiving something rather than doing something is content analysis with a semiotic point.
Content analysis on images needs the same discipline as any coding: defined categories, tested for agreement between coders. Without that the counts look objective and are not.

The Layers
Why the two layers matter
Roland Barthes distinguished denotation (the literal content) from connotation (the cultural meanings layered on top). A photograph never just 'shows' — it always also 'says'.
Much misleading development imagery works exactly here: it presents a loaded connotation — helplessness, exotic poverty — as if it were neutral fact.
LayerQuestionExample
DenotationWhat is literally shown?A child carrying a water pot
ConnotationWhat is culturally signified?Poverty, girlhood, burden, the rural
Whose connotation?Read by whom?It differs by viewer
The third row is where Barthes is usually truncated. Connotation is not a property of the image; it is what a particular audience brings, so the same photograph signifies differently in the village and in a donor deck.
Ask the people in the image what it connotes for them. It is the check that distinguishes analysis from the researcher’s own cultural reading presented as the image’s meaning.

The Unseen
Read what the image excludes
Analysis is not only about what is in the frame. Ask what has been cropped out, who is absent, what the photographer chose not to record. The edges of an image are full of meaning.
  • Who is never photographed in this archive?
  • What is just outside the frame — and why?
  • Which moments were not considered worth a shot?
  • Whose perspective does the framing assume?

Reflexivity
Critical awareness of how your own position — identity, assumptions, presence and power — shapes the images you make and the meanings you read from them.
There is no view from nowhere. A reflexive analyst states who they are, why they framed as they did, and how that colours the findings — rather than hiding behind the camera's apparent objectivity.

Don't let the image testify alone
An image is one source among many. Read it against your fieldnotes, interviews, the maker's intent and the wider context. A photograph confirms, complicates or contradicts — it rarely proves on its own.

The Ethics of the Image
CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 · ImpactMojo 101 Series75www.impactmojo.in

The Core
The camera is a form of power
To photograph someone is to take something and to hold power over how they appear to others. In development settings — researcher with resources, subject often without — that power is sharply unequal.
To photograph people is to violate them, by seeing them as they never see themselves.
— Susan Sontag, On Photography (1977)
The asymmetryIn a development setting
Who holds the cameraThe researcher or the funder’s staff
Who decides the captionNot the person depicted
Who sees the imageAudiences the subject will never meet
Who can refuseSomeone dependent on the service, in theory
Who can withdraw laterAlmost nobody
The fourth row is why consent in these settings needs particular care. Refusal costs nothing only if the person believes it costs nothing, and a beneficiary asked by a service provider rarely does.
Ask through someone with no power over them, make the refusal private, and notice whether anyone ever declines. If nobody does across a whole project, the consent was not real.

Two Consents
Consent to photograph ≠ consent to publish
To be photographed
Agreement that an image may be made in the moment. Necessary — but it does not license any later use.
To publish / share
Separate agreement to show the image in a report, online, in a campaign. People may say yes to one and no to the other.
Get both, in language people understand, and explain where the image could end up — including 'forever, on the internet'.
Consent to photographConsent to publish
GivenIn the momentRequires knowing the use
CoversThe making of the imageWhere, how long, in what company
Can be given byThe person, thenThe person, informed of the venue
Commonly collectedYesRarely, separately
Collecting the two as one is the standard practice and the standard error. Someone comfortable being photographed at a training may not want that image in a fundraising campaign.
Show the actual intended use where you can. "This may appear in a report and on our website" is abstract; showing last year’s report is a consent conversation people can actually engage with.

Ownership
Who owns the image?
By default, copyright usually rests with whoever pressed the shutter — not the person depicted. In participatory work this is ethically fraught: the community's images may legally belong to the project.
  • Agree ownership and licensing before shooting
  • Consider giving participants rights over their own images
  • Distinguish the photographer's copyright from the subject's rights
  • Be explicit about reuse, sale and archiving
Copyright defaultThe ethical problem
Rests with whoever pressed the shutterNot with the person depicted
In participatory workParticipants made the images
Institutions often claim itThrough the project agreement
ResolutionAgree it in writing, before shooting
Photovoice projects that hand over the camera and keep the copyright have participated in the shooting only. Ownership is where the participation claim is tested and most often fails.
The workable arrangement is participants holding the rights with a licence to the organisation for named uses. It takes one paragraph and is almost never done.

The Gaze
Whose way of seeing is encoded?
Every image embeds a gaze — a position of looking shaped by power, gender, class and history. The colonial gaze, the male gaze, the donor gaze: each frames the subject for a particular viewer.
Ask of any field image: who is this made for? If the answer is 'a distant donor' rather than 'the people in it', examine the gaze you are reproducing.
GazeEncodes
ColonialThe subject as specimen or type
MaleThe subject as object of looking
DonorThe subject as evidence of need
ClinicalThe subject as a case
InsiderThe subject as a person known
The donor gaze is the one practitioners produce without naming it. It is not malicious; it is what results when the audience is a funder and the image must demonstrate that help is required.
Ask who the imagined viewer is for each image. The answer largely determines the framing, the caption and whether the subject appears as an agent or as a condition.

Children
Children and vulnerable subjects
  • Children cannot give full legal consent — seek guardian consent and the child's assent
  • Never publish images that could endanger or shame a child
  • Extra care for survivors of violence, trafficking, illness, stigma
  • When in doubt, do not photograph — or anonymise thoroughly
Many organisations bar identifiable images of children in vulnerable situations entirely. A safeguarding policy should govern this, not a deadline or a donor's appetite for a 'strong image'.
Vulnerable subjectRequirement
ChildrenGuardian consent and the child’s assent
Survivors of violenceNever identifiable; consider not photographing

Do No Harm
Anticipate downstream harm
An image that seems harmless to you may endanger its subject: a photo identifying an undocumented migrant, a protester, a survivor, a person whose community would punish what the picture reveals.
Think one step ahead: who might see this, and what could they do with it? The 'right to be seen' must always be balanced against the right to safety.
Downstream harmTo
An image identifying a migrant’s statusTheir security
A protester, publishedTheir safety
A survivor, recognisableTheir standing at home
A person with HIV, namedEverything
A worker photographed complainingTheir job
The harm arrives later, elsewhere, and you will not hear about it. That asymmetry is why anticipation has to be deliberate rather than left to whether anyone objected at the time.
Ask a local colleague to review the set before publication with one question: could this image cause anyone in it a problem? They will see risks that are invisible to an outsider.

Codes
Ethics is ongoing, not a signature
Formal ethics review and consent forms are a floor, not a ceiling. Visual ethics is a continuing relationship — revisited as the project, the images and their uses evolve.
  • Let people see how their image will be used — and object
  • Honour withdrawal of consent, even later
  • Follow your organisation's safeguarding and data-protection rules
  • Treat dignity, not the image, as the deliverable
Ethics as a signatureEthics as a relationship
A form at the startRevisited as the project changes
One decisionRenewed when the use changes
Satisfies the committeeSatisfies the person
Ends at approvalContinues past publication
Formal approval is a floor. An ethics committee cannot anticipate what a specific image will mean in a specific village three years after the project closed.
The practical version is a check before each new use: is this the use they agreed to, and would they still agree? It takes minutes and prevents most of the harm in this section.

Found Images
Ethics of images you did not make
Archival and social-media images carry their own consent and copyright burdens. A photograph posted publicly was not posted for your study — and the people in it never agreed to be your data.
Public availability is not consent. Treat found images with the same care as ones you make: check rights, weigh harm, and anonymise where reuse could expose someone.
Found imageQuestion
Public social-media postPosted for their circle, not your report
Family albumWhose consent — the owner, or those depicted?
Colonial archiveThe subjects never consented to anything
NGO archiveConsent for the original purpose only
Publicly posted is not published for you. Legal availability and ethical availability are different questions, and terms of service settle neither.

The Problem
Poverty porn
Poverty porn
Imagery that exploits the suffering or destitution of subjects to provoke pity and donations — stripping people of agency, context and dignity for emotional effect.
The flies-on-the-face, hand-outstretched, nameless-child photograph is the archetype. It raises money in the short term and corrodes dignity and truth in the long term.
MechanismHow it works on the viewer
NamelessnessRemoves the person, leaves a category
DecontextualisationSuffering appears causeless, so unfixable except by charity
Passive posturePositions the subject as recipient, never actor
Child subjectsBypasses argument entirely, goes to pity
White rescuer in frameLocates the agency outside the community
It is worth being precise about why this is a research problem and not only a moral one: an image built to provoke pity is an image built to suppress analysis. It cannot carry a finding, because it has removed the context a finding would need.
The commercial defence — that it raises more money — is contested even on its own terms, and the sector codes that now govern it were written by fundraisers, not critics.

Stereotype
How harmful images work
  • Reduce a whole community to a single image of lack
  • Erase agency — people as passive victims, never actors
  • Strip context — no history, no cause, no structure
  • Flatten difference — 'the poor', 'the village', undifferentiated
Stereotyped images do not just misrepresent; they shape policy and public attitudes towards the people depicted.

Poverty porn works, which is why it persists. Guilt converts; it also produces donors who give once and a subject whose dignity was spent to achieve it.
The single-story problem is structural rather than per-image. Any one photograph of hardship is fine; a body of work containing only hardship is the misrepresentation.

Agency
Show agency and voice
Dignified representation centres what people do and say, not only what they lack. Name them (where safe), quote them, show them acting, and let their account frame the image.
Nothing about us without us.
— disability-rights principle, widely adopted in participatory practice
Instead ofShow
A person as a conditionA person doing something, with a name where safe

"Nothing about us without us" entered wide use through the disability-rights movement and is now standard in participatory practice. Applied to images it has a concrete test: did the person see the photograph, and did they have a real option to say no to it?
Real means the refusal costs them nothing — not their place in the programme, not the relationship, not the payment. If it costs them something, you have permission, not consent.

Ethical Codes

Sector codes — such as Dóchas / the international Code of Conduct on Images and Messages — set out principles for respectful imagery: consent, accuracy, dignity, and showing people as equals.

  • Obtain informed consent for image and use
  • Give people a say in how they are shown

The Test
The dignity test
A simple check before publishing any field image: would the person in it be content to see this picture, this caption, in this place — and would you be comfortable if it were you, or someone you love?
If the honest answer is no, the image fails — however powerful, however much it might raise. Dignity is the deliverable.
The dignity testAsk
Would she be content to see this image?In this place, with this caption
Would you be content to explain it to her?Face to face
Would you publish it of your own family?A useful calibration
Does the caption say what she would say?Or what the audience wants
The second question is the sharper one. Most images that fail this test would not survive the author having to explain the caption to the person in it.
It takes ten seconds and catches almost everything Section 10 describes. Apply it to every field image before it leaves the organisation.

Captions
The caption does heavy lifting
A caption anchors meaning. The same photograph, captioned two ways, tells two different truths. Honest captions give context, attribute voice, and resist putting words in the subject's mouth.
  • State who, where, when — the verifiable facts
  • Use the subject's own words where you can
  • Avoid loaded adjectives that supply your verdict
  • Never caption a generic image with a specific false story
CaptionEffect on the same photograph
"A village without water"Deficit; anonymous
"Sunita walks 3 km each morning"A person, a specific fact
"The handpump has been broken since March"A system failure, with an author
"We have asked four times"Her words; agency and a demand
Four captions, four different truths, one image. The caption is not description added to a photograph — it is what determines which of several possible readings the viewer arrives at.
The fourth is usually the strongest and the least used. A direct quote makes the subject the author of the claim rather than its illustration.

Give It Back
Return the work to the community
Visual research too often extracts: images flow out to reports and donors, and nothing flows back. Ethical practice closes the loop.
Hold the exhibition in the community first. Give people prints of themselves. Let participants present their own images. The audience that matters most is the one in the photographs.
