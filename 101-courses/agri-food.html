Starting point
Three official sources describe Indian farming, and each counts something different
Before quoting any number about Indian farmers, a practitioner has to know which source it came from, because the three main sources do not count the same unit. The Agriculture Census counts operational holdings, which are parcels of land run as one unit, whoever owns them. The Situation Assessment Survey of the National Statistical Office counts agricultural households, defined by the value of what they produce. The Periodic Labour Force Survey counts workers by the industry of their main and subsidiary work.
The three give different totals for the same country in the same years, and none of them is wrong. A holding run jointly by two brothers is one holding and two households. A landless family that earns its living harvesting other people's fields is outside the Situation Assessment Survey altogether, because households living entirely on farm labour are excluded by definition.

A long decline
The average holding has more than halved since 1970-71
Average size of an operational holding, all India (hectares)
Agriculture Census 2015-16, All India Report, Department of Agriculture
The average operational holding was 2.28 hectares in 1970-71 and 1.08 hectares in 2015-16. The fall has been steady across ten censuses. What has not changed is the average within each class. A marginal holding averaged 0.40 hectares in 1970-71 and 0.38 hectares in 2015-16. A large holding averaged 18.10 and 17.07 hectares.
So the national average fell because the mix shifted, with more and more holdings dropping into the marginal class as land was divided among heirs. The size of a marginal farm stayed close to 0.4 hectares for half a century.
Read the trend as a shift in the number of small holdings. Holdings in each class kept roughly the same size.

Owned and operated
Owning land and operating a holding are different things
Ownership holding
Land a household holds with permanent heritable possession, with or without the right to transfer it. Land held in owner-like possession under a long lease, or a tribal household's customary land, also counts as owned in the NSS 77th round.
Operational holding
All land used wholly or partly for agricultural production and managed by one household, without regard to title, size or location. It includes land owned, leased in or otherwise possessed.
A tenant who leases in two plots and owns none has an operational holding and no ownership holding. Schemes that pay owners and schemes that pay operators therefore reach different people.

Measuring income
The survey builds income from five sources and offers two answers
India has no regular survey of household income. The Situation Assessment Survey is the only official source of household-level income for a large part of the population, as Bakshi points out. It adds up wages and salaries, rent from land leased out, and net receipts from crops, animals and non-farm business, where net receipt is receipts less expenses for that activity.
The 77th round reports income twice. The first answer deducts only paid-out expenses, the cash a household spent. The second also deducts imputed costs, which are the market value of family labour, home-grown seed and manure, and rent on the household's own land. The second answer is closer to the cost concepts the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices uses for MSP.

The average is taken over all agricultural households, including the half with no debt, so the average loan among indebted households is roughly twice as large. The survey asks about the amount outstanding on the day of the first visit, which falls in the first half of 2019.
Reading a debt figure
Two loans of the same size, one from a bank at a low rate and one from a moneylender at a high rate, appear as the same rupees in an outstanding-debt total. The burden lies in the interest rate, the repayment schedule and what the loan was for. Ask for the share of debt from institutional sources, the purpose of borrowing, and whether the loan funds farming or consumption and health spending.
Debt and distress
A high average debt is not evidence of distress on its own. A household that borrows to buy a pump and one that borrows to pay a hospital bill carry the same debt on paper. Look at debt relative to income and assets, and at who the lender is.

The Dalwai Committee set the target national farmer income for 2022-23 at ₹1,92,694 at 2015-16 prices, or ₹2,71,378 at current prices, counting farm and non-farm income together (Government of India 2018, volume 14, as quoted by Bakshi).
Between 2012-13 and 2018-19 the real income of an agricultural household grew by about 15 per cent. Doubling in the following four years would have required growth far faster than anything the survey records. Bakshi concludes that the numbers point to the near-impossibility of meeting the goal.
The 2019 round is the latest Situation Assessment Survey used in this course, and it predates the 2022 deadline. A claim that incomes doubled, or did not, needs a later household survey to support it.
When a target is announced without a measurement plan, ask which survey will measure it and when.

The Kisan Credit Card gives a farmer a revolving crop loan sized to the crops grown and the area cultivated. Practice
Questions to ask of any claim about farm incomes
  • Name the survey. A Situation Assessment Survey figure is per agricultural household. A GVA figure divided by workers is per worker and includes plantation companies.
  • Name the cost concept. Paid-out costs give ₹10,218 a month in 2018-19. Paid-out and imputed costs give ₹8,337.
  • Say whether a figure is nominal or real. The 2012-13 and 2018-19 totals look like a 59 per cent rise in rupees and a 15.6 per cent rise after inflation.
  • Prefer the median to the mean where you can, because a few large commercial farms pull the mean up. Ask for incomes by size class of land.
  • Convert household figures to per person. A household of six on ₹10,218 a month has about ₹1,700 per person.

What MSP is
A price the government announces for 22 crops, and buys at for a few
Every year the Union government announces Minimum Support Prices for 22 mandated crops on the recommendation of the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices (CACP), after hearing state governments and central ministries. The CACP also recommends the Fair and Remunerative Price for sugarcane.
An MSP is a price at which public agencies stand ready to buy. It is not a legal floor on private trade. A farmer who sells to a private trader below MSP breaks no law, and public agencies buy only where purchase centres operate. Rice and wheat are bought in large volumes for the central pool. Pulses, oilseeds and copra are bought under the Price Support Scheme of PM-AASHA when market prices fall below MSP, and only from pre-registered farmers with valid land records.

Cost concepts
Which cost the 50 per cent is added to is the heart of the MSP dispute
A1 and A2
Paid-out costs: all out-of-pocket expenses plus the imputed value of owned animal and machine labour. A1 is the measure for an owner-cultivator and A2 for a tenant-cultivator, who also pays rent for leased-in land.
Family labour (FL)
The imputed value of the work done by the farmer's own family, valued at market wages.
C2 and C3
Comprehensive cost. C2 includes imputed costs on owned land and family labour. C3 also includes a value for the farmer's management input.
Bakshi describes the CACP as using nine cost concepts, all of which include some kind of imputed cost. The Swaminathan Commission recommended that MSP be set 50 per cent above the total cost of production, defined as all paid-out and imputed costs, including family labour and the rental value of owned land. Farmers' movements have insisted on that recommendation, and Bakshi notes it was central to the debate around the 2020 farm laws.
The margin in each MSP release is calculated on the cost column printed beside it, and the release defines that cost in a footnote. If the cost leaves out the rental value of the farmer's own land, a C2 calculation of the same MSP shows a smaller margin. When someone says MSP is or is not 1.5 times cost, ask which cost.
The SAS 77th round collected imputed costs for the first time, which lets researchers compute farm income on cost definitions close to the CACP's.

Agricultural marketing in India is organised under state APMC laws, and each state decides which produce is regulated in its markets, what fee is charged and who may be licensed to trade.

The argument for
What the government said the laws would do
More buyers for the farmer
Allowing trade outside APMC yards would let farmers sell to any buyer, including processors and exporters, in any place. Competition between the mandi and private buyers was expected to raise the price farmers received.
Lower transaction costs
Trade outside the yards would pay no state market fee. Electronic trading would let buyers from other districts and states bid.
Price certainty before sowing
A contract signed before sowing, with an agreed price, would shift price risk to the buyer and give the farmer access to the buyer's inputs and technical advice.
Private storage investment
Removing routine stock limits on cereals, pulses, onions and edible oils was meant to encourage private investment in warehouses and cold chains, on the argument that stock limits discouraged it.
Some of these arguments have empirical support elsewhere. Goyal's study of soybean markets in Madhya Pradesh, discussed in section 9, found that a private buyer's direct purchase channel raised mandi prices.

The argument against
What farmers' organisations and critics said
The fear for MSP and procurement
Farmers in the states where procurement is large feared that a parallel trade channel without fees would hollow out the mandis, and with them the procurement system through which they sell rice and wheat at MSP. The protests were most widespread in Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh.
Bargaining power
Critics argued that a marginal farmer with under one hectare cannot negotiate a contract with a large buyer on equal terms, and doubted that the Act's own dispute process would protect them.
Federalism and revenue
State governments lost the power to levy fees on trade outside their market yards, in a subject the Constitution gives them.
Process
The government promulgated the three measures as ordinances in the first week of June 2020, before Parliament met, and the Rajya Sabha passed the bills by voice vote.
Among the protesters' demands was to make MSP and state procurement of crops a legal right, which goes well beyond anything the three Acts had changed.

Leakage
Diversion from the PDS was high and fell sharply where states reformed it

Leakage is estimated by comparing the grain the FCI released to states with the grain households report buying in a survey. Drèze and Khera, writing in the Economic and Political Weekly in February 2015, estimated that leakage fell from 54 to 42 per cent between 2004-05 and 2011-12 using NSS data, and from about 50 to about 30 per cent using the India Human Development Survey.

How fertiliser is subsidised
Urea is cheap at the counter, and farmers use more of it
Urea, the main source of nitrogen, is sold to farmers at a retail price set by the government, and the government meets the difference between that price and the cost of supply. Phosphatic and potassic fertilisers are supported through nutrient-based pricing, a fixed subsidy per unit of nutrient. The result is that nitrogen is much cheaper relative to phosphorus and potassium than its cost would make it.
The Economic Survey 2025-26 says plainly that the urea subsidy has increased application and that this increased use has been reported to affect soil productivity negatively. It lists neem-coating of urea, Aadhaar-linked verification at the point of sale and the Integrated Fertiliser Management System as steps that improved control over physical flows without changing the price signal farmers face.
The Survey's diagnosis, in its words
“As long as one nutrient is vastly cheaper than others, its overuse is structurally embedded, regardless of monitoring or enforcement.”

A proposal
Raise the urea price a little and pay farmers the difference per acre
The Economic Survey 2025-26 proposes separating income support from fertiliser purchase. The retail price of urea would rise modestly, and an equivalent amount would be transferred directly to cultivators per acre, indexed to agro-climatic zone and cropping pattern. Farmers keep the same purchasing power, but nitrogen's price moves closer to its agronomic cost.
The Survey expects efficient users to gain, because they receive the full transfer and spend less at the counter. Over-users face a reason to shift to balanced fertilisation. A per-acre transfer is paid to someone identified with the land. Where a portion of the land is cultivated by renters, the owner may receive the transfer while the tenant pays the higher urea price. The Survey suggests that pilot designs include tenancy-heavy districts to refine the mechanism.

Power for pumps
Free electricity is rationed, and rationing misallocates water
Where farm power is free or nearly free, the only limit on pumping is how many hours of supply the utility provides. Nicholas Ryan and Anant Sudarshan studied this system in Rajasthan (Rationing the Commons, Journal of Political Economy 2022, 130(1): 210-257).
They find that farmers face near-zero prices for pumping, but rationing binds, so on average farmers use roughly the socially optimal amount of water. The rationing regime is still very inefficient because it misallocates water across farmers. A ration of hours, for example, gives a farmer with a high-value crop the same water as a farmer with a low-value one. They estimate that a Pigouvian reform, which prices pumping at its cost to the commons, would raise agricultural surplus by 12 per cent of household income.

Why it happens
The incentives in the north-west all point towards pumping
Free or cheap power
Where farm power is free or flat-rated, the cost of pumping one more hour is close to zero for the farmer. Ryan and Sudarshan show in Rajasthan that rationing hours keeps average use in check but misallocates water between farmers.
Assured prices for a thirsty crop
Rice is the most irrigated crop, at about two-thirds coverage, and in the states with large procurement it is bought at MSP. A farmer comparing paddy with a less water-intensive crop that has no assured buyer has a strong reason to choose paddy.
A common pool
Groundwater under a village is shared. Water one farmer does not pump may be pumped by a neighbour, so restraint by one farmer brings little benefit to that farmer. This is the logic of the commons, and it is why advice to individual farmers to save water needs a collective rule behind it.
Rules that are hard to enforce
The Economic Survey notes that Uttar Pradesh's Ground Water Rules of 2020 strengthened regulation of extraction, and that by 2025 recharge had risen marginally while extraction intensity also increased.

Drip and the rebound
Water-saving technology can increase total water use
Drip irrigation delivers water to the roots and wastes less to evaporation and runoff. Per hectare, it uses less water for the same crop. The farmer, though, may use the water saved to irrigate more land or to switch to a thirstier, more profitable crop. Total extraction from the aquifer can then stay the same or rise.
This is the rebound problem. It is why water-saving subsidies work best alongside a limit on how much is extracted, such as a cap on hours, a metered connection, or a village agreement on the area under each crop.
A programme that reports hectares under drip is reporting an input. Ask what happened to the water table and to the area irrigated.

Practice
Using groundwater data in programme design
  • Look up the category of the block you work in before promoting any crop, pump or irrigation scheme. A semi-critical block today may be over-exploited in five years.
  • Ask whether a proposed livelihood depends on new borewells. In an over-exploited block it will compete with existing users for a falling resource.
  • Pair any water-saving subsidy with a rule on total use, or report what happened to the area irrigated.
  • In rain-fed blocks, treat surface storage, tanks and soil moisture conservation as the main irrigation strategy.
  • Remember that the stage of extraction is a block average. Wells near a canal and wells at the tail end of a block can face very different conditions.
A note on solar pumps
PM-KUSUM has installed 11.49 lakh off-grid solar pumps. In a safe block a solar pump can replace diesel and cut costs. In an over-exploited block it removes the last cost on pumping. Check the block category before promoting one.

Evidence on insurance demand
Farmers say rainfall is their biggest risk and still buy little insurance
Shawn Cole, Xavier Giné, Jeremy Tobacman, Petia Topalova, Robert Townsend and James Vickery ran randomised experiments on rainfall index insurance in rural Andhra Pradesh and Gujarat (American Economic Journal: Applied Economics, 2013, 5(1): 104-35).
Only 5 to 10 per cent of households bought the insurance, even though they overwhelmingly named rainfall variability as their most important risk. Demand was sensitive to price, with an estimated price elasticity between -0.66 and -0.88. Price was not the whole story. The authors point to lack of trust, liquidity constraints and limited salience, and argue that even contracts as generous as those in the United States would see limited take-up without addressing these.
What PMFBY's design does about these frictions
A heavily subsidised premium answers price. Automatic enrolment of loanee farmers answers liquidity and salience for borrowers, because the premium is deducted from the loan. Trust depends on whether claims are paid on time and are seen to match losses.
What remains
Area-based settlement means a farmer whose plot failed in a unit that did well may not be paid, and that experience shapes trust in the next season. The Digiclaim module, used since kharif 2022, pays claims through the Public Financial Management System and has paid over ₹55,000 crore.

A phone hotline changed what farmers did, and the yield gains were unclear

  • Treated farmers relied less on other farmers and on input sellers for advice.
  • They adopted more effective pesticides and cut spending on less effective and hazardous ones.
  • They sowed more cumin, a lucrative but risky crop.

Advice that farmers can ask for, on their own crop and their own pest, changes behaviour. Changing input choices is a real outcome, especially when it replaces hazardous pesticides. Sharecropping
The tenant gives the owner a share of the harvest. Owner and tenant share the risk, and sometimes the input costs.
Informal tenancy
An oral agreement with no written lease, which leaves the tenant with no document to show a scheme, an insurer or a bank.
Tenancy is governed by state law under Entry 18 of the State List, which covers the relation of landlord and tenant. The Agriculture Census counts land leased in as part of an operational holding, but the schemes in this course pay according to recorded ownership, so a tenant who appears in the census as an operator is often invisible to the schemes.
The Economic Survey 2025-26 raises this problem itself in its proposal for per-acre fertiliser transfers, noting that a portion of the land is cultivated by renters and suggesting that pilots include tenancy-heavy districts. PMFBY has enrolled more than 1.44 crore tenant and sharecropper farmers since 2018, which shows that including them is possible when a scheme allows other proof of cultivation.
When a programme says it covers farmers, ask whether it means owners or cultivators.

Projected yield losses
Without adaptation, rain-fed rice could lose a fifth of its yield by 2050
Projected change in yield without adaptation (%)
ICAR, Lok Sabha Unstarred Q. 2365, 10 Dec 2024
ICAR's network project National Innovations in Climate Resilient Agriculture (NICRA) ran computer simulation models for major crops. In a written reply to the Lok Sabha on 10 December 2024, the Minister of State for Agriculture said that in the absence of adaptation, climate change is likely to reduce rain-fed rice yields by 20 per cent in 2050 and 47 per cent in 2080, irrigated rice by 3.5 and 5 per cent, wheat by 19.3 and 40 per cent, and kharif maize by 10 to 19 per cent in 2050 and more than 20 per cent in 2080.
These are model projections under stated scenarios. Their value lies in the comparison. Irrigation buffers rice against warming, and wheat, a winter crop sensitive to heat at grain filling, is among the most exposed.
The farmers with the least irrigation face the largest projected losses.

Weather shocks now
Heat, unseasonal rain and El Niño already cut yields and quality
Grain quality
The same Lok Sabha reply notes preliminary findings that elevated carbon dioxide and temperature reduced iron, zinc and protein in certain maize genotypes, that elevated carbon dioxide altered rice grain quality, and that heat stress in wheat caused shrivelled grains with less starch and protein, while excess rain at grain filling caused lodging and discoloured grain.
Yields below the national average
A CACP report cited in the Economic Survey 2025-26 found that several major rice-producing states, including West Bengal, Uttar Pradesh, Telangana, Odisha, Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu, had per-hectare yields below the national average, and named unseasonal rain, heat stress and dry spells at critical crop stages as the main causes.
El Niño and pulses
A NITI Aayog report cited in the Economic Survey found that in 15 of the 27 El Niño years between 1951 and 2024, pulse acreage fell by 2 to 9 per cent, production by 6 to 30 per cent and yields by 5 to 25 per cent year on year. During La Niña years acreage, production and productivity rose. Pulses are mostly rain-fed, with about 26 per cent of their area irrigated.
Climate risk shows up first in rain-fed crops and in the quality of food, as well as in the quantity of rice and wheat.

Evidence on adaptation
A flood-tolerant rice variety paid off through the investments it encouraged
Kyle Emerick, Alain de Janvry, Elisabeth Sadoulet and Manzoor Dar ran a randomised trial in 128 villages in Odisha with Swarna-Sub1, a version of the popular Swarna rice variety carrying a gene that lets it survive submergence. In normal years it yields the same as Swarna. In a flood it loses much less (American Economic Review, 2016, 106(6): 1537-61).
Farmers given the new seed did more than plant it. They adopted a more labour-intensive planting method, cultivated more area, used more fertiliser and took more credit. A large share of the expected gain came from these crowded-in investments, because lowering the risk of total loss made it worth investing in the crop.

Prices and markets
What the evidence says about selling crops
The Indian evidence on agricultural markets in this course is about information and buyer competition. Goyal's soybean study shows that when farmers could see prices and sell to a second buyer, mandi prices rose. Cole and Fernando show that farmers use advice when they can ask for it, and that changing what farmers do does not automatically raise yields within a season.
On MSP, the descriptive evidence is clear and the causal evidence is scarce. The Situation Assessment Surveys show that a minority of farmers sell to procurement agencies, and the share of wheat output sold at MSP fell between 2013 and 2019. What MSP does to prices in districts where agencies buy, and to crop choice and groundwater, needs more rigorous study.

Safety nets
What the evidence says about food and income transfers
The PDS evidence shows that delivery can improve quickly when states reform it. Drèze and Khera found leakage fell sharply in Chhattisgarh, Odisha and Bihar and barely changed in some other large states. Technology can cut theft, and the Jharkhand experiment shows it can also cut people off, depending on how the transition is managed.
PM-KISAN's early evidence suggests that a small, regular, unconditional transfer eases the cash constraints of farmers who lack credit. It also shows the cost of tying transfers to land records, which leaves out tenants and labourers.
Principles that carry across schemes
Measure exclusion as carefully as leakage. Keep a fallback for people a new verification step fails. Tie eligibility to the activity you want to support, cultivation or need, and check how well the records match it. Pay on a predictable schedule, before the season's costs fall due.

Risk and technology
What the evidence says about risk, inputs and water
Three findings fit together. Farmers value protection against bad weather but buy little insurance when they must pay for it, do not trust it, or lack cash when the premium is due (Cole et al. 2013). When a technology removes the worst outcome, farmers invest more in everything else (Emerick et al. 2016). And when a key input such as water is free, rationing it by quantity limits the total but sends it to the wrong farmers (Ryan and Sudarshan 2022).
The Economic Survey 2025-26 applies the same logic to fertiliser. A price far below cost embeds overuse, and an income transfer with prices closer to cost could protect farmers and soils at once. The Survey proposes pilots, so the idea has not been tested at scale.

Practice
Ten questions to ask of any agricultural scheme
  • Who is on the list: owners, cultivators, households or individuals?
  • How is the list made, and how does someone left off get on?
  • What does the scheme do to the relative prices of crops, water and fertiliser?
  • Which crops and which regions receive most of the money?
  • Does it reach rain-fed farmers, or mostly irrigated ones?
  • What share of beneficiaries are women, and in whose name is the payment?
  • Is the headline figure a count of people, applications, hectares or rupees?
  • Is spending reported as budget, revised estimate or actual?
  • Has any evaluation compared beneficiaries with a credible counterfactual?
  • What happens to the groundwater, the soil and the household's diet if the scheme works as intended?
