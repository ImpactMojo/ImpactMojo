Definition
Social and emotional learning, defined
Children do not leave their feelings at the classroom door. Social and emotional learning (SEL) is the process through which young people and adults acquire and apply the knowledge, skills and attitudes to understand and manage emotions, set goals, show empathy, build relationships and make responsible decisions.
Social and emotional learning (SEL)
The process of developing the self-awareness, self-control and interpersonal skills that are vital for school, work and life success — definition adapted from CASEL.
SEL is not a separate subject bolted on to a busy day. It is the human foundation that all other learning is built upon.
What SEL teachesAt 8 years old, that looks like
Understand and manage emotionsCan say “I am angry” before throwing the pencil box
Set and work toward goalsKeeps at the tables practice for a week without being chased
Feel and show empathyNotices a classmate has not eaten and says so to an adult
Build and keep relationshipsRepairs a friendship after a fight instead of dropping it
Make responsible decisionsDoes not join in when three others exclude a fourth
Why the definition is long: every clause in it is doing work. “Acquire and apply” rules out a lesson children can recite but never use. “Young people and adults” puts teachers inside the definition rather than outside it. “Across contexts” is the hardest part: a child who is calm in the SEL period and dysregulated in the maths period has not yet learned the skill.
A test you can apply to any claim about SEL: can it be observed in a child’s behaviour? “Has good values” cannot. “Waits for a turn to speak in a group of six” can. If the outcome cannot be seen, it cannot be taught deliberately or checked honestly.

The Name
What does CASEL stand for?
The most widely used SEL framework comes from CASEL — the Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning, a US-based organisation founded in 1994 that has shaped how schools worldwide define and teach these skills.
Throughout this course, when we say 'the five competencies' we mean CASEL's framework. We will meet each one in turn.
FrameworkOriginHow it differs
CASEL 5Chicago, 1994Five competencies plus four widening settings; the reference point for most school programmes
WHO life skillsGeneva, 1993Ten skills framed for health and risk behaviour; the basis of most adolescent-health curricula in South Asia
UNESCO / UNICEFGlobalFramed as transferable skills for employability and citizenship as well as wellbeing
NEP 2020India, 2020Uses “socio-emotional” alongside values and life skills; sets direction rather than a taxonomy
Why the frameworks overlap so much: they are describing the same set of human capacities from different starting points — schooling, public health, employment. If a programme in your district uses the WHO life-skills language rather than CASEL’s, it is not a different subject. Map the terms and move on; arguing taxonomy is a way of avoiding practice.
Where it matters: only in measurement. A tool built for the CASEL five will not report cleanly against a life-skills curriculum, and vice versa. Choose the framework first, then the assessment — not the other way round.

Why It Matters
Emotions drive attention, memory and learning
Neuroscience is clear: emotion and cognition are deeply linked. A child who is anxious, dysregulated or unsafe cannot attend, encode or recall well. Regulating emotion is a precondition for academic learning, not a distraction from it.

The practical version of the neuroscience: a dysregulated child is not refusing to learn, and usually cannot choose otherwise in the moment. Consequences applied at that moment land on a system that is not available to receive them, which is why they so often fail to change anything and have to be escalated.
Do not overclaim the brain science. “Emotion and attention interact” is well established. Specific claims about which brain region “switches off” are usually a simplification borrowed from popular writing, and a sceptical head teacher will spot it. The classroom argument stands without them.

What SEL Is Not
Clearing up four misconceptions
  • Not therapy or counselling — it is everyday teaching, for all
  • Not 'soft' or optional — it predicts hard outcomes
  • Not just being nice — it is a set of teachable skills
  • Not a Western luxury — every culture nurtures these capacities
SEL names and structures something good teachers have always done. It makes the implicit explicit and teachable.
What you will hearWhat to say back
“We have no time — the syllabus is huge.”The evidence is that SEL does not compete with academics; the time is largely recovered in fewer disruptions
“That is the counsellor’s job.”SEL is universal teaching for all children; counselling is targeted support for some. Different jobs
“Our children need discipline, not feelings.”Self-management is discipline — built from the inside so it holds when no one is watching
“This is a Western import.”Nai Talim and Shantiniketan made the same argument decades before CASEL existed
The objection worth taking seriously: that SEL becomes a way of asking children to regulate themselves through conditions adults should be fixing. That risk is real, and this course returns to it in Sections 10 and 11. The answer is not to drop SEL but to refuse the version that stops at the child.
Who to have the conversation with first: the head teacher, and the person who sets the discipline policy — often not the same person. A classroom teaching empathy inside a school running public punishment will lose.

Skills, Not Traits
These are skills, and skills can be taught
The trait myth
'Some children are just well-behaved; others aren't.' This treats emotional skill as fixed and lets schools off the hook.
The skills view
Self-control, empathy and cooperation are practised capacities — like reading or arithmetic, they grow with instruction and feedback.
If it can be taught, it can be learned by every child — the equity case for SEL begins here.
Said about a childThe same thing said as a skill
“He is aggressive.”“He has few strategies for anger and reaches the last one fast.”
“She is shy.”“She has not yet practised entering a group that is already talking.”
“They are careless.”“They have not been taught to check work before handing it in.”
“She is a natural leader.”“She reads a group well and speaks without dominating — both learnable.”
Why the wording matters more than it looks: a trait describes a child and stops. A skill description contains its own next step, so it tells the teacher what to do on Monday. Rewriting your three most-used labels this way is the single cheapest change in this deck.
The equity argument, stated plainly: traits are distributed by luck and treated as fixed, so a trait view quietly writes off the children who arrive with less. A skills view obliges the school to teach. That obligation is the whole point.

A Long Lineage
An old idea with a new name
Educating 'the whole child' — head, heart and hand — runs through Gandhi's Nai Talim, Tagore's Shantiniketan, Aristotle's ethics and countless Indigenous traditions. SEL is a modern, evidence-based expression of a very old wisdom.
Educating the mind without educating the heart is no education at all.
— commonly attributed to Aristotle
TraditionThe claim it made
Nai Talim (Gandhi, 1937)Head, heart and hand educated together through productive work; character is formed by what a child does daily, not by what is preached
Shantiniketan (Tagore, 1901)Learning in and with the natural world, through art and music, because feeling is a form of knowing rather than a distraction from it
Buddhist and Sufi practiceAttention to one’s own mind as a trainable discipline, centuries before “self-regulation” had a research literature
What is genuinely new is not the idea. It is the evidence base, the fact that the skills have been broken down into teachable parts, and the finding that how a programme is run decides whether it works at all. That is worth being clear about: claiming SEL is entirely ancient wisdom is as unhelpful as claiming it is entirely new science.
On the quotation: it is widely attributed to Aristotle and does not appear in his surviving work. Use it if it helps, but say “commonly attributed”, as this slide does. A deck that is careless with an easy attribution invites doubt about its harder ones.

The Landmark Study
Durlak et al. (2011): the foundational meta-analysis
The most cited evidence for SEL is a meta-analysis by Durlak and colleagues (2011), which pooled 213 school-based SEL programmes involving more than 270,000 students from kindergarten to high school.
213

270,000+

What the study wasDetail
DesignMeta-analysis: pooled results from many separate studies rather than one new trial
Programmes included213 school-based SEL programmes
StudentsMore than 270,000, kindergarten through high school
DeliveryUniversal — whole classes, not children selected for difficulty
PublishedChild Development, 2011; Durlak, Weissberg, Dymnicki, Taylor & Schellinger
Why a meta-analysis carries more weight than any one trial: a single study can be lucky, small, or run by the people who designed the programme. Pooling 213 of them averages out that noise and lets you ask which programme features predict success — which is where the SAFE finding in Section 9 comes from.
Its main limitation, stated by the authors: the pooled studies are overwhelmingly from high-income, largely English-speaking systems. That does not make the finding wrong here. It does mean the size of the effect in an Indian government school is an open question, not a settled one.

The Headline Finding
An 11-percentile-point academic gain
In Durlak et al. (2011), students in SEL programmes showed an 11-percentile-point gain in academic achievement compared with control groups — alongside improved social skills, attitudes and behaviour, and reduced emotional distress.
Read this carefully: it is an average effect across many programmes, and it shows that attending to emotion does not crowd out academics — it supports them.
Caution: a percentile-point gain is not the same as a percentage rise in marks. Attribute the figure to Durlak et al. (2011); do not inflate it.
What it meansWhat it does not mean
A student at the 50th percentile would, on average, sit near the 61stThat marks rose by 11 per cent
An average across 213 programmes of varying qualityThat any given SEL programme will produce it
Measured against control groups in the same studiesMeasured against the same children before the programme
Attributable to Durlak et al. (2011)A figure to quote without the source attached
This is the most misquoted number in the field. It appears in funding proposals as “11% improvement in academic results”, which is a different and unsupported claim. If a proposal crossing your desk says that, the person writing it has not read the paper.
The finding that actually matters for a head teacher: time given to SEL did not come out of academic achievement. That is the fear behind most refusals, and it is the fear the study addresses directly.

Beyond Marks
Behavioural and life outcomes
  • Behaviour: fewer conduct problems, less bullying and aggression
  • Wellbeing: lower anxiety, depression and emotional distress
  • Belonging: better relationships and a more positive school climate
  • Long term: follow-up studies link SEL to better later wellbeing
SEL is one of the few interventions that improves how children feel, how they behave and how they learn — at the same time.
DomainWhat was measured in the studies
Social and emotional skillsDirect assessments of identifying emotions, perspective-taking, problem-solving
AttitudesSelf-perception, bonding to school, beliefs about others
Positive social behaviourTeacher and observer reports of helping, cooperation, getting along
Conduct problemsDisruption, aggression, disciplinary referrals
Emotional distressReported anxiety, depression, withdrawal
Academic performanceTest scores and grades
Six domains at once is unusual. Most school interventions move one thing. The reason SEL moves several is that it is upstream of all of them: the same regulation skill shows up as fewer fights, less distress and more available attention.
Which domain to promise: promise conduct and climate. They move first, they are visible to everyone in the building, and they are what buys you the patience to keep going long enough for the rest.

The Long View
Effects that show up years later
Longitudinal research (including later work by Taylor, Durlak and colleagues) finds that the benefits of good SEL programmes persist — predicting better mental health, relationships and conduct months and years after the programme ends.
Skills, once built, keep paying off. This is why early, sustained SEL is more than a feel-good add-on.
Time after the programmeWhat follow-up research reports
ImmediatelyGains across skills, attitudes, behaviour, distress and academics
Six months to a yearEffects smaller than at the end, and still present
Several yearsFollow-up work (Taylor, Oberle, Durlak & Weissberg, 2017) reports gains persisting, including in wellbeing and conduct
Fading is normal and is not failure. Almost every educational effect shrinks after the intervention stops; the question is whether anything is left. For SEL the answer so far is yes, which is a stronger result than it sounds.
The implication for planning: a single year of SEL in Class 3, never revisited, is a weaker design than a thinner thread running from Class 1 to Class 8. Sustained beats intensive, and it is usually cheaper.

A Sound Investment
The economic case
Cost-benefit studies of SEL programmes have reported favourable returns — savings from reduced behavioural problems, grade repetition and later social costs. The exact ratio varies by programme and setting.
Treat specific return-on-investment figures cautiously and check their source and context before quoting them. The direction — SEL tends to pay for itself — is well supported.
Where the claimed savings come fromHow solid it is
Fewer disciplinary incidents and exclusionsReasonably direct; measurable inside the school year
Less grade repetitionPlausible but slow to observe and confounded by many other factors
Later mental-health and justice-system costsModelled far into the future; the assumptions do most of the work
Treat every headline ratio with suspicion — including the ones favourable to your case. They are built from cost assumptions specific to one country’s health, welfare and justice spending, and they transfer to South Asia badly. A ratio computed on US incarceration costs says almost nothing about a district in Bihar.
What you can defend: the direction, and your own school’s numbers. Count disciplinary incidents and teacher hours spent on them for one term before you start, and again a year later. A small honest local figure persuades a sceptical administrator more than a large imported one.

Read Evidence Well
What the evidence does and doesn't say
It does say
  • Well-implemented SEL improves outcomes on average
  • Quality of implementation matters greatly
  • Benefits span skills, behaviour and academics
It doesn't say
  • That any SEL label guarantees results
  • That one number applies to every context
  • That poorly run programmes will work
Most evidence is from high-income settings. Promising South Asian studies are growing — we return to this in Section 11.
Question to ask of any SEL claimWhy
Who was studied, and where?Most of the evidence is from high-income systems with small classes
Universal or targeted?Results from a programme for selected children do not transfer to whole classes
Who ran it — teachers or specialists?Effects are usually larger when specialists deliver, and rarer in practice
Who evaluated it?Developer-led evaluations report larger effects than independent ones
A fifth question: what was the comparison group doing? “Better than nothing” is a much weaker claim than “better than the lesson it replaced”, and brochures rarely say which one they mean.

These five are enough to appraise almost any brochure you will be handed. None needs statistical training, and a vendor who cannot answer them has told you something useful.

A Global Endorsement
Why SEL has spread worldwide
Because of evidence like this, SEL has moved from the margins to the mainstream — adopted by school systems across the US, Europe, Latin America, Africa and increasingly South Asia, and backed by bodies such as UNESCO and UNICEF as part of quality education.
Global momentum is real, but context still matters. A programme that worked in one setting must be tested and adapted, not assumed, in another.
What travelled wellWhat did not
The framework — five competencies as a shared vocabularySpecific lesson scripts, stories and names
The SAFE quality test for programme designAssumptions about class size and lesson length
The insistence that adults model the skillsNorms about eye contact, assertiveness and self-expression
The whole-school argumentDiscipline models that assume the school controls its own policy
The useful distinction is between the frame and the furniture. Frameworks and quality tests are general claims about how skills are learned, and they hold up. Materials are cultural artefacts, and they do not. Most failed transplants imported the furniture and left the frame behind.
Momentum has a cost. When something becomes mainstream, it also becomes a label. Section 11 covers how to tell an SEL programme from a rebranded assembly.

Self vs Others
Two halves: managing self, navigating others
Intrapersonal
  • Self-awareness — knowing your inner world
  • Self-management — steering it
Interpersonal
  • Social awareness — reading others
  • Relationship skills — connecting with them
Responsible decision-making sits at the centre, drawing on all four to choose well in real situations.
When this is the weak halfWhat you see in class
Intrapersonal — self-awareness and self-managementSudden outbursts, work abandoned at the first difficulty, a child who cannot say what is wrong
Interpersonal — social awareness and relationship skillsA child who is calm alone but cannot join a group, dominates it, or reads every disagreement as an attack
Both, under pressureThe pattern that appears in exam weeks and in the term after a family upheaval
Why the split is useful diagnostically: two children can produce the same behaviour — a fight at break — from opposite halves of the framework. One could not regulate the anger; the other misread a joke as contempt. The same consequence will fail one of them.
Sequencing for a whole class: most teachers get further starting with the intrapersonal half, because a class that can settle itself is the precondition for the group work the interpersonal half needs.

More Than Five
The CASEL 'wheel': SEL is systemic
CASEL pictures the five competencies at the centre of a wheel, surrounded by four widening settings. SEL is not just what happens in a child's head — it is nurtured (or undermined) by the systems around them.

Do not wait for all four rings. Waiting for community alignment before starting in your classroom is a way of never starting. Work outward from the ring you control.

Classrooms
The innermost ring: classrooms
Closest to the child is the classroom — through explicit SEL instruction, supportive teacher-student relationships and integration of SEL into how subjects are taught.
A warm, predictable, participatory classroom is itself an SEL intervention — before a single dedicated lesson is taught.
Free, and available on MondayWhat it teaches without a lesson
Greeting every child by name at the doorYou are known here, and being noticed is not conditional on your marks
A predictable order to the lessonNothing sudden is coming, so attention need not be held in reserve
Responding to a wrong answer with curiosityBeing wrong is survivable, so attempting is worth the risk
Naming your own state out loudAdults have feelings and manage them; that is what managing looks like
Repairing after you lose your temperRupture is normal and can be mended — possibly the most valuable lesson here
This slide is the answer to “we have no budget”. Every item costs nothing and needs no approval. A teacher who does all five and never teaches a scripted SEL lesson is doing more than a school that buys a curriculum and changes nothing else.
It is also the hardest slide. Doing these on a Friday afternoon in a class of sixty, after a difficult morning, is real work. Which is why Section 9 treats teacher support as part of the programme rather than a courtesy.

Schools
The next ring: the whole school
SEL thrives when the whole school is aligned — in discipline policies, in how staff treat each other, in the morning assembly, the corridors and the playground, not only in timetabled lessons.
Mixed signals undo SEL: teaching empathy in a lesson while running a harsh, punitive discipline system teaches the opposite.
Taught in the lessonContradicted by the school
Name your anger instead of acting on itA teacher who shouts across the corridor daily
Mistakes are how you learnMarks read aloud in rank order at assembly
Everyone belongs hereSeparate seating or serving by community, however informal
Talk it out rather than fightBoth children caned regardless of what happened
Children resolve the contradiction in favour of what the school does, not what it says. This is not cynicism on their part; it is accurate reading of where the power is. A lesson cannot outvote a policy.
Where to start if you lead a school: the discipline policy, before the curriculum. It is cheaper, it is faster, and until it changes, the curriculum will not take.

Families
Families and caregivers as partners
Children's strongest emotional learning happens at home. When schools partner with families — sharing language, respecting home cultures, inviting caregivers in — SEL is reinforced rather than contradicted.
In many South Asian homes, joint families and elders are powerful emotional teachers. Build on that, do not bypass it.
Usual practiceWhat to do instead
Contact home only when there is a complaintSend one specific piece of good news per child per term; it costs minutes and changes the relationship
A printed note in EnglishThe home language, and spoken where literacy cannot be assumed
Meetings at 11am on a working dayTimes that a daily-wage earner can attend without losing pay
“Support your child’s emotional development”One concrete thing: ask at dinner what was hard today, and listen without fixing
Partnership is not a workshop. Most family-engagement plans fail because they ask parents to attend something. The versions that work change what the school already sends home, which requires no attendance at all.
Build on what is there. Grandparents, elder siblings and neighbours are doing emotional teaching already, often skilfully. Asking a family what they do when a child is upset, and using their words in class, is respect and good practice at once.

Communities
The outer ring: communities
Community partners — faith groups, NGOs, sports clubs, youth programmes, local leaders — extend SEL beyond the school gate and anchor it in children's wider lives.
The four rings are why SEL is best understood as a systemic commitment, not a single lesson plan.
PartnerWhat they can carry that a school cannot
Anganwadi and ASHA workersReach children before school age and know the family situation first-hand
Sports clubs and coachesCooperation and losing well, practised where it is felt rather than discussed
Local NGOs and youth groupsAdolescents, out-of-school children, and the hours after the school day
Faith and community institutionsMoral vocabulary the family already trusts, and adults children already know
The point of the outer ring is coverage in time. A school holds a child for six hours of a waking sixteen, and not at all in the holidays. Skills practised in only one of those settings tend to stay there.
One caution: alignment means shared language and shared expectations, not handing the work over. Outsourcing SEL to a visiting NGO session, with the school unchanged, reproduces exactly the one-off failure Section 9 describes.

Competency One
Self-awareness: knowing your inner world
Self-awareness
The ability to understand one's own emotions, thoughts and values, and how they influence behaviour across contexts — including accurate self-perception and a sense of purpose. (CASEL)
It is the first competency because you cannot manage, express or regulate a feeling you cannot first notice and name.

Name to Tame
Naming emotions calms them
A core SEL practice is building an emotional vocabulary. Children who can say 'I feel frustrated' rather than only act it out gain a moment of choice between feeling and reaction.
'Name it to tame it.' Putting words to feelings is one of the simplest, most powerful regulation skills — for children and teachers alike.

Growth Mindset
'I can't do it' — 'I can't do it yet'
Growth mindset
The belief, developed in Carol Dweck's research, that ability can grow with effort and good strategies — as opposed to a fixed mindset that treats it as innate and unchangeable.
A growth mindset is part of healthy self-awareness: it shapes how a child interprets struggle, failure and feedback.
SituationFixed readingGrowth reading
Fails a test“I am weak in maths.”“I did not know this part yet.”
Work is hardEvidence of not being cleverEvidence of learning happening
Given feedbackA verdict to be defended againstInformation about the next attempt
A classmate does wellA threat — her success is my failureA source; find out what she did
Be honest about the research. Growth-mindset effects in large replications are real but modest, and largest for students who were struggling. It is a useful lens for how a child reads difficulty. It is not a substitute for teaching the content well, and a school that offers only mindset to children who lack textbooks has changed nothing.
The failure mode to avoid: “yet” used as a way of declining to help. Adding the word to an unchanged lesson tells a child the gap is theirs to close alone.

Praise the Process
How feedback shapes mindset
Fixed-mindset praise
'You're so clever!' ties worth to talent — so failure feels like proof of being not-clever.
Growth-mindset praise
'You worked hard and tried a new strategy!' ties worth to effort and method — which a child can always change.
Mindset is not magic. It works alongside real teaching and fair conditions — never as a way to blame children for structural barriers.
What you might sayWhat the child hears
“Brilliant! You are so bright.”My worth rides on the next mark
“Good job.”Nothing in particular; it is filler
“You checked your working before handing it in.”That specific act is what produced this, and I can do it again
“You tried a different method when the first stalled.”Being stuck is a step, not a verdict
“Not yet — and here is the part to work on.”There is a route, and someone will show me it
Effort praise for work that was easy backfires. Children read it accurately as consolation, and conclude the adult expects little of them. Praise the process only where there was a process.
A small experiment: for one week, write down what you said to the three children you praised most. Most teachers find it is trait praise to the already-confident. Redirecting it is free.

A Caution
Self-awareness without judgement
Building self-awareness should never tip into making children feel there is something wrong with their feelings. The goal is to notice and accept emotions — all of them — not to label some as bad.
'There are no bad feelings, only feelings that need understanding.' Anger and sadness are information, not misbehaviour.
Well-meant, but harmfulWhy
“Don’t be sad, it is nothing.”Teaches that the feeling was wrong, so next time it is hidden
“Big boys don’t cry.”Assigns feelings by gender; the cost lands years later
“Look how well she is coping.”Makes composure a competition and shame the price of losing
“Calm down.”An instruction with no method attached, delivered when method is what is missing
The distinction to hold: every feeling is acceptable; not every action is. “You are allowed to be furious with him. You are not allowed to hit him” keeps both halves, and it is the sentence most worth practising until it is automatic.
Watch for the gendered pattern in your own class. In most classrooms boys’ sadness and girls’ anger are the two feelings least often allowed. Both children lose a piece of the vocabulary this section is trying to build.

Self-management
The ability to manage emotions, thoughts and behaviours effectively across situations — including regulating emotions, managing stress, controlling impulses, and setting and working toward goals. (CASEL)

Stress
Understanding stress — and toxic stress
Some stress is normal and even helpful. But chronic, severe stress — from poverty, violence or neglect — can overwhelm a child's developing regulation system and harm learning and health.
Self-management skills help, but they are not a substitute for safety. A child facing real danger needs protection first, breathing exercises second.
TypeWhat it looks likeWhat helps
Positive stressNerves before a recitation; passesNormal, and useful. Do not remove it
Tolerable stressA bereavement, an illness at homeA steady adult relationship buffers it
Toxic stressSustained violence, hunger, neglect, with no reliable adultProtection and material change first; skills cannot substitute
The distinction decides what your response should be. Breathing exercises are appropriate for the first two rows and close to insulting for the third. A child who is not safe needs the adults around them to act, and SEL that stops at teaching the child to cope becomes part of the problem.
What a teacher can still do in the third row: be the reliable adult. The buffering effect of one consistent, predictable relationship is among the better-established findings in the developmental literature, and it is within reach on an ordinary school day.

Impulse Control
The space between stimulus and response
Self-management grows the pause between an urge and an action — the space in which choice lives. This is impulse control, and it strengthens with practice and brain maturation.
Between stimulus and response there is a space. In that space is our power to choose our response.
— commonly attributed to Viktor Frankl
AgeWhat is reasonable to expect
5–7Waits briefly with adult support; needs the pause named for them out loud
8–11Can use a rehearsed strategy if it was practised calm, and often forgets under pressure
12–15Capacity grows, but peer presence and status raise the stakes sharply; public correction backfires
16+Adult-like in the abstract; still uneven when tired, hungry or humiliated — as with adults
Adolescent impulsivity is not regression. The period in which social reward is felt most intensely arrives before the capacity to weigh it matures. Treating that as defiance produces a discipline record; treating it as development produces a plan.
Practical consequence: with adolescents, correct privately. The same words that land as guidance in a corridor land as a challenge to status in front of thirty peers, and the child will defend the status.

Delayed Gratification
Persistence and patience
Working toward a goal means tolerating frustration and delaying reward — finishing the hard problem, practising the difficult passage. SEL builds the stamina that turns intention into achievement.
Frame setbacks as information, not verdicts: 'That strategy didn't work — what will you try next?'
After a setback, instead ofSay
“You did not try hard enough.”“Which part stopped you? Let us look at that bit.”
“Never mind, it does not matter.”“It mattered to you. What would you change next time?”
“See, this is what happens.”“That strategy did not work. What is the next one to try?”
Persistence is not willpower. It is mostly having a next move. A child who abandons work after one failure often has no second strategy, and no amount of exhortation supplies one. Teaching the alternative method is the intervention.
Be careful with “grit” language in unequal settings. Asking a child to persist through obstacles the school could remove — no light to study by, no textbook, a four-kilometre walk — moves a structural problem onto a ten-year-old.

  • Predictable routines and clear signals reduce the need to self-regulate from scratch
  • A visible daily schedule lowers anxiety about what comes next
  • Brief movement or breathing breaks reset a restless class
  • Co-created class agreements give children ownership of behaviour
Structure is kindness. A well-ordered classroom does much of the regulating for children, freeing them to learn.
RoutineThe regulation it does for the child
A visible schedule for the dayRemoves the low background anxiety of not knowing what is next
A consistent quiet signalEnds the shouting cycle that raises everyone’s arousal, adult included
Two-minute movement break each hourDischarges restlessness before it becomes a disruption to punish

Empathy

Empathy
Sensing and understanding what another person feels — 'I can imagine how that felt for you.'
Not the same as
Pity (looking down on), or agreement. You can empathise with someone you disagree with.
Empathy is the emotional root of kindness, cooperation and the will to reduce others' suffering.

The Empathy-Action Gap
From feeling to doing
Social awareness matters most when it leads to action. A child who notices a classmate's distress and acts on it — a kind word, an invitation, standing up to unfairness — turns empathy into compassion.
This is the bridge to the next competency: awareness of others becomes useful only through the relationship skills that let a child act.

Cooperation
Working together
Cooperation — sharing tasks, taking turns, contributing to a shared goal — is learned through structured group work, classroom jobs and play, not exhortation alone.
Design groups so every child has a real role. 'Group work' where one child does everything teaches the opposite of cooperation.

  • Brainstorm several possible solutions, without judging yet
  • Weigh each option's likely consequences
  • Choose, act, then review how it went

Agency

When a measure becomes a target
When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.
— Goodhart's law, as popularised by Marilyn Strathern
Attach rewards or punishments to an SEL score and you change the very behaviour you are trying to read — this is especially dangerous for something as intimate as a child's emotional life.

Equity in Measurement
Whose 'good behaviour' are we measuring?
SEL measures can carry cultural bias. Norms for 'assertiveness', 'eye contact' or 'self-control' differ across cultures, castes, genders and neurotypes. A tool can mistake difference for deficit.
Ask: whose standard of 'competence' is built into this tool? Measurement must not become a new way to label marginalised children as lacking.
Item scores “low” forThe alternative explanation
“Makes eye contact when spoken to”In many families, lowering the eyes to an elder is respect
“Speaks up confidently in a group”Girls in many classrooms are penalised for exactly this

Measure Responsibly
Principles for responsible SEL measurement
  • Measure to improve, never to rank or punish
  • Use multiple methods; treat scores as clues, not verdicts
  • Check tools for cultural and linguistic fairness
  • Protect privacy — emotional data is sensitive data
  • Focus on programmes and climate, not on labelling individuals
PrincipleWhat it looks like on paper
Measure to improveThe purpose, and the prohibited uses, written down before collection
Multiple methodsNo decision rests on a single instrument
Check fairnessItems reviewed by local teachers; translation checked back
Protect privacyNamed responses seen by named people only; retention limited
Focus on the programmeReported as class and school patterns, not child-level scores
A one-page measurement plan covering these five points is enough, and it is the document that protects the children when the person who set the programme up has moved on and someone new wants the individual scores.
The honest default: if you cannot meet all five, measure climate and programme delivery only, and leave individual skills unmeasured. Less data, held responsibly, beats more data held badly.

Policy
NEP 2020 puts emotion on the agenda
India's National Education Policy 2020 explicitly emphasises socio-emotional learning and the holistic development of learners — not just cognitive skills — placing wellbeing, values and life skills at the centre of education.
This is a major opening. SEL in India is no longer a fringe idea — it is aligned with national policy direction.
What NEP 2020 opensWhat it does not settle
Language: SEL is named policy, not an NGO add-onWho trains teachers to deliver it, and when
Curricular space for life skills and valuesHow that space survives board-exam pressure in Classes 9–12
Mother-tongue instruction in early yearsImplementation in multilingual classrooms and cities
Holistic report cards including self-assessmentWhether these become another thing children are ranked on
Use the policy language when you argue internally. A head teacher who will not be moved by a meta-analysis will often be moved by the fact that this is national policy direction, and the second argument costs you nothing.
The fourth row is the risk to watch. Holistic progress cards are a genuine opening and also the most likely route by which SEL becomes a graded, ranked, high-stakes thing — the outcome Section 10 spends eight slides warning about.

SEL initiatives across the region

Don't Just Import
Contextualisation, not copy-paste
Most SEL curricula were designed in the Global North. Transplanting them unchanged can misfire — in language, examples, values and classroom realities. SEL must be adapted, not merely translated.
Ask: do the stories, names, dilemmas and emotion words fit the children in front of you? If not, rebuild them with local teachers and communities.
In the imported materialWhat it assumesAdaptation needed
“Talk to your parents about your feelings”A two-adult home with time and privacyWiden to any trusted adult, including elders and siblings
A scenario about a sleepoverA social form most children will not recogniseRewrite with a wedding, a festival, a shared courtyard
“Find a quiet space to calm down”Space existsStrategies that work standing in a crowded room
Names: Emma, JackNothing, but it signals the material is not for youLocal names, and vary community and gender deliberately
Adaptation is not translation. A faithfully translated scenario about a sleepover is still a scenario about a sleepover. The work is to keep the skill being practised and rebuild the situation around it — which teachers can do, and vendors usually cannot.
Do it with teachers, in a room, in an afternoon. Give them the skill list and let them write the scenarios. The material improves and, more usefully, the teachers now own it.

Culture
Individual and collective selves
Imported framing
Often centres the individual — 'my' feelings, 'my' goals, personal self-expression.
Many South Asian settings
Centre family, community and interdependence — duty, respect and the collective self.
Neither is 'more advanced'. Good SEL honours collective values while still giving each child voice and dignity.
QuestionIndividualist framingInterdependent framing
What is a good decision?One I chose for myselfOne that holds the family together
What is self-management?Getting what I want, laterNot burdening others with my state
What is confidence?Speaking upKnowing when not to
Whose feelings matter?Mine, expressedThe room’s, read
Neither column is the advanced one. Each carries a real risk: the left can produce children indifferent to those around them; the right can silence a child who needs to be heard, especially a girl, especially about harm inside the family.
What good SEL holds onto in both: every child can name what they feel, every child can say no to harm, and every child is answerable for the effect they have on others. Those three survive translation between the columns.

Equity
SEL and India's inequalities
SEL can either challenge or reinforce inequality. Done well, it builds belonging across caste, class, religion and gender. Done carelessly, it can blame marginalised children for 'poor self-control' shaped by injustice.
Never use SEL to pathologise poverty or difference. A hungry, frightened or excluded child needs justice and safety, not a lesson in 'grit'.
SEL that reinforces inequalitySEL that challenges it
Teaches “self-control” to hungry childrenFixes the midday meal first, then teaches
Scores a Dalit child low on “confidence”Asks what in this classroom makes confidence costly for her
Praises compliance in girls, tolerates it less in boysNotices the pattern and states it aloud to the staff
Treats a disabled child’s exclusion as her skills gapTreats it as the class’s relationship-skills gap
The left column is not a caricature. It is the ordinary result of applying a child-focused framework in an unequal setting without asking who created the conditions. Nothing in SEL prevents it; only the people running it do.
One question, asked routinely: is this skill missing, or is this environment making the skill impossible? Ask it before every intervention aimed at an individual child.

Trauma-Informed
Being trauma-informed
Many children in the region face adversity — poverty, violence, displacement, loss. A trauma-informed approach asks not 'what's wrong with this child?' but 'what has this child been through?'
  • Prioritise safety, predictability and trusted relationships
  • Recognise that 'misbehaviour' is often a stress response
  • Know your limits — refer serious distress to trained help
BehaviourRead as misconductRead as stress response
Explodes at a small correctionDefiance; punishThreat system already primed; reduce, do not escalate
Vacant, unresponsive in classLaziness; shameShutdown; re-engage gently, privately
Refuses to be alone with an adultRudenessPossible history; note it, do not force it
Hoards food or possessionsStealingScarcity; address the scarcity
Trauma-informed does not mean excusing behaviour. Limits still hold; the child still repairs. What changes is the explanation you start from, and therefore what you try first — and the middle column reliably makes the behaviour worse.
Know the edge of your role. Suspected abuse, self-harm or sustained distress goes to your school’s child-protection route and to trained help, today. A teacher is not a therapist, and acting as one delays the help the child actually needs.

Language
Teaching feelings in the mother tongue
Emotions live most vividly in a child's home language. SEL in an unfamiliar language stays abstract; SEL in the mother tongue — with local idioms, stories and emotion words — reaches the heart.
This aligns with NEP 2020's emphasis on early learning in the mother tongue. Feelings and first language belong together.
PracticeWhy it matters here
Collect emotion words in every language in the roomSome carry distinctions English does not; children become the experts
Let a child answer the check-in in any languageThe alternative is measuring English fluency and calling it self-awareness
Use local stories, proverbs and songsThe emotional vocabulary is already in them, and already known at home
Keep key SEL terms in the home languageShared language with families depends on it
In a classroom with five home languages, the aim is not to teach in all five. It is that no child is required to perform an inner state in a language they are still acquiring — which is a test of English, scored as a test of feeling.
This is also the cheapest inclusion measure in the deck, and it aligns with NEP 2020’s early-years emphasis, so it is easy to defend upward.

Cautions
Honest cautions for SEL in our context
  • Crowded classrooms and exam pressure leave little space — plan realistically
  • Most evidence is from elsewhere; build and share local evidence
  • Beware SEL as a buzzword that ticks a box without changing practice
  • Protect it from becoming one more thing children are tested and ranked on
ConstraintRealistic response
Sixty children, one room, no spaceRoutines and language, not activities needing floor space
Board-exam pressure from Class 9Integrate; explicit slots will not survive, and integration can
Teacher already teaching three subjectsChoose two practices and drop the rest; partial and sustained beats complete and abandoned
Evidence is mostly from elsewhereRecord your own: incidents, attendance, what teachers notice
SEL as a box to tickAsk what changed in the timetable; if nothing did, nothing changed
These constraints are real and are not solved by enthusiasm. A plan that assumes them away will fail in the second term, and the failure will be attributed to SEL rather than to the plan.
Build the local evidence, however small. One school’s honest before-and-after count of disciplinary incidents is worth more in your own state’s policy conversation than another meta-analysis from elsewhere.
