The Method
Observation is data, not decoration
Most development evidence comes from what people tell us — surveys, interviews, self-reports. Observation is the disciplined practice of learning by watching what people actually do, in the real settings where they do it. It is a method with its own rigour, not a coffee break.
Observation
Systematically watching, listening to and recording behaviour, interactions and settings in the field — to understand what happens, not only what people say happens.
You do not need a clipboard to start. You need to be present, curious and honest about what you actually see.
Evidence sourceRecordsFails when
SurveyWhat people reportReporting differs from doing
InterviewWhat people can articulateThe practice is below awareness
Administrative recordWhat was written downThe register is aspirational
ObservationWhat actually happenedThe observer is present
Each method fails differently, which is the entire argument for using more than one. Observation is not more truthful than a survey — it is wrong about different things.
It is also the only method here that can surface something nobody thought to ask about. A survey returns answers to your questions; observation returns what is there.

The Core Gap
The say–do gap
People do not always do what they say — not from dishonesty, but because memory is faulty, norms shape answers, and habits run below awareness. The say–do gap is the distance between reported and observed behaviour.

→

Treating the gap as dishonesty is the error that ruins the finding. A respondent reporting they wash their hands is usually reporting the practice they endorse, which is real information about norms.
So the gap has two readings: what people do, and what they believe they should do. Both matter, and only having the two together tells you which is which.

What Surveys Miss
The questionnaire never asked
  • The unspoken: a clinic queue where men are served before women
  • The unmeasured: the broken hand-pump nobody listed as a problem
  • The contextual: who sits where, who speaks, who stays silent
  • The routine: habits so normal that respondents forget to mention them
A survey can only return answers to questions you already knew to ask. Observation surfaces the questions you did not.
CategoryExampleWhy a survey misses it
The unspokenMen served before women in a queueNobody would report it as a problem
The unmeasuredA broken hand-pump on no listIt is not in the instrument
The contextualWho sits where in a meetingNot a variable
The routineThe workaround everyone usesToo ordinary to mention
The routine is the richest category and the hardest to see. Practices so ordinary that nobody would think to describe them are exactly what a newcomer with a notebook can catch.
It is also why prolonged engagement matters. In the first week you see everything and understand none of it; by the fourth you understand it and have stopped noticing.

Being Present
The value of simply being there
Time in the field builds tacit understanding — the feel of a place, its rhythms, its tensions. Much of what matters in development is relational and spatial, and only becomes visible to someone who lingers.
You can observe a lot just by watching.
— attributed to Yogi Berra
Being present gives youWhich cannot be got from data
The rhythm of a placeWhen things happen, and when nothing does
Spatial relationsWho is near the centre, who is at the edge
Tone and tensionWhat is not said in a meeting
Credibility with the people thereThey have seen you show up
The last row is instrumental as well as ethical. Observers who have been present repeatedly are told things that a one-day visitor with a questionnaire never hears.
Tacit understanding is real and is not evidence. It should shape which questions you ask and what you look for — not appear in the findings as a claim.

A Caution
Observation is powerful, not infallible
Watching is interpretation. Two observers can see the same scene and record different things. The rest of this course is about making observation systematic, transparent and ethical — so it earns the word 'evidence'.
Good observation is not 'just looking'. It is looking with a question, a method, and a record.
Observation riskWhat makes it systematic instead
Two observers see different thingsDefined categories; inter-observer checks
You see what you expectActively seeking disconfirming cases
Presence changes behaviourProlonged engagement; naming the effect
Interpretation smuggled into the recordLow-inference descriptive notes
Watching is interpretation, and the response is not to stop interpreting but to separate the layers. Every technique in this deck exists to keep the description recoverable from the reading.
The rest of the course is essentially this table expanded. Each risk gets a discipline, and none of the disciplines removes the risk — they make it visible and checkable.

Role
The participant↔non-participant spectrum
Gold & others mapped this classic continuum. More participation buys you depth and trust; less buys you distance and a wider view. You will often move along it within one study.

Gold’s continuum dates from 1958 and still frames the choice. Its value is in forcing the decision: most fieldwork drifts to the third position without anyone selecting it.
You will move along it during a study, and that is fine as long as you record when and why. Undocumented drift is what makes a methods section unverifiable.

Participant
Participant observation
The observer takes part in the life of the setting — joining a self-help group meeting, working a shift, sharing a meal — while observing. The anthropologist's classic stance.
Gains
  • Trust and access insiders never grant outsiders
  • Felt, embodied understanding of routines
  • The unguarded, backstage moments
Costs
  • Time-intensive — weeks or months
  • Risk of 'going native' and losing distance
  • Your presence changes the scene
Participant observation givesAt the cost of
Access to what insiders seeYour presence changes the setting
Understanding through doingDifficulty taking notes in the moment
Trust over timeTime — months, not days
Tacit knowledgeRisk of going native
Note-taking is the practical constraint people underestimate. You cannot participate in a self-help group meeting and write continuously, so jotted notes and same-day expansion become non-negotiable.
Decide in advance what you will do if participating means witnessing something you should act on. That situation arrives more often than the methods literature suggests.

Non-Participant
Non-participant observation
The observer stays apart — watching a school classroom from the back, timing a clinic queue, mapping a market — without joining in. More detached, more easily structured.
Gains
  • Cleaner, more comparable records
  • Less time per site; cover more sites
  • Easier to standardise across observers
Costs
  • Surface view — misses meaning
  • Less trust, less backstage access
  • A watcher in the corner still alters behaviour
Non-participant observationSuits

Watching a classroom from the backStructured tallies
Mapping a marketSpatial patterns

Detachment buys comparability and costs understanding. You can count what happens in the queue and you will not learn why people accept the order they are served in.
It is also more visible than it feels. Someone sitting at the back of a classroom with a clipboard is an event in that classroom, whatever they intend.

Disclosure
Overt vs covert
Overt
People know they are being observed and have consented. The ethical default. Costs you some of the natural behaviour you came to see (the observer effect).
Covert
People do not know they are observed. Yields unguarded behaviour — but denies consent and carries serious ethical and legal risk. Rarely justifiable in development work.
Default to overt. Covert observation needs an exceptional justification and ethics-committee approval — we return to this in Section 10.
OvertCovert
ConsentGivenImpossible
Observer effectPresent, decays with timeAbsent
Ethical statusThe defaultRequires exceptional justification
ApprovalRoutineRarely granted
Risk if discoveredNoneSevere, and borne by those observed
The observer effect decays with prolonged engagement, which removes most of the argument for covert work. People cannot sustain performance for weeks, so time buys what deception was meant to buy.
Covert observation in public space is a different case from covert observation in private or semi-private settings. Section 10 sets the bar; it is high, and it is rarely met.

Choosing
Match the type to the question
  • Want meaning and process? Lean participant and unstructured.
  • Want comparable counts across sites? Lean non-participant and structured.
  • Watching a private or sensitive setting? Overt, with consent, always.
  • Most real studies blend these, and shift as understanding grows.
There is no single 'best' type — only the type that fits your question, your ethics and your time.
Your questionLean toward
How does this actually work?Participant, unstructured
How do sites compare?Non-participant, structured
Does the register match reality?Non-participant, structured, brief
Why does uptake differ here?Participant, then interviews
Anything private or sensitiveProbably not observation at all
The last row is the one to hold onto. Some questions should not be answered by watching, and choosing observation because it is available rather than appropriate is a real risk in field work.
Match the type to the question before the fieldwork is scheduled. The type decides the time required, and a design chosen after the calendar is fixed will be chosen by the calendar.

The Problem
A field site overwhelms the eye
Stand in a busy market, an anganwadi, a gram sabha — a thousand things happen at once. Without a framework you record randomly, miss patterns, and tire fast. You need a lens that tells you what to look at.
The cure for overwhelm is not to watch harder. It is to watch with a structure.
Without a frameworkWith one
You record what catches the eyeYou record what you decided matters
Patterns are missedPatterns accumulate across sessions
Two observers diverge wildlyThey can be compared
You tire in an hourYou can sustain a session
Fatigue is the practical argument nobody mentions. Unstructured watching of a busy setting is exhausting, and the quality of what you record falls sharply after the first hour.
Plan sessions of a length you can actually sustain, with breaks to write. Three good forty-minute observations beat one unfocused half-day.

Spradley
Spradley's nine dimensions of a social situation
James Spradley (Participant Observation, 1980) gave fieldworkers a checklist of nine dimensions present in any social situation. Run through them and you will rarely miss something important.
DimensionAsk…

ActorsThe people present — who, how many

ActivitiesWhat is being done?

Mapping
Draw the space
A simple sketch map — doorways, seating, the queue, where the worker stands — captures spatial patterns words cannot. Who sits near the front? Who is pushed to the margins? Space encodes power.
Keep a sketch map in every field notebook. Mark movement with arrows; mark where people cluster and where they avoid.
Sketch map recordsWhich reveals
Doorways and thresholdsWho may enter, who waits outside
SeatingHierarchy, made physical
Where the worker standsWhether they face or turn from people
Where the queue formsWho is served first
Where children playWhat space they are given
Space encodes hierarchy in ways nobody would state aloud. Who sits on the mat and who sits on the chair is a caste and gender fact that no interview would surface as such.
Draw the map badly and quickly. A rough sketch with positions marked carries the finding; waiting until you can draw it properly means you will not draw it.

Your Lens
What you look for shapes what you find
A framework focuses attention — but a fixed framework can also blind you to what it leaves out. Hold your lens firmly enough to be systematic, loosely enough to be surprised.
Leave a column in your notes for 'things that don't fit my framework'. That column is often where the insight hides.
FrameworkHelps byBlinds by
Spradley’s nine dimensionsSystematic coverageNothing much — it is broad
A programme logframeFocusing on what you fundExcluding everything you do not
A checklist from another siteComparabilityImporting another place’s categories

The logframe row is the practitioner’s trap. Observing a programme against its own indicators finds out whether it did what it said, and never finds out what else is happening.
Keep a section of every session for what falls outside the framework. If it stays empty across many sessions, either the framework is very good or you have stopped looking.

The Record
If it isn't written down, it didn't happen
Observation produces nothing without a record. Memory decays fast and reshapes itself to fit our expectations. Field notes are the raw data of observation — the single most important craft in this course.
Writing fieldnotes is not a record of what happened so much as a way of thinking about what happened.
— Emerson, Fretz & Shaw, Writing Ethnographic Fieldnotes
Time since observingWhat survives
DuringEverything — but you cannot write it all
Same dayMost detail, sequence, exact phrasing
Next dayThe outline; specifics gone
A week laterA story shaped to fit what you concluded
The last row is the danger and it does not feel like forgetting. Memory reconstructs coherently, so a week-old account feels vivid and reliable while having quietly organised itself around your interpretation.
Write up the same day, without exception. Fieldwork schedules that pack sessions back to back without writing time are producing worse data than fewer sessions would.

Two Moments
Jotted notes, then expanded notes
Jotted notes (in the field)
Quick scratch notes, key words, a sketch, an exact quote. Brief and unobtrusive — just enough to trigger memory later.
Expanded notes (after)
Written up the same day, while memory is fresh: the full scene, in order, with detail. The jottings are the seed; the expanded notes are the crop.
The golden rule of fieldnotes: write up the same day. One night's sleep erases more than you think.
Jotted notesExpanded notes
WhenIn the field, duringSame day, after
ContentKey words, quotes, a sketchFull scene-by-scene account
LengthA pageSeveral pages per hour observed
PurposeTrigger memoryThe actual data
Budget roughly an hour of writing per hour observed, more at the start. Fieldwork plans that ignore this arithmetic produce jottings nobody ever expands, which is to say no data at all.
Keep jottings unobtrusive. Visible sustained note-taking changes the behaviour you came to see, which is why the expansion happens afterwards rather than in the room.

Low Inference
Describe before you interpret
High inference
'The mother was hostile and uncooperative.' — a judgement, already an interpretation, hard to verify.
Low inference
'The mother folded her arms, looked away, and answered in one word.' — observable detail anyone could have recorded.
Record the behaviour, not your label for it. Labels can be revised later; lost observations cannot be recovered.
High inferenceLow inference
"The mother was hostile""She folded her arms and did not reply for several seconds"
"The class was disengaged""Eleven of thirty had heads down; four were writing"
"The worker was dismissive""She answered without looking up from the register"
"The meeting was tense""Two people spoke; nobody responded to either"
The left column cannot be checked and the right column can. A reader who disagrees with your reading of the mother can still use your description; the judgement offers them nothing to work with.
You will still interpret — deciding to record the folded arms is already a choice. The discipline records the observable thing alongside the reading, rather than only the reading.

Thick Description
Thick description
Thick description
Clifford Geertz's term: an account that records not just an action but its context and meaning — the difference between a 'twitch' and a 'wink' lies in what it means to the people involved.
Thin: 'a man nodded.' Thick: 'when the upper-caste landlord entered, the labourer stopped speaking mid-sentence and nodded — a gesture of deference everyone present understood.' Thick description carries the meaning.
Thin descriptionThick description adds
"He winked"Whether it was a twitch, a signal, a parody
"They queued"Who joined where, who was let in, who left
"The meeting ended"Who left first, and what that meant there
Geertz’s point is about checkability, not richness. Thick description records enough context that a reader can assess whether your interpretation is right — it is a rigour standard, not a style.
If your write-up gives a reader no basis to disagree with your reading, it is not thick description. It is assertion with field notes attached.

Caution
Don't over-reach from what you saw
Observation at a handful of sites cannot, on its own, prove a statement about a whole district. State what your observation can carry — rich explanation, mechanism, hypotheses — and be honest about what it cannot.
The fastest way to lose credibility is to claim population-level certainty from a few vivid visits. Match the claim to the evidence.

The Risk
The observer is part of the experiment
In observation there is no neutral camera. You select, perceive and interpret — and your presence changes the scene. Rigour is not the absence of bias; it is the disciplined management of it.
The honest question is not 'am I biased?' (you are) but 'which biases are at work here, and what am I doing about them?'
Source of biasDiscipline
You select what to recordA framework, and space for what falls outside
You perceive selectivelyMultiple observers
You interpretSeparate descriptive and reflective layers
Your presence changes the sceneProlonged engagement; name the effect
Rigour is not the absence of bias, it is its management and disclosure. An observational study claiming neutrality has misunderstood the method; one that names its observer’s position has not.
Each discipline here reduces one source and removes none. Reporting which you used, and which you could not, is what lets a reader weigh the findings appropriately.

Observer Effect
The observer effect & the Hawthorne effect
Observer (Hawthorne) effect
People behave differently when they know they are being watched — named after the 1920s–30s Hawthorne Works studies, where worker output changed simply because attention was being paid.
Mitigate by spending enough time that your presence becomes ordinary, observing routine rather than special events, and being honest in reporting that you were there.
Observer effectWhat reduces it
Behaviour changes when watchedTime — performance is hard to sustain
Peaks on the first visitRepeat visits, unannounced where ethical
Stronger with a clipboard or cameraMinimal, unobtrusive recording
Cannot be eliminatedName it in the methods
The effect decays, which is the strongest practical argument for prolonged engagement. A single announced visit observes a performance; the fourth visit observes something much closer to routine.
The Hawthorne studies of the 1920s and 30s gave the effect its name and their original interpretation is now much disputed. The phenomenon is real; the founding evidence for it is weaker than the label suggests.

Selective Attention
Selective attention
The eye cannot take in everything, so it filters — and the filter is shaped by what you expect and care about. The famous 'invisible gorilla' experiment showed observers can miss the obvious while counting something else.
Counter it with a framework that forces attention to dimensions you might skip — and with a second observer who notices what you do not.

Attention narrows under loadShorter sessions; structured prompts
You cannot notice what you missedThis is why it is dangerous
The invisible-gorilla studies showed people focused on a counting task failing to see something obvious in plain view. Field observation is exactly that task, sustained for an hour.
The last row is why individual effort cannot fix it. You have no access to what your attention filtered out, which makes a second observer the only real remedy.

Confirmation Bias
Confirmation bias
Confirmation bias
The tendency to notice, record and weight what fits what you already believe, while overlooking what contradicts it — the single most dangerous bias in field observation.
The discipline of hunting for disconfirming cases (Section 6) exists precisely to fight this. If everything confirms your prior, suspect yourself, not the world.
Confirmation bias shows asCounter
Recording what fits, skipping what does notA quota for disconfirming observations
Weighting a confirming case more heavilyCount instances, do not weigh impressions
Asking questions that presume the answerHave someone else read your guide
Stopping once the pattern is clearKeep observing past the point of comfort
It is the most dangerous bias in observation because it feels like insight. The pattern becomes clearer with each session precisely because you are noticing the confirming instances more readily.
The practical counter is to write down, before fieldwork, what you expect to find — and then check your notes specifically for evidence against it.

Going Native
Going native — losing the distance
Going native is the opposite risk to detachment: the observer so identifies with the group that they stop questioning, adopt its assumptions, and lose the outsider's vantage that made observation valuable.
The fieldworker's tightrope: close enough to understand, distant enough to see. Step off and write reflective notes regularly to keep your footing.

RiskMisreading behaviourAdopting the group’s assumptions
RemedyProlonged engagementPeer debriefing; time away
Both ends fail and the second is harder to notice from inside. Detachment feels like insufficiency; going native feels like understanding, which is why it needs an outside reader.
Schedule someone who was not there to read your notes periodically. They will ask the questions the setting has trained you to stop asking.

Reflexivity
Reflexivity
Reflexivity
The practice of examining how you — your assumptions, reactions and presence — shape what you observe and conclude, and recording it openly as part of the data.
Keep a reflexive thread in your notes: What surprised me? What irritated me? What did I expect to see? Your reactions are evidence about both the setting and yourself.
Reflexive journal recordsWhich later
What you expected before going inLets you check what you found against it
Your reactions duringSeparates discomfort from evidence
How people responded to youExplains what you could and could not see
Changes in your own readingForms part of the audit trail
Keep it alongside field notes, dated, from the first day. Written at the end, reflexivity is recollection; written throughout, it is data about how the data was produced.
It is not confession and not self-indulgence. Its purpose is to let a reader judge which parts of your account your presence made accessible and which parts it foreclosed.

Positionality
Positionality
Who you are — gender, caste, class, age, language, the agency you represent — shapes what people show you and what you are able to see. Positionality is naming that honestly.
A young male outsider and a local older woman will observe different versions of the same household. Neither is the truth; both must be named.

First Principle
You are watching real people's lives
Observation enters people's spaces, routines and vulnerabilities — often without them gaining anything. Ethics is not a clearance form at the end; it is a duty that runs through every minute in the field.
The privilege of observing someone's life carries the obligation to protect it.
— a principle of field research ethics

Consent
Informed consent in observation
People should know who you are, that you are observing, why, what you will do with what you record, and that they may decline — in a language and form they truly understand. Consent is harder in observation, because settings are fluid and people come and go.
Gain consent from gatekeepers and the people themselves. A headmaster's permission is not a child's consent.

That you are observingNot visiting

Power
Power and vulnerability
The observer usually holds more power than the observed — literacy, institutional backing, the ability to leave. In settings of poverty, caste or gender hierarchy, a 'request' from an outsider can feel impossible to refuse.
Make refusal genuinely costless. Watch for consent given only because saying no felt unsafe — that is not real consent.

Harm
Do no harm — including by what you reveal
  • What you write could expose someone to retaliation if read by the wrong person
  • Disguise identities; aggregate; remove tell-tale details in small communities
  • Some things you observe should never be published — judgement, not rules
  • If you witness serious harm, your duty of care may override your role as observer
Anonymity in a small village is hard: 'the only widow with a disabled son' identifies a person. Protect with care, not just labels.

Covert Again
The high bar for covert observation
Covert observation denies people the chance to consent. It can only be contemplated when the knowledge is important, no other method is possible, and harm is minimal — and even then it needs formal ethics approval. In most development work it is neither necessary nor defensible.
If you find yourself hiding why you are there, stop and ask whether the study should happen at all.
Covert observation requires all ofRarely met
The knowledge matters substantiallySometimes
No other method could obtain itRarely
Harm to those observed is minimalDepends entirely
Independent ethics approvalAlmost never granted
All four must hold, which is why the bar is effectively prohibitive in development practice. The second is where most proposed covert work fails: prolonged overt engagement would have worked.
Mystery-client studies of service delivery are the main legitimate case, and even there the observed staff are the ones bearing the risk of what you find.

Reciprocity
Give something back
People give you their time, their space and their trust. Ethical observation asks what flows back: findings shared in usable form, recommendations that improve the service they use, respect for their time and their account of their own lives.
The test of ethical fieldwork: would the people you observed feel fairly treated if they read everything you wrote about them?
