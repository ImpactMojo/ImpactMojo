The Definition
Gender mainstreaming, in the words that coined it
Mainstreaming a gender perspective is the process of assessing the implications for women and men of any planned action — including legislation, policies or programmes, in all areas and at all levels.
— UN ECOSOC Agreed Conclusions, 1997
The aim is equality as the goal; mainstreaming is the strategy for reaching it. Note: it is a process, not a one-off activity.
The definition commits you toWhich rules out
Assessing implications for women and menAssuming a programme is neutral
Any planned actionConfining it to "gender projects"
All areas and all levelsDelegating it to one unit
Making it a dimension of designAdding it at the end
The wording is from ECOSOC's 1997 agreed conclusions, which is why it reads like a legal text rather than a slogan. Every clause was negotiated, and each closes off a common evasion.
Read the last clause carefully: gender is to be an integral dimension of design, implementation, monitoring and evaluation. Three of those four are where mainstreaming usually stops.

Not an Add-On
Integral, not an afterthought
Add-on thinking
  • 'Let's also do something for women'
  • A separate gender component or annex
  • A line in the budget at the end
  • Counted, then forgotten
Mainstreamed thinking
  • Gender shapes the core design
  • Asked at every stage and every level
  • Resourced from the start
  • Owned by everyone, not the 'gender person'
Mainstreaming means gender is everybody's business and built into the main work — not outsourced to a single specialist or a separate project.
Add-on tell-taleMainstreamed alternative
A gender annexGender in the problem statement
A women's componentGendered design of every component
A gender line at budget endGender costs inside each activity
Disaggregated reporting onlyDisaggregated targets from the start
A focal point who "does gender"Every manager accountable
A quick diagnostic on any proposal: delete the gender paragraph and see whether the rest of the document changes. If nothing else moves, gender was an annex.
The reverse test also works. If the gender analysis is real, it should have changed something concrete — a timing, a location, a delivery channel, a target.

Why Bother
Why neutral is rarely neutral
A 'gender-blind' programme is not neutral — it quietly assumes the user is a man with a man's time, mobility and assets. The default excludes whoever does not fit it.
Gender-blind
Ignoring gender as a variable — treating women and men as if their needs, constraints and opportunities were identical. Usually reinforces existing inequality by default.
Example: a toilet block with no separate, lockable facilities for women is technically 'sanitation for all' — and used by almost no women.
Design assumesExcludes whoever
The user can travel to a centreHas restricted mobility
The user is free mid-morningIs doing unpaid care work
The user holds the land titleFarms land in someone else's name
The user has a phone and an IDShares a household phone
The user can queue for hoursMust be home before dark
None of these assumptions is written down anywhere. They are inherited from the designer's own experience of using services, which is why they survive review by people with similar experience.
This is also why the fix is not sensitivity but data: ask who actually turned up, at what hour, from how far, and the assumptions become visible.

Two Goals
Equality of treatment and of outcome
Equality (sameness)
Treating everyone the same. Fair in principle, but identical inputs on an unequal starting line preserve the gap.
Equity (fairness)
Allocating according to need to reach a fair outcome. Different inputs where starting points differ — the route to real equality.
Mainstreaming uses equity (treating differently when needed) to achieve equality (a fair result for all).
Equality of treatmentEquity of outcome
RuleSame input for everyoneInput according to need
AssumesA level starting lineAn unequal starting line
Looks fairOn paperIn outcomes
ObjectionPreserves existing gapsAccused of preferential treatment
The legal frame in India accommodates both: Article 14 guarantees equality, and Article 15(3) explicitly permits special provision for women and children, which is what makes reservation and targeting constitutional.
CEDAW takes the same position: temporary special measures to accelerate equality are not discrimination under the Convention.

Mandate
It is policy, not charity
Gender mainstreaming is a binding commitment under CEDAW, the SDGs (Goal 5 and gender targets across all goals), and most national gender policies in South Asia. It is an obligation, not an optional kindness.
  • CEDAW — the women's rights treaty (ratified across the region)
  • SDG 5 — gender equality, with gender data cutting across all 17 goals
  • Beijing+ reviews — periodic national progress reporting
InstrumentWhat it obliges
CEDAWEliminate discrimination in law and practice; report periodically
Beijing PlatformAct across 12 critical areas; report at reviews
SDG 5 and gender targetsReport disaggregated indicators
National gender policyWhatever it specifies, usually mainstreaming
India ratified CEDAW in 1993 with declarations, and reports periodically to the CEDAW Committee. The Committee's concluding observations are public and specific, and are rarely used domestically as an advocacy tool.
Framing gender work as an obligation rather than a value has a practical advantage inside institutions: obligations get budget lines, values get statements.

Common Myths
What gender mainstreaming is not
  • Not just 'adding women' or counting female heads
  • Not a separate women's project run on the side
  • Not the job of one focal point while others opt out
  • Not anti-men — it analyses relations, and engages men too
  • Not a one-time activity — it is an ongoing process
Clearing these myths early saves years of well-meaning effort spent on the wrong thing. Mainstreaming changes the mainstream — that is the whole point.
The mythWhy it persists
"It means adding women"Headcounts are easy to report
"It is a separate project"Separate is easier to manage
"It is the focal point's job"It removes it from everyone else
"It is anti-men"It threatens an existing arrangement
"We did it last year"It treats analysis as a one-off
Each myth is a way of containing the work: making it small, making it someone else's, or making it finished. That is worth naming out loud, because the response differs for each.
The anti-men objection is worth engaging rather than dismissing. Gender analysis examines relations, and men and boys are inside the frame — as stakeholders, as allies, and as people harmed by rigid norms.

The Foundational Distinction
Sex is biological; gender is social
Sex
Biological characteristics — chromosomes, anatomy, reproductive function. Largely universal and relatively fixed.
Gender
Socially constructed roles, behaviours and expectations a society assigns to women, men and others. Varies by culture and changes over time.
Because gender is learned, not given, it can be unlearned and changed. That possibility is the whole premise of mainstreaming.
SexGender
SourceBiological characteristicsSocial assignment
Varies across societiesLittleGreatly
Changes over timeRarelyContinuously
Can be changed by policyNoYes — that is the point
The last row is the whole reason the distinction matters for practitioners. If gendered outcomes were biological they would be fixed; because they are social, they are a legitimate object of policy.
Data practice follows: surveys almost always collect sex, rarely gender identity. Where your data has a sex column, be accurate about what it measures and what it does not.

Roles
Gender roles and the gendered division of labour
Societies assign different work to women and men — the gendered division of labour. Caroline Moser's framework names three kinds of work women routinely do, often simultaneously.
Productive
Paid or income-generating work — farming, wages, enterprise
Reproductive
Unpaid care & domestic work — cooking, water, childcare
Community
Collective work — organising, ceremonies, local services
Kind of workPaid?Counted in GDP?
ProductiveUsuallyYes
Reproductive / careNoNo
Community managingNoNo
Community politicsSometimesPartly
Two of the three roles Moser identified are invisible to the national accounts, which is why time-use surveys matter: they are the only instrument that makes unpaid work countable.
India's Time Use Survey (2019, and repeated since) is the source to cite for unpaid work in the Indian context, and it shows a very large gap in time spent on unpaid domestic and care work.

Moser's Triple Role
The triple burden
Moser's insight: women typically carry a triple role — productive, reproductive and community work at once — while men's roles are more often single and sequential. Reproductive work is unpaid, unmeasured and assumed.
A 'time-neutral' programme that adds a meeting, a training or a worksite to a woman's day stacks it on top of an already full reproductive load. Mainstreaming asks: whose time are we spending?
Programme assumptionCost it imposes
Women will attend a daytime meetingDisplaces paid or care work
Women will maintain the assetUnpaid labour added
Women will form and run a groupMeeting time plus travel
Women will be the "community volunteer"A full role, unpaid
The triple role means a new activity does not slot into free time, because there is little. It displaces something else — usually rest, or a daughter's schooling.
The design question follows directly: what is this activity replacing in her day, and have we asked? A time-use question in the baseline answers it in one line.

Condition vs Position
Improving lives vs shifting power
Condition
A woman's material state — her workload, income, health, access to services. A new handpump improves her condition.
Position
Her social and economic standing relative to men — rights, status, decision-making power. Land in her name shifts her position.
You can improve condition without touching position. Lasting equality needs both — or the gains evaporate when the project ends.
InterventionImproves conditionShifts position
New handpumpYesNo
Cash transfer to her accountYesPartly
Land title in her nameYesYes
Seat on a panchayat with a budgetIndirectlyYes
Childcare at the worksiteYesEnables position change
Programmes overwhelmingly deliver in the first column, because condition improvements are visible, measurable within a project cycle and uncontroversial.
That is not a reason to stop delivering them. It is a reason to state honestly which column your programme is in, rather than describing a handpump as empowerment.

Empowerment
Empowerment is about power, not just access
Empowerment (Kabeer)
The expansion of people's ability to make strategic life choices in a context where this ability was previously denied to them — the process of gaining power to define and act on one's own goals.
Naila Kabeer frames empowerment as resources → agency → achievements: not just having things, but the capacity to use them to act, and the outcomes that follow.
Kabeer's three dimensionsWhat it means
ResourcesMaterial, human and social preconditions
AgencyThe ability to define goals and act on them
AchievementsThe outcomes that follow
The point of separating the three is that resources alone do not constitute empowerment. A woman may have an account she does not operate, a title to land she cannot sell, a vote cast by instruction.
Measurement follows the structure: measure the resource, the agency and the achievement separately, and report where the chain breaks. "Accounts opened" tells you about one link.

Intersectionality
Gender never travels alone
A woman is never only a woman. Caste, class, religion, disability, age, region and sexuality intersect to shape very different experiences. A Dalit woman farm-labourer faces compounded, not merely additive, disadvantage.
Treating 'women' as one uniform group hides the women most excluded. Mainstreaming must disaggregate within gender, not only across it.
AxisIn Indian data, visible as
CasteLarge gaps in outcomes between SC/ST and others
ClassWealth quintile gradients on nearly every indicator
DisabilityRarely collected, so mostly invisible
ReligionReported in NFHS and NSS
LocationRural-urban, and state-level variation
Crenshaw's term names a specific analytical claim: the disadvantages are not additive, so a Dalit woman's experience is not the sum of a separate caste effect and a separate gender effect.
Operationally this means cross-tabulating rather than controlling. Reporting a gender gap and a caste gap separately hides the intersection where the largest gap usually sits.

Beyond the Binary
Gender is not only women
Mainstreaming examines relations between women, men and gender-diverse people. It engages men and boys as stakeholders and allies, and recognises transgender and non-binary people — legally affirmed in India by the NALSA judgment (2014).
'Gender' is relational. Changing women's lives requires engaging the norms that govern everyone's behaviour.
Beyond a binary lensPractical implication
Men and boys as stakeholdersNorms work with men, not only about them
Transgender and non-binary peopleForms and data systems that admit a third option
Relations rather than categoriesAnalyse the household, not just the woman
NALSA v. Union of India (2014) recognised transgender persons as a third gender with the right to self-identification, and the Transgender Persons Act followed in 2019 — with substantial criticism from trans-rights groups over its provisions.
For programme systems the concrete step is small and often skipped: a data field that admits more than two values, and a plan for what to do with the third.

Harvard
The Harvard / Gender Roles Framework
One of the earliest (1985), the Harvard Analytical Framework is efficiency-oriented and data-driven. It maps activities by gender and the access to, and control over, resources and benefits.
  • Activity profile: who does what (productive / reproductive)
  • Access & control profile: who uses, who decides over resources
  • Influencing factors: what shapes these patterns
Strength: concrete and visible. Limit: it describes the division of labour but says little about why power is unequal.
Harvard profileQuestion it answers
Activity profileWho does what, when, where
Access and control profileWho uses, who decides
Influencing factorsWhat shapes the pattern
Project checklistWhat the design should change
Its strength is that it is quick, concrete and produces something a sceptical manager will read: a table. Its limitation is that it describes the division of labour without asking why it exists.
Use it as a first pass and do not stop there. A Harvard analysis that finds women do 80% of the water fetching will suggest a closer water point, not a discussion of why the work is theirs.

Moser
The Moser Framework
Caroline Moser's framework is explicitly transformative. It builds on the triple role and the practical / strategic needs distinction to ask whether a programme merely eases life or reshapes power.
  • Identifies the triple role in a given context
  • Distinguishes practical vs strategic needs
  • Assesses how policy approaches address (or ignore) each
  • Examines who controls resources and decisions in the household
Moser asksDesign consequence
Which of the triple roles does this add to?Budget for the time it takes
Practical or strategic need?Say which, and do not overclaim
Does it shift the division of labour?If not, say so
Who in the household gains?Target the transfer accordingly
Moser set out the framework in Gender Planning and Development (1993), as a planning methodology rather than a research tool — it was written to be used by the people designing programmes.
The demanding part is the honesty it requires. Applied properly it will tell you that most of your portfolio meets practical needs, which is a finding managers do not enjoy.

Kabeer
Kabeer's Social Relations Approach
Naila Kabeer's Social Relations Approach shifts the lens from women alone to the institutions that produce inequality — and how they reproduce it across the household, market, state and community.
4 institutions
Household, market, state, community — each carries gendered rules
5 dimensions
Rules, resources, people, activities, power — analysed in each institution
Its power: inequality is not a women's deficit to fix but an institutional pattern to change.
SRA institutionProduces inequality through

MarketOccupational segregation, wage gaps

The SRA's contribution is to move the analysis off the individual woman and onto the rules, resources, people, activities and power in each institution — which is where the pattern is actually produced.
It is the framework to reach for when the question is why a gap persists despite a programme, because it will point at an institution your programme does not touch.

What They Share
Four questions every framework asks
  • Who does what? — the gendered division of labour
  • Who has what? — access to resources and services
  • Who decides? — control over resources and choices
  • Who benefits? — who gains from the activity or programme
Memorise these four. They are the portable core of gender analysis — usable even without a formal framework in hand.
QuestionData that answers it
Who does what?Time-use, activity mapping
Who has what?Asset ownership, service use, disaggregated
Who decides?Agency questions in surveys; qualitative work
Who benefits?Outcome data disaggregated, plus who reports gain
The four questions are the portable version of the frameworks. A practitioner who asks only these of every design will catch most of what a formal framework would.
The third and fourth are the ones that get skipped, because they need either survey questions you did not include or conversations you did not budget for.

Definition
What gender analysis actually is
Gender analysis
The systematic examination of the different roles, needs, constraints, opportunities and power relations of women, men and gender-diverse people in a given context — and what a policy or programme will do to them.
It is the first step of mainstreaming, not a specialist luxury. Without it, 'gender-sensitive design' is a guess.
Gender analysis isGender analysis is not
Systematic examination of roles and powerA paragraph about women
Predictive about programme effectsA retrospective justification
Done before the design is fixedAttached to an approved design
Grounded in data and in voicesA consultant's impression
The timing point is the one that decides everything else. An analysis commissioned after the design is approved can only annotate it, and everyone involved knows that.
If you are asked for a gender analysis on a finished design, say what it can and cannot do. That sentence is more useful than the analysis.

Beyond Sex
Gender data is more than a column for sex
True gender data captures issues that disproportionately affect one gender (unpaid care, violence), reflects women's own realities, and is collected by methods that reach women — not just a tick-box added to a male respondent's form.
  • Disaggregate by sex and by caste, disability, age
  • Measure unpaid work, mobility, voice — not only income
  • Interview women directly, in private, in their language

Splits existing indicators by sexMeasures gendered issues
A columnA question set
Coverage, enrolment, useUnpaid care, violence, mobility, agency
NecessaryAlso necessary
The distinction matters because "we disaggregate by sex" is often given as a complete answer. It tells you the size of a gap in the things you already measure, and nothing about what you never asked.
Collection method is part of the definition. A survey administered to the household head, in the presence of the family, will not produce accurate data on violence or on decision-making.

Run any intervention through these four. The answers reveal where it will help, where it will miss, and where it might backfire.
QuestionA common wrong answer

Who decides?"Decisions are joint"

The Crucial Distinction
Access is not control
Access
The opportunity to use a resource. A woman may farm the land, draw from the well, attend the clinic.

Definition

Gender-responsive budgeting (GRB)
Not a separate budget for women, but the analysis and restructuring of the whole budget — revenue and expenditure — to assess and advance its impact on gender equality.
GRB asks: does this 'gender-neutral' road, subsidy or salary line actually serve women and men equally? Budgets are policy stated in money.

The Core Strategy
Two tracks, run together
Experience showed that mainstreaming alone can dilute into nothing, while women-only projects alone stay marginal. The twin-track approach runs both at once — and is now standard in EU, UN and most donor policy.

Integrate gender into every policy, programme and budget — everywhere, by everyone.
Track 2: Target

The balance shifts by context. Where gaps are wide and women's voice is weak, Track 2 may need to lead at first — building the agency that lets women claim space in the mainstream Track 1 then opens.
Think of Track 2 as building capability and Track 1 as opening opportunity. Empowerment without opportunity frustrates; opportunity without capability excludes.

Strong policy, weak deliveryTrack 1 with accountability
Backlash risingBoth, with do-no-harm foregrounded
The sequencing logic is that Track 2 builds the capability to claim space, and Track 1 creates the space to claim. Doing the second without the first produces open doors nobody walks through.
Whatever the mix, keep both alive. A portfolio with no Track 2 has nowhere to develop specialist capacity; one with no Track 1 leaves the bulk of the money untouched.

Increasingly, twin-track practice adds work with men and boys — on shared care, respectful relationships and challenging harmful masculinities — because women's gains stick only when norms around men shift too.

Gender Focal Points
The promise and trap of focal points
What they should do
Champion, advise, build capacity and keep gender visible — while the work stays everyone's responsibility.
The trap
Becoming the dumping ground — usually a junior woman, given the title but no authority, time or budget, while others opt out.
A focal point needs seniority, a mandate and resources — otherwise the role de-mainstreams gender by making it one person's burden.

Accountability
What gets measured gets done
  • Gender targets in organisational and staff performance plans
  • Mandatory disaggregated reporting on every programme
  • Gender markers tracked across the whole portfolio
  • Consequences and incentives tied to gender results
  • Periodic gender audits of the organisation itself

Gender Audit
Holding the organisation to its own mirror
Gender audit
A participatory self-assessment of how well an organisation has mainstreamed gender — in its policies, programmes, staffing, budget and culture — pioneered in the ILO's participatory model.
An audit turns the gender lens inward. An NGO preaching equality with an all-male senior team and no childcare has an internal gap to close before it can credibly mainstream outward.
Gender audit examinesTypical finding

Programme design and dataDisaggregation patchy
Staffing and payWomen concentrated in junior roles
Budget allocationNo line for gender work
Culture and meetingsCare responsibilities not accommodated
The ILO's participatory gender audit is the best-documented methodology, and its distinctive feature is that staff assess their own organisation rather than being assessed.

Culture
The hardest layer to change
Policies and structures are the easy part. Organisational culture — whose ideas get heard, who gets promoted, whether meetings respect care responsibilities, whether harassment is tolerated — is where mainstreaming is truly tested.
An organisation cannot transform gender relations in communities while reproducing them in its own corridors. Walk the talk, or the talk rings hollow.

Leadership
It rises or falls on leadership
Where leaders own gender as core business — not a side issue delegated to a focal point — mainstreaming takes root. Where they treat it as compliance, it withers into reporting.

An Honest Reckoning
A good idea, unevenly delivered
Three decades on, mainstreaming is near-universal in policy language and patchy in practice. Taking the critiques seriously is not betrayal of the agenda — it is how the agenda gets better.
The recurring problem is not the concept but the gap between the rhetoric of mainstreaming and the reality of programmes.
CritiqueWhat it targets
Policy evaporationThe gap between commitment and delivery
TokenismForm without substance
DepoliticisationThe technical turn
Co-optionLoss of the feminist frame
Capacity failurePractical under-resourcing
These are distinct diagnoses with distinct remedies, and they are often collapsed into a general complaint that mainstreaming has failed. Which one applies changes what you should do.
Two of them — evaporation and capacity — are fixable with resources and accountability. The other two are arguments about the frame itself, and cannot be fixed by better implementation.

Policy Evaporation
The classic critique: policy evaporation
Policy evaporation
Coined by Sara Hlupekile Longwe: good gender-equality commitments made at the top 'evaporate' as they pass down through the bureaucracy, so little survives into actual implementation.
Strong policy at headquarters; almost nothing on the ground. Longwe's image — commitment evaporating like water in heat — remains the sharpest indictment of weak mainstreaming.
Where commitments evaporateSymptom
Policy to strategyNot carried into the plan
Strategy to budgetNo allocation
Budget to workplanNobody's task
Workplan to deliveryDropped under time pressure
Delivery to reportingAggregated away
Longwe's term captures a specific mechanism rather than a general failure: each handover down the bureaucracy is a point at which the gender content can be lost without anyone deciding to drop it.
The counter is to name a person and a check at each handover. Evaporation happens where nothing is verified, not where someone objects.

Dilution
Tokenism and the checkbox
When 'mainstreaming' becomes a box to tick — a paragraph in a proposal, a women's photo in a report, a quota met on paper — the form survives but the substance drains away.
  • Gender 'addressed' by one line in the logframe
  • A women's activity bolted on, core design untouched
  • Disaggregated data collected but never acted upon
  • 'Gender' invoked, power never mentioned
Tokenism looks likeAsk instead
A gender paragraph in the proposalWhat did it change?
A photograph of womenWhat is the disaggregated data?
A quota met on paperWho spoke and who decided?
A gender indicator in the logframeIs it measured and reported?
The form survives because it is what gets checked. A donor template asks for a gender paragraph, so a gender paragraph is produced, and the review verifies its presence rather than its consequence.
Changing what is checked is therefore the intervention with the most leverage — and it sits with whoever designs the template, which may well be you.

Technical vs Transformative
The depoliticisation critique
The deepest critique: mainstreaming has been turned into a technical exercise — tools, checklists, markers — that quietly strips out its political core. Gender equality is about power; bureaucratising it can defang it.
Technical (thin)
Apply the tool, count the women, file the report. Comfortable, measurable — and toothless.
Transformative (thick)
Confront unequal power and challenge the norms that hold it. Uncomfortable, contested — and the actual point.
Technical turn givesAnd costs
Tools everyone can useThe analysis of power
Comparable markersThe question of whose interests
Checklists that scaleJudgement about context
Institutional acceptabilityThe political edge
The critique — associated with Sylvia Walby, Shahra Razavi, Aruna Rao and others — is not that tools are bad. It is that the tools made the agenda adoptable by institutions precisely by removing what made it contentious.
The practical response is to keep the power question explicitly in the tool. A checklist that asks "who decides" and "who loses" carries more of the politics than one that asks how many women attended.

Whose Agenda
Co-option and the loss of the feminist edge
Critics note that as 'gender' entered mainstream institutions, it was sometimes drained of its feminist politics — 'gender' replacing 'women', 'efficiency' replacing 'rights', the radical demand softened into managerial routine.
The question to keep asking: is this mainstreaming changing power, or just making existing systems look gender-friendly while leaving them intact?
Shift in languageWhat was lost
"Women" to "gender"The named beneficiary of the demand
"Rights" to "efficiency"The basis of the claim
"Movements" to "stakeholders"The source of pressure
"Patriarchy" to "gender relations"The name of the problem
The efficiency argument — that gender equality raises growth — is effective with finance ministries and carries a risk: an argument that rests on payoff can be defeated by evidence about payoff.
Rights-based and instrumental arguments can be used together, and it is worth knowing which one you are making and to whom, rather than sliding between them.

The Backlash
Resistance is real — and rising
Mainstreaming faces open and quiet resistance: 'gender is Western', 'our women are content', 'this divides the family'. At household level, women's gains can trigger backlash; at policy level, hard-won space can be rolled back.
Resistance is a sign the work is touching real power — not a reason to retreat. But it must be anticipated, navigated and managed, not wished away.
ResistanceResponse that works
"Gender is a Western import"Cite the domestic law and the local movement
"Our women are content"Ask the women, separately
"This divides the family"Engage men early; show shared gains
Quiet non-complianceMake it a checked requirement
The imported-agenda claim is answerable on the facts: India's constitutional guarantees, its women's movement, and its own legislation on dowry, domestic violence and workplace harassment are domestic in origin.
Quiet resistance is more common than loud, and more effective. It is defeated by verification rather than by argument.

Keeping mainstreaming honest

  • Resource and staff the work properly, or admit it is rhetoric

  • Treat critique as course-correction, not as the enemy

A Practitioner Checklist
Twelve questions for your next programme
  • Do I have sex-disaggregated baseline data?
  • Have I run the four questions — does, has, decides, benefits?
  • Did I separate practical from strategic needs?
  • Does the design touch control, not just access?
  • Are gender objectives explicit, with indicators and budget?
  • Will delivery actually reach women (time, place, staff)?
