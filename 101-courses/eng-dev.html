Why Communication Matters in Development

The Core Truth
Good work fails when it can't be explained
A brilliant programme that nobody can describe clearly will lose the grant, miss the policy window, and confuse the community it serves. In development, communication is not a soft add-on — it is how evidence travels, how money moves, and how people are heard.
Your impact is capped by your clearest sentence. If the reader does not understand it, the work might as well not exist.
Unclear writing costs youAt which moment
The grantA reviewer reading forty proposals
The policy windowA brief that arrives unreadable
Your team’s timeThree emails to clarify one
Community trustA meeting nobody could follow
Each of these is a real, dateable loss rather than an abstraction, and none of them is recorded anywhere as a communication failure.
That invisibility is why writing gets no budget and no training time. The cost is paid, and it is never attributed.

The Working Language
English as the sector's lingua franca
Across South Asia, development work runs on English — donor proposals, journal articles, MoUs, global conferences. It is the language that connects a field office in Bihar to a funder in Geneva. That gives English real reach — and real power.
This is a fact to use, not a hierarchy to accept. English opens doors; it should never be a gate that keeps good people out.
English opensEnglish closes
Donor funding and partnershipsParticipation by non-English staff
International publicationCommunity understanding
Regional and global networksLocal knowledge in other languages
Formal record and contractSpeed and nuance in the field
Both columns are true at once. Treating English as neutral infrastructure hides the second, and treating it as pure imposition ignores the first.
The practical response is bilingual practice: work in the language the work happens in, and translate deliberately for the audiences that need English.

Power & Politics
English carries power dynamics
  • It can exclude brilliant field staff who think in other languages
  • It can make jargon a gatekeeping ritual that signals status
  • It can privilege the articulate over the right
  • It can silence the communities whose lives the data describes
Good development communication uses English to include — plain, generous, translatable — never to perform expertise or to keep others out.
Exclusion mechanismCheap counter
Meetings held in EnglishInterpretation, or the local language
Jargon as a status signalBan acronyms in mixed rooms
The articulate carry the decisionWritten input as well as spoken
Reports only in EnglishA one-page local-language summary
The last row is the one most within your control and the least done: a one-page summary in the local language costs an afternoon and changes who can hold you to account.
Notice that fluency and being right are unrelated. A process that rewards the first will systematically discard some of the second.

Reframe
Writing in your second or third language
The hidden advantage
ESL writers often write plainer English than native speakers, because you reach for the simple, direct word. Plain English is exactly what the sector needs.
The mindset
You are not writing a literature exam. You are getting a job done. Clarity beats fluency. A short, correct sentence always beats a long, impressive, broken one.
Confidence comes from a method, not from vocabulary. This course gives you the method.
The worryThe reality
"My English is not good enough"Plain is the standard; ornate is the fault
"I should sound more academic"Academic prose is the sector’s worst writing
"Native speakers write better"They write longer, more often
"I need more vocabulary"You need fewer, plainer words
The advantage is real and specific: writers reaching for the simple word produce exactly what plain-language guidance asks for, without having to unlearn a habit.
What genuinely needs work is usually narrow — articles, tenses, a handful of prepositions — and Section 7 covers it.

The Plain-Language Revolution
CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 · ImpactMojo 101 Series11www.impactmojo.in

The Movement
Plain language is a global standard
The plain-language movement — from the UK's Plain English Campaign (founded 1979) to plain-writing laws in the US and international standards — holds a simple idea: a document works if its intended reader can find what they need, understand it, and use it, the first time.
Plain language
Writing the reader can understand the first time they read it. Not dumbed-down or childish — clear, direct and respectful of the reader's time.
Plain-language testWhich means
The reader finds what they needStructure and headings
They understand it first timeWords and sentence length
They can act on itA clear ask
This three-part test is the definition used across the plain-language standards, and it is worth noting that only the middle part is about words.
Most documents that fail do so on the first and third. The writing is comprehensible; the point is buried and the ask is missing.

The Principle
Clarity over complexity
If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out.
— George Orwell, “Politics and the English Language” (1946)
Complex writing rarely signals a complex mind. It usually signals that the writer has not finished thinking. Clear writing is the final, hardest draft — not the first.
Complex writing usually signalsNot
Unfinished thinkingA complex mind
Anxiety about authorityPrecision
Copying a templateRigour
Something being hiddenDepth
Orwell’s essay makes a stronger claim than a stylistic one: unclear prose is often used to make bad decisions sound reasonable.
The corollary is useful in practice. When a paragraph resists being made clear, the problem is usually the idea rather than the words.

The Trap
The curse of knowledge
The curse of knowledge
Once you understand something deeply, you forget what it is like not to understand it — so you skip the steps and the definitions the reader needs.
You know what ‘the intervention’, ‘the framework’ and ‘the modality’ mean. Your reader, opening cold, does not. Write for the reader you had a year ago.
You assume the reader knowsThey may not
What "the intervention" refers toWhich of the four
What the acronym stands forAny of them
Why the baseline mattersWhat a baseline is
The programme’s historyAnything before this year
The curse of knowledge is not carelessness; it is the direct consequence of expertise, which makes it invisible from the inside and impossible to self-diagnose.
The only reliable fix is an outside reader. Give the draft to someone who does not work on the programme and ask them what it is asking for.

Readability
What makes writing readable
  • Short sentences — one main idea each
  • Short, common words — the word you would say out loud
  • Active voice — say who does what
  • Concrete nouns — ‘farmers’, not ‘stakeholders’
  • Clear structure — headings and white space
Readability is not about a low reading age. It is about respecting a busy reader who has forty other documents to get through.
FeatureDo thisNot this
SentenceOne main ideaThree clauses and a list
WordsThe one you would sayThe impressive one
VoiceSay who did whatIt was decided
NounsFarmers, nurses, childrenStakeholders, beneficiaries
LayoutHeadings and white spaceA wall of text
Readability formulas measure only the first two rows, which is why a document can score well and still be unreadable in practice.
Layout does more work than any sentence-level edit. A reader who cannot see the structure will not discover the clarity inside it.

Sentence Length
Aim for short, vary the rhythm
A useful target for professional writing: an average of 15–20 words a sentence. Not every sentence — an average. Mix short punchy lines with the occasional longer one for rhythm.
Sentence lengthReader experience
Under 15 wordsEasy — crisp and clear
15–25 wordsFine if well structured
Over 30 wordsReader gets lost; re-reads or gives up
Over 40 wordsAlmost always two or three sentences hiding as one
Average sentence lengthReader experience
Under 12 wordsChoppy if sustained
15-20 wordsThe working target
25-30 wordsEffortful
Over 35 wordsReread required
It is an average, not a limit. A page of uniformly short sentences reads as a list; the rhythm comes from varying around the target.
The practical technique: find your longest sentence on the page and split it. Do that once per page and the average takes care of itself.

Active Voice
Active voice: say who does what
Passive (vague)Active (clear)
It was decided that funds would be reallocated.The board reallocated the funds.
Mistakes were made in the survey.Our field team mis-coded 40 forms.
The training was attended by 60 women.Sixty women attended the training.
Active voice names the actor. It is shorter, clearer and more honest — passive voice is often used to hide who did what.
Passive hidesWhich matters when
Who decidedAccountability is the question
Who made the errorA report should be honest
Who must actYou are asking for something
Who is responsible nextFollow-up depends on it
Bureaucratic passive is often not accidental. "Mistakes were made" is a construction chosen precisely because it has no subject.
The test: read the sentence and ask who did this. If the sentence cannot answer, decide whether that omission is deliberate and defensible.

When Passive Is Fine
Passive has its place
Passive voice is not a crime. Use it deliberately when the doer is unknown, unimportant, or obvious — or when you want to keep the focus on the thing acted upon.
  • “The wells were contaminated” — the cause is what matters, not who
  • “Samples were collected weekly” — in a methods section, fine
  • Default to active; choose passive on purpose, not by habit
Use passive whenExample
The doer is unknownThe wells were contaminated
The doer is obviousThe forms were collected
The object is the pointForty households were displaced
You are protecting someoneDeliberately, and knowingly
The advice is to use it deliberately, not to avoid it. Blanket passive-avoidance produces its own awkwardness and sometimes misplaces the emphasis.
The fourth row is legitimate and should be a conscious choice. Naming an individual in a public report can expose them.

Plain language in action
Before

After

26 words become 12, and the meaning finally appears. The plain version says something; the original only sounds like it does.

The Problem
NGO-speak: a dialect nobody loves
Every sector grows its own jargon, and development has a famously thick one. Used inside the team, shorthand saves time. Used with a donor, a journalist or a community, it becomes a fog that hides meaning and excludes outsiders.
Test: would the woman in your case study recognise the words you use to describe her life? If not, rewrite.
Jargon withEffect
Your own teamUseful shorthand
A donorSometimes expected, often noise
A government officerTheir jargon differs from yours
A journalistThey will paraphrase, possibly wrongly
A communityA wall
Jargon is not always wrong. It is precise and efficient among people who share it, which is exactly why it is invisible to the people who use it most.
The third row is worth noting: sector jargon and government jargon are different dialects, and using yours in a government meeting signals outsider rather than expert.

Acronym Soup
Drowning in abbreviations
“The CSO submitted the DPR to the PMU for the WASH intervention under the CSR-funded SHG programme.” A reader outside your office is now completely lost.
  • Spell out every acronym on first use — every document
  • If you use a term once, do not abbreviate it at all
  • Cut acronyms the reader does not need; keep only the load-bearing ones
  • A page with five acronyms a sentence is a page nobody finishes
Acronym ruleWhy
Spell out on first use, every documentReaders do not read in order
More than five in a document, add a listNobody holds five in memory
Never in a title or subject lineIt is the one line everyone reads
Never in community materialsThey are a pure barrier
"Every document" is deliberate. Writers spell out an acronym once in a series and then assume it, and each document has readers who saw none of the others.
Read the example sentence aloud to someone outside your office. The blankness on their face is the fastest possible training in this.

Nominalisation
Nominalisation: verbs hiding as nouns
Nominalisation
Turning a strong verb into a heavy noun — ‘implement’ becomes ‘implementation’, ‘decide’ becomes ‘decision-making’. It drains energy and adds words.
Nominalised (heavy)Verb (light)
We undertook the implementation of the plan.We carried out the plan.
The utilisation of resources was poor.We used resources poorly.
A decision was reached regarding closure.We decided to close it.
NominalisedVerbWords saved
We undertook an implementation ofWe implemented3
The utilisation ofUsing2
Provision of support was madeWe supported3
Decision-making processesDeciding2
Nominalisation drains the energy from a sentence because it removes the actor along with the verb: a process has no subject.
The quick scan: circle every word ending in -ation, -ment, -ance and -ity. Most can become verbs, and each conversion shortens the sentence.

Empty intensifiers & filler
  • “In order to” → to
  • “Due to the fact that” → because

The First Question
Who is reading, and what do they want?
Before writing a single line, name your reader. A donor, a village committee, a government officer and a journalist want different things from the same project — and reward completely different writing.
There is no ‘good writing’ in the abstract — only writing that is right for this reader, doing this job.
Before writing, nameAnd write it down
The single readerNot "stakeholders"
What they want from thisIn one line
What they will do nextThe action
What would lose themThe jargon, the length
Writing the reader down at the top of the draft, then deleting it before sending, is a two-minute habit that changes the whole document.
Where there are genuinely several readers, write for the primary one and add a short section for the others rather than averaging the register.

Register
Register: matching the formality
Register
The level of formality and tone you choose for a reader and a situation — from a quick WhatsApp to a formal MoU. Getting it wrong feels jarring.
Formal
Proposals, MoUs, policy briefs, journal articles. Full sentences, no slang, careful claims.
Informal
Team chat, quick updates, field notes. Warm, brief, direct. Still clear and respectful.
RegisterUse forMarker
FormalProposals, MoUs, policy briefsFull forms, no contractions
ProfessionalMost email and reportsContractions fine; plain words
InformalTeam chat, field coordinationShort, fast
Getting register wrong is jarring in both directions: an informal note to a ministry reads as careless, and a formal one to a colleague reads as displeasure.
When unsure, match the register of the message you are replying to. It is a reliable default and costs no thought.

Tone
Tone is how the reader feels reading you
Tone is separate from formality. You can be formal and warm, or informal and cold. Development writing should usually be confident, honest and human — never boastful, never apologetic, never bureaucratic for its own sake.
  • Confident: state findings plainly, own the limits
  • Honest: report what failed, not only what worked
  • Human: there are people behind every number
Aim forAvoid
ConfidentBoastful
Honest about limitsApologetic
HumanBureaucratic
DirectBlunt
Formality and warmth are independent axes. A formal letter can be warm, and an informal message can be cold, which is why "be less formal" is rarely the right advice.
Where you have bad news, be direct and warm at once: say it plainly in the first line, then explain. Burying it reads as evasion.

Reader's Time
Respect is measured in minutes
Assume your reader is overworked and skim-reading on a phone between meetings. Front-load the point. Use headings. Keep paragraphs short. Make it scannable.
If a busy reader can grasp your main message from the headings and first lines alone, you have done your job.

OverworkedFront-load the point
On a phoneShort paragraphs
SkimmingHeadings that state content
Between meetingsOne ask, clearly marked
Writing so that the headings alone carry the message is the single most useful structural discipline, because that is what a busy reader actually reads.
Test it: print the document, read only the headings and the first line of each paragraph. If the message survives, the structure works.

The Skill
Turn statistics into sentences people feel
Numbers do not speak for themselves; you translate them. The skill is to carry a statistic into a plain sentence that a non-specialist reader can picture — without distorting it.

Reader-Friendly Numbers
Round, compare, anchor
  • Round for prose: ‘about a third’ beats ‘32.7%’
  • Compare: ‘twice the state average’ means more than a bare number
  • Anchor: ‘1 in 5 children’ is more vivid than ‘20%’
  • One number at a time — never a sentence stuffed with five
In a report table, keep the precise figure. In a sentence, give the reader a number they can hold in their head.
TechniqueExample

Compare"Twice the state average"
Anchor"1 in 5 children", not 20%

Anchoring works because readers picture people rather than percentages. "One in five children" is imaginable; twenty per cent is arithmetic.

False Precision
Don't fake an accuracy you don't have
Writing ‘47.3% of beneficiaries’ from a sample of 60 people pretends to a precision the data cannot support. The decimal is false precision — it misleads by looking exact.
False precision
“47.3% of the 60 respondents…” — that is about 28 people. The decimal is noise.
Honest
“Roughly half (28 of 60) respondents…” — precise about the count, honest about the share.
Sample sizeDefensible precision
60 respondentsWhole numbers, or "about half"
600Whole percentage points
6,000One decimal, sometimes
Administrative censusAs recorded
A decimal point is a claim about precision. Writing 47.3% from sixty people asserts an accuracy of one part in a thousand from a sample of sixty.
The reader cannot tell the difference between a well-founded decimal and a spurious one, which is why the burden sits with the writer.

Honest Qualifiers
The words that keep you honest
Good data writing carries its own uncertainty. Qualifiers are not weakness — they are accuracy. They tell the reader how far to trust the number.
  • ‘about’, ‘roughly’, ‘around’ — for estimates
  • ‘in this sample’, ‘in these 5 villages’ — scope of the claim
  • ‘suggests’, ‘is associated with’ — not ‘proves’ or ‘causes’
  • ‘preliminary’, ‘early data’ — when the work is unfinished
QualifierSignals
About, roughly, aroundThis is an estimate
In this sampleNot necessarily the population
At leastUnder-reporting is expected
Appears to, is associated withNot established as causal
IllustrativeNot from a specific study
Qualifiers are accuracy rather than hedging, and the distinction is whether they are specific. "Roughly a third" is precise about its imprecision; "somewhat significant" is not.
Stacked hedges cancel out. "May possibly indicate a potential trend" tells a reader nothing and reads as evasion.

Don't let language claim causation
Overclaims causeHonest wording

Unless you ran a proper evaluation with a comparison group, write ‘is linked with’ or ‘followed’ — not ‘caused’. The verb is where overclaiming hides.
Over-claims causeHonest wording

The causal verb is where the claim inflates: raised, reduced, cut. Each asserts a counterfactual the data usually cannot support.
Say what you observed and, separately, what you think explains it. A reader can accept the first while disputing the second, which is a stronger position than blending them.

Sourcing
Always name the source and the date
  • Name the source: ‘(NFHS-5, 2019–21)’, not ‘studies show’
  • Give the date — a 2011 figure may be a different country now
  • State the denominator: per 1,000? of eligible children?
  • Flag your own estimates as illustrative or approximate
A sourced number is trusted; an unsourced one invites doubt about everything around it. Citation is credibility.
Sourcing elementWithout it
The source name"Studies show" is unverifiable
The yearA 2011 figure may describe another country
The denominatorThe number is uninterpretable
Whether it is your own estimateIt reads as official
"Studies show" is the weakest phrase in evidence writing. It names nothing, can be checked by nobody, and is used most where the evidence is thinnest.
Flag your own estimates explicitly as illustrative. A number that turns out to be invented does more damage than a gap would have.

Why Stories
A story carries what a number cannot
Numbers prove scale; stories create understanding. A single, well-told human story lets a reader feel why the work matters — and people act on what they feel. The best communication pairs the two.
The death of one person is a tragedy; the death of millions is a statistic.
— a grim adage on why one story moves us more than a number

Pairing them is the standard advice and the sequencing matters: the story makes the reader care, and the number establishes that the case is not exceptional.

The Red Line

Imagery or writing that exaggerates suffering to provoke pity and donations, stripping people of dignity and agency and reducing them to their hardship.

Consent
Consent in storytelling is non-negotiable
  • Ask permission to tell the story — and to use the name and photo
  • Explain where it will appear and for how long

  • Take special care with children, survivors and vulnerable groups

Non-Extractive

Extractive storytelling takes a community's pain, packages it for distant audiences, and gives nothing back. Non-extractive practice shares power: people help shape how they are shown.

  • Show the story to the community, in a form they can use

Speaking Counts Too
You will be heard as much as read
Communication is not only on the page. You will pitch to funders, brief officials, present at workshops and answer hard questions live. The same principles apply: know your audience, lead with the point, keep it plain.
A clear speaker who pauses and breathes always beats a fast one who never stops. Slow is clear; clear is confident.

Handling Q&A
Answering questions under pressure
  • Pause before answering — a breath is not weakness
  • Restate the question briefly — buys time, confirms you heard it
  • Answer the question asked; if you don't know, say so and offer to follow up
  • Keep answers short; a long answer hides the point

Orwell's Six Rules
Orwell's six rules for clear writing
  • Never use a metaphor, simile or figure of speech you are used to seeing in print
  • Never use a long word where a short one will do
  • If it is possible to cut a word out, cut it out
  • Never use the passive where you can use the active
  • Never use a foreign phrase or jargon word if there is an everyday English equivalent
  • Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous
From ‘Politics and the English Language’ (1946). Eighty years on, still the best one-page writing course.

The sixth rule is the one that keeps the list usable: it explicitly subordinates the rules to whether the sentence works.
