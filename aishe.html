What is AISHE — and why it matters

Every year, every degree-granting institution in India reports its students, teachers and programmes to AISHE. Its headline number is the Gross Enrolment Ratio (GER) — enrolment in higher education as a share of the 18–23 population. GER is where the whole learning pipeline ends up: a child who can't read a Std II text (ASER) in a school with no library (UDISE+) rarely reaches college at all. Two facts stand out — India's GER is still barely above a quarter of young people, and it varies enormously by state; yet women now enrol at a higher rate than men, one of the quietest big changes in Indian society.

Making sense of the numbers

What GER measures. The Gross Enrolment Ratio is total higher-education enrolment divided by the population aged 18–23, as a percentage. "Gross" means it counts everyone enrolled regardless of age, so a state that draws students from elsewhere (or has many older learners) can post a very high GER — read it as reach, not a pass rate. The Gender Parity Index (GPI) is women's GER divided by men's: 1.0 is parity, above 1 means women enrol at a higher rate.

Reading it responsibly. A high state GER (Tamil Nadu, Puducherry, the hill and southern states) reflects a dense network of colleges and strong school-completion; a low one (Bihar, and much of the north and north-east) reflects where the pipeline leaks earliest. Women's GER now exceeds men's nationally and in most states — a real and important shift — but GER says nothing about the quality or field of that education, or whether it leads to work. Treat it as the door, not the room.

Note on years & comparability. State GER is from AISHE 2023-24 (the latest edition). The national GER and GPI time-series (2011-12 to 2021-22) are published on the 2011-census population-projection basis; AISHE 2023-24 revised its denominators, so its all-India GER (27.8%) is not directly comparable to the older series (28.4% in 2021-22).
