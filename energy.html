What this shows, and what it can't

This explorer sits on the supply and resource side of India's energy account: what each state could produce from renewables, what it has installed, its total capacity and its coal reserves, and a derived view of how much of its renewable potential each state has actually built. Read together, they tell the transition story. Some states have high potential but have built little, and some sit on huge coal reserves that make moving away harder. What this report does not break down by state is the demand side: electricity consumption, per-capita use and village electrification are published as national figures only (India reached 100% village electrification by March 2022). So there is no "per-capita power by state" here; those national trends are shown on their own, clearly labelled, and where two official sources measure the same thing differently, the explorer says so.

  • State figures are a snapshot rather than a trend, because this source gives one year by state. Only the national series run year on year.

Making sense of the numbers

What the state measures mean. Renewable potential is the maximum capacity a state could install across wind, solar, small hydro, biomass and bagasse cogeneration plus large hydro, as estimated by MNRE. It is a ceiling and not a plan. Installed renewable capacity is grid-interactive renewables actually connected today (solar, wind, bio-power, small hydro, waste-to-energy: it excludes large hydro). Total installed capacity is all utility generation in the state: thermal, hydro, nuclear and renewables. Coal reserves are the geological estimate in the ground (proved + indicated + inferred), not what is mined. % of potential built divides installed renewables by assessed potential; because potential includes large hydro while installed does not, read it as an indicative build-out ratio, not an exact percentage. In Chandigarh installed renewables (79 MW) exceed the assessed potential (23 MW), so its ratio is above 100%.

The potential estimate was revised, so do not compare it with last year's. The 2026 edition puts India's renewable potential at 47,04,043 MW as on 31 March 2025, against 21,09,655 MW in the 2025 edition. Most of the change is solar: 33,43,378 MW against 7,48,990 MW. State figures moved too; Rajasthan's rose from 4,28,322 MW to 11,14,794 MW and Jammu & Kashmir's (with Ladakh) fell from 1,25,813 MW to 31,908 MW. A state whose "% built" fell between the two editions may have built more, because the denominator grew. Nationally, installed renewables rose from 1,43,645 MW to 1,72,368 MW in the year.

Reading it responsibly. A big-potential state that has built little (Rajasthan, Maharashtra) is not "behind" in any moral sense. Potential reflects land, wind and sun, while build-out reflects investment, grid access and policy. Coal-rich states (Odisha, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh) face a harder transition because coal is also jobs and state revenue. Total capacity by state excludes inter-state central-sector stations (106.37 GW of the 475.21 GW of utility capacity, a little over a fifth) that aren't allocated to a single state, so the state figures sum to less than the all-India total shown.

Two official numbers, both shown. India's per-capita electricity use here is 1,153 kWh (2024-25, provisional): MoSPI's figure, which divides final electricity consumption by population. The Central Electricity Authority publishes a higher per-capita number (1,460 kWh for 2024-25) because it divides gross consumption, including transmission-and-distribution losses, by population. Same country, two valid methods; this explorer uses MoSPI's series and flags the difference rather than hiding it. The national trends also show the real tension of the transition. Capacity, generation and the renewable share all climbed, and coal production kept rising (to a provisional 1,047.52 MT in 2024-25), growth and greening at once.
