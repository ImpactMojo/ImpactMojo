Analysing Indian survey data: weights, design and the errors that matter

NFHS, PLFS and HCES are sample surveys, and every figure you take from their unit-level files depends on three things beyond the data itself. These are the weight each record carries, the strata and primary sampling units the sample was drawn from, and the rules each survey sets for its own variables. This course works through all three in R and Python side by side, running in your browser. The files it uses are synthetic, built to show the method, so their numbers say nothing about India.

How the live code works. Each cell has an R tab and a Python tab. The first Run downloads the language engine once (R about 7 MB, Python about 10 MB). The R cells that set a survey design install the survey package the first time, and one Python cell loads scipy, so those take longer. The working folder already holds three files, survey_hh.csv, dhs_kids.csv and plfs_persons.csv. All three are synthetic. They copy the shape and the variable names of the real files so that the code carries over, but every value was generated for this page and none of them describes any place or person in India. The real unit-level files come with terms of use from the DHS Program and from MoSPI, and you request them yourself.
Module 1 of 6

Why survey estimates need weights and design

A national survey does not interview a simple random sample of households. It divides the country into strata (in Indian surveys, usually rural and urban parts of each state or district), draws a small number of primary sampling units (PSUs, which are villages or urban blocks) in each stratum, and then interviews a fixed number of households in each PSU. Two things follow from that. Some households had a much higher chance of being picked than others, so each record carries a weight, the number of households in the population it stands for. And households in the same village resemble each other, so 12 interviews in one village carry less information than 12 interviews in 12 villages.

These files are synthetic. This course uses survey_hh.csv, a household file shaped like an HCES file. It holds 720 households in 60 PSUs across 6 strata, with a weight, household size, social group, MPCE and an LPG indicator. Synthetic data The values were generated for this page and mean nothing about India.

The design of this file oversamples urban households. The next cell compares each sector's share of the interviews with its share of the households the weights say it represents.

Urban households are half of the interviews and about a fifth of the population. Any figure that ignores the weights treats them as half the population. The cost shows in the numbers.

Both the unweighted share of households with LPG and the unweighted mean MPCE are higher than the weighted ones, because the richer, oversampled urban households pull them up. The weighted figures are the estimates of the population. The unweighted ones describe the 720 households interviewed and nothing else.

The design changes the standard error too

Weights correct the point estimate. The standard error needs the design as well. An ordinary formula such as sd(x) / sqrt(n) assumes 720 independent draws. The real sample is 60 villages and blocks, and households in each are alike, so the true uncertainty is larger. R's survey package takes the design in svydesign(). The Python tab writes the same calculation out with numpy and pandas, so you can see what it does.

The design-based standard error is more than twice the naive one. A confidence interval built on the naive figure would be less than half as wide as it should be, and a difference between two groups that looks significant may not be. nest = TRUE tells R that PSU numbers are unique only within a stratum, which is the safe setting for Indian files.

Python and R agree here to every printed digit. Both use the same method, Taylor linearisation with PSUs treated as drawn with replacement within strata.
Module 2 of 6

The weight and the anthropometry flags in NFHS and DHS files

The National Family Health Survey is India's Demographic and Health Survey. The DHS Program distributes NFHS-5 (2019-21) as a standard DHS survey in its DHS-7 recode structure, so its files use the standard DHS variable names, and the DHS Program's Guide to DHS Statistics is the reference for how each indicator is computed.

Three rules from the Guide matter on almost every analysis. The weight is v005 in the women's file, mv005 in the men's and hv005 in the household and household-member files, and it must be divided by 1,000,000, because the weights are stored without the decimal point. The PSU is usually v021 (hv021) and the stratum usually v023 (hv023), though the Guide warns that stratification was not defined consistently across surveys and should be checked against Appendix A of the survey report. And the z-scores for child anthropometry, hc70 for height-for-age, are stored as the z-score times 100, with values of 9990 and above used as flags for implausible or incomplete measurements.

These files are synthetic. dhs_kids.csv is shaped like the child rows of a DHS household-member (PR) file. It has cluster, stratum, residence, weight, whether the child slept in the household the night before, age in months, sex and hc70. Synthetic data It holds 397 invented children, and the flag values were scattered at random to show the error.

For a mean or a percentage, dividing the weight by 1,000,000 changes nothing, because the scale cancels. It matters for anything that counts, such as a frequency weight in software that reads the weight as a number of cases. The Guide divides it, and so should you.

Drop the flags before dividing by 100

The Guide defines stunting as a height-for-age z-score below -2 standard deviations from the median of the WHO Child Growth Standards, hc70 < -200. Its denominator is de facto children aged 0 to 59 months (hv103 == 1) with a valid, non-flagged score, hc70 < 9990. The order of operations is the trap. Divide hc70 by 100 first and a flag of 9996 becomes a z-score of 99.96. That child is counted as not stunted, so every flag adds one to the denominator and nothing to the numerator, and the stunting rate falls. The mean z-score is worse, because each flag adds a value near 100.

In this synthetic file about a tenth of children carry a flag, more than you should expect in a real survey, so the error is large on purpose. In a real file it is smaller and harder to see. The mean z-score is the giveaway, since a mean height-for-age above zero in an Indian sample would be a very strange result.

With the design

The Guide's own R example sets the design with svydesign() from the survey package. The children who fail the filter are not removed from the data. They are left out of the estimate with subset() on the design, which keeps every cluster in the variance calculation. Module 5 shows why that matters.

Module 3 of 6

The PLFS combined weight, usual status and current weekly status

Until December 2024 the Periodic Labour Force Survey drew its first-stage units (FSUs) in two independent sub-samples. Each sub-sample on its own gives an estimate of the whole population, and the unit-level file carries a multiplier for each record calculated for its own sub-sample. The NSO's README for the calendar-year 2022 files names three variables. NSS is the number of FSUs surveyed in the record's sub-sample within its stratum and sub-stratum, NSC is the number surveyed in both sub-samples together, and MLTS is the multiplier. For a combined estimate from both sub-samples the README sets the final weight to MLTS/100 where NSS = NSC and MLTS/200 otherwise.

The reason for the two cases is that a stratum with FSUs in both sub-samples has two estimates of its total, which must be averaged, hence 200. A stratum where only one sub-sample was surveyed has one estimate, and its multiplier is used whole, hence 100. Applying MLT/100 to every record counts most of the population twice.

Check which release you have. The README's exact variable name and rule differ between releases (quarterly files use a different divisor), so read the README shipped with your files. From January 2025 PLFS changed its design. MoSPI's release of the PLFS Annual Report 2025 says FSUs are now drawn "with one FSU drawn per stratum/sub-stratum each month and no provision for independent sub-samples", so the two-case rule above belongs to files up to 2024. Take the weight for 2025 files from their own README.
These files are synthetic. plfs_persons.csv is shaped like a pre-2025 PLFS person file, restricted to persons aged 15 and over, in 4 strata. Stratum 4 has FSUs in one sub-sample only, so its records have nss == nsc. Synthetic data The sex codes (1 male, 2 female) and status codes follow the NSS conventions, but every value was generated for this page.

With MLT/100 everywhere the three strata that have both sub-samples double, and the population total is close to twice the right one. Ratios such as the unemployment rate move less, because the error partly cancels between numerator and denominator, which is exactly why it goes unnoticed. Any total, such as the number of workers, is wrong by nearly a factor of two.

Usual status and current weekly status

PLFS classifies each person's activity in two ways. The usual status uses the 365 days before the survey. The principal status (ps) is the activity on which the person spent relatively long time in that year, and the subsidiary status (ss) records economic activity pursued for 30 days or more in addition to the principal one. A person counts as a worker in usual status (ps+ss) if either is work. The current weekly status (CWS) uses the 7 days before the survey. CWS figures are lower for work done in season, because a person who works for part of the year may not have worked in the reference week.

The three rates are defined by MoSPI as follows. The labour force participation rate (LFPR) is the percentage of persons in the labour force, meaning working or seeking or available for work, in the population. The worker population ratio (WPR) is the percentage of employed persons in the population. The unemployment rate (UR) is the percentage of persons unemployed among those in the labour force. The UR's denominator is the labour force, which is smaller than the population, so a UR and an LFPR are shares of different bases and cannot be added.

The status codes follow the NSS scheme. Codes 11 to 51 are kinds of work (own-account work, employer, unpaid helper in a household enterprise, regular wage or salaried work, casual labour). Codes 61 to 72 are people who had work but did not work in the reference period. Code 81 is seeking or available for work, with 82 added in the weekly status for those available but not seeking. Codes 91 to 99 are outside the labour force (education, domestic duties, rentiers and pensioners, disability, young children and others).

In this file LFPR and WPR are lower in CWS than in usual status, because the synthetic data has some people who work during the year but did not work in the reference week. Always say which status a figure uses. PLFS publishes both, and MoSPI's headline annual figures are in usual status (ps+ss) for persons aged 15 and over.

Module 4 of 6

MPCE in HCES 2023-24, the two sectors and imputed free items

The Household Consumption Expenditure Survey measures what households consume. Its main indicator is monthly per capita consumption expenditure (MPCE), a household's consumption in a month divided by its size. MoSPI's press release of 30 January 2025 on HCES 2023-24 (August 2023 to July 2024, 2,61,953 households) puts average MPCE at ₹4,122 in rural India and ₹6,996 in urban India. It reports every figure for rural and urban India separately and gives no single figure for the two together, and the same is true of its state tables.

The sectors are separate by construction. The microdata portal's study description says the rural sample is drawn from a frame of Census 2011 villages and the urban sample from a frame of Urban Frame Survey blocks, with sub-units formed by different rules in each. Rural and urban are estimated as two populations. A pooled all-India average can be computed from the unit-level data with the weights, but it is your own calculation. MoSPI has published no such figure, so label it as yours and never set it beside the published rural and urban numbers as if it were one of them.

With and without imputation of free items

HCES 2022-23 and 2023-24 publish two sets of MPCE. The first leaves out the value of items households received free through welfare programmes. The second imputes a value for them. The imputed items are named in the release, among them rice, wheat and atta, millets, pulses, salt, sugar and edible oil, and laptops, tablets, mobile handsets, bicycles, motorcycles, school uniforms and school shoes. Free health care such as PM-JAY and fee waivers in education were not imputed. With imputation, average MPCE in 2023-24 is ₹4,247 in rural India and ₹7,078 in urban India. The release reports the first set in its main section and the second after it. Whichever you use, say so, and compare like with like across years.

These files are synthetic. The MPCE columns in survey_hh.csv (mpce without free items, mpce_free_items with them) were generated for this page. Synthetic data Their averages are not the published figures above and should not be compared with them.

Per person or per household

An average across persons gives each household its weight times its size. An average across households uses the weight alone. Because larger households tend to have lower MPCE, the two differ. Find out which one a published table reports before you try to match it.

Both sectors are reported side by side, never as one number. Standard errors by sector come from the design in the same way as before, with each sector as a domain.

Module 5 of 6

Design-based standard errors, domains and design effects

Every estimate in the earlier modules has a design-based standard error behind it. This module sets out the three tools you need to report one properly.

These files are synthetic. The cells below use survey_hh.csv again. Synthetic data Its numbers are invented and mean nothing about India.

Confidence intervals and design effects

The design effect (DEff) is the variance of an estimate under the real design divided by the variance a simple random sample of the same size would have given. A DEff of 4 means the 720 households carry as much information as 180 drawn at random. That smaller number is the effective sample size. For a confidence interval, R's confint() uses the normal distribution by default. With few PSUs a t distribution on the design's degrees of freedom, the number of PSUs minus the number of strata, is more honest. deff = "replace" compares against simple random sampling with replacement, the right choice when the weights are relative, as DHS weights are.

Subgroups are domains, so do not cut the data first

To estimate for a subgroup, such as Scheduled Tribe households, the tempting move is to keep only those rows and then declare the design. That throws away the PSUs where no ST household was interviewed. Those PSUs carry information. They tell you that this part of the sample contributed zero ST households, and the number of ST households in a PSU is itself random. Dropping them understates the standard error, and if a stratum is left with one PSU the variance cannot be computed at all. Declare the design on the whole file, then take the subgroup as a domain with subset() on the design or svyby() in R, or by giving rows outside the domain zero weight while keeping every PSU, as the Python function does.

The point estimate is the same both ways, and the standard error differs. The survey package's documentation includes a vignette, "Estimates in subpopulations", on exactly this.

Small domains. A standard error is itself an estimate, and with few PSUs in a domain it is unreliable. If a subgroup has very few households or appears in only a handful of PSUs, report that alongside the estimate, or do not report the estimate.
Module 6 of 6

A checklist of the errors that matter

Each item below is a way to get a wrong number from these files with no error message to warn you. Go through them before you report a figure from NFHS, PLFS or HCES unit-level data.

  1. Use the weight. An unweighted mean describes the people interviewed and no one else. Indian surveys do not give every household the same chance of selection. HCES 2023-24, for one, allotted every state and UT at least 40 FSUs whatever its population.
  2. Use the right weight. DHS and NFHS weights are v005/1000000 (or mv005, hv005). PLFS files up to 2024 use MLT/100 where NSS = NSC and MLT/200 otherwise, for combined estimates. Read the README that ships with your release.
  3. Use the right unit. MPCE per person weights households by weight times size. PLFS rates are per person. NFHS women's indicators come from the women's file.
  4. Drop anthropometry flags before dividing by 100. Valid scores are hc70 < 9990. Restrict to de facto children aged 0 to 59 months.
  5. Say which activity status. Usual status (ps+ss) and current weekly status give different LFPR, WPR and UR. The unemployment rate is a share of the labour force.
  6. Keep rural and urban HCES figures apart and say whether MPCE includes imputed free items. A pooled figure is your own calculation and must be labelled as one.
  7. Declare the design. Strata and PSUs, with nest = TRUE where PSU numbers repeat across strata. Check the stratum variable against the survey report.
  8. Estimate subgroups as domains. Never cut the file before setting the design.
  9. Report the uncertainty. Give design-based standard errors or confidence intervals, and the design effect where it helps a reader judge the sample.
  10. Do not compare across a design change without saying so. MoSPI states that PLFS annual estimates for 2025 may not be strictly comparable with earlier ones because the sampling design changed in January 2025.
  11. Do not share the microdata. The DHS Program and MoSPI each set terms for their files. Share your code and your aggregate results instead.
