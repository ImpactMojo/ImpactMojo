Tufte’s Principles: Quick Reference

A one-page checklist for diagnosing and redesigning the POSHAN Abhiyaan dashboard

How to use this. This is Document 3 of 3. Hold each principle below against the current dashboard and against the sample dataset. The principles come from Edward Tufte’s The Visual Display of Quantitative Information, framed here for monitoring dashboards.

1. Data–ink ratio: maximise it

Of all the ink on a chart, the share that encodes actual data should be as high as possible. Every pixel that is not data (gradients, 3D shading, drop shadows, heavy gridlines, boxed legends) competes with the numbers for attention.

AvoidFaux-3D bars, background fills, redundant borders, decorative colour.
PreferFlat marks, thin or no gridlines, direct labels, white space.

2. Eliminate chartjunk

Chartjunk is decoration that conveys no information: 3D effects, moiré patterns, rainbow palettes, and “ducks” (graphics that are mostly ornament). 3D bars in particular distort length comparisons because the depth and perspective add visual weight that is not in the data.

AvoidA 12-category 3D bar chart in 12 different colours.
PreferA flat, single-hue, sorted bar chart, or a dot plot.

3. Use the right chart for the data type

Match the encoding to the question:

You want to show…UseNot
Change over time (a trend)Line chartA pie chart per time point
Ranking / comparison across categoriesSorted bar or dot plot3D bars, rainbow bars
Part-to-whole at one momentA single bar or (sparingly) one pieMultiple pies to imply a trend
Many series across many unitsSmall multiples (see #4)One overloaded chart or a giant table
One headline numberA clearly labelled big number / KPIAn unlabelled gauge with no scale

A pie chart cannot show a trend. Slices encode shares of a whole, not values over time: readers cannot judge whether a quantity rose or fell from the angles of four separate slices.

4. Small multiples

To compare the same measure across 12 districts, repeat one small, identically-scaled chart in a grid: one tiny line or bar panel per district. The eye scans the grid and spots outliers instantly. This replaces both the cluttered 12-category chart and the 47-column table.

AvoidOne chart cramming all 12 districts, or a 47-column table.
PreferA 3×4 grid of identical mini-charts, shared axes.

5. Graphical integrity & the lie factor

The size of an effect shown in the graphic should match the size of the effect in the data.

lie factor = (size of effect shown in graphic) ÷ (size of effect in data)

A lie factor far from 1.0 signals distortion. Common culprits: a y-axis that does not start at a meaningful baseline, inconsistent scales between panels, area used to encode a single value, and, again, 3D perspective. Always label axes: a chart with no axis labels has an undefined scale and cannot be read honestly.

6. Accessible, meaningful colour

Roughly 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women have some form of colour-vision deficiency: most commonly red–green. A dashboard that encodes “good vs bad” only in red and green is unreadable for them and fails WCAG.

AvoidRed/green as the only status signal; rainbow categorical palettes.
PreferColourblind-safe palettes (e.g. blue–orange), plus a second cue (an icon, label, shape, or position) so meaning never rests on hue alone. Check contrast (WCAG AA: 4.5:1 for text).

7. Information hierarchy & layout

A dashboard is read top-left first. Lead with the few decision-relevant headline metrics, then supporting detail, then the full data on demand. Don’t give a raw 47-column table equal billing with the headline KPIs, and don’t make users scroll horizontally to find a number. Provide sorting, filtering, and consistent number formatting (decide once: is it a percent, a rate, or a count?) so the same column always means the same thing.

One-line redesign checklist

  1. Replace each pie-for-a-trend with a line chart.
  2. Replace the 3D 12-district bar chart with a flat, sorted bar or dot plot, or small multiples.
  3. Label every axis; start axes at a defensible baseline; keep scales consistent across panels.
  4. Swap red/green for a colourblind-safe palette and add a non-colour cue.
  5. Demote the master table; add sort + filter; standardise number formats.
  6. Order the layout by decision priority, not by what fit on the screen.
