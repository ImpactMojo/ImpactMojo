The Five SEL Competencies: Adapted for Field Teams

A working reference for designing adult, workplace-based social-emotional learning activities

How to use this document. This is one of two case files for the challenge “Design a Conflict Resolution Workshop for Field Teams”. Read it alongside the Team Profile Summary. Use the five competencies below as the backbone of your workshop: pick the ones most relevant to this team's tensions and build experiential activities around the example behaviours and reflection prompts. You do not need to cover all five: depth beats coverage in a half-day.

1. What is the SEL framework?

Social and Emotional Learning (SEL) is the process through which people develop the awareness and skills to understand and manage emotions, build healthy relationships, and make constructive decisions. The most widely used model organises this into five interrelated core competencies. This model was developed and is maintained by CASEL: the Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning (casel.org), whose “CASEL 5” framework is the reference adapted here.

SEL was first articulated for school settings, but the same five competencies apply directly to adult teams. In a workplace (and especially in a stressed, recently-merged field team) they show up as how colleagues notice their own reactions, stay regulated under pressure, take each other's perspective, communicate and repair relationships, and make fair joint decisions. The descriptions below keep CASEL's five competencies intact but translate the example behaviours and prompts into an adult, field-team context.

2. The five competencies in detail

2.1 Self-awareness

Recognising your own emotions, triggers, values and biases, and how they affect your behaviour and your colleagues.

Example behaviours in a field team:

  • Naming what you are feeling (“I notice I get defensive when my plan is questioned”) rather than acting it out.
  • Recognising your own triggers: e.g. feeling dismissed, feeling rushed, feeling your seniority is ignored.
  • Being honest about the assumptions or stereotypes you hold about other staff or the other district.
  • Knowing your communication style and how it lands on quieter colleagues.
Reflection prompts
  • What situations at work most often leave me frustrated or shut down?
  • When a meeting goes badly for me, what was I feeling just before I reacted?
  • What story do I tell myself about why a colleague behaves the way they do, and how sure am I that it is true?

2.2 Self-management

Managing emotions, impulses and stress so you can act with intention rather than reaction, and follow through on what you commit to.

Example behaviours in a field team:

  • Pausing and breathing before responding when a colleague says something that stings.
  • Choosing to raise a frustration calmly and at the right time, instead of going silent for weeks.
  • Managing field stress (long travel, deadlines, weather) without taking it out on the team.
  • Following through on what you said you'd do, so colleagues can rely on you.
Reflection prompts
  • What helps me stay calm when I'm under pressure? What makes it worse?
  • Is there a frustration I've been carrying silently? What would it take to raise it well?
  • When I'm stretched thin, how does my behaviour change, and who notices?

2.3 Social awareness

Taking the perspective of others, including those from different backgrounds, and reading the emotional cues in a room with empathy.

Example behaviours in a field team:

  • Noticing when a junior colleague has stopped contributing and wondering why.
  • Understanding the pressures the other programme or district is under before judging their work.
  • Recognising that a colleague who seems “difficult” may be reacting to something you can't see.
  • Being aware of how language, seniority and gender shape who feels safe to speak.
Reflection prompts
  • What might a typical week look like from the other team's point of view?
  • Who in our team rarely gets heard, and what might they be experiencing?
  • What pressure might the colleague I find hardest to work with be carrying?

2.4 Relationship skills

Building and maintaining healthy working relationships: communicating clearly, listening actively, giving and receiving feedback, and repairing relationships after conflict.

Example behaviours in a field team:

  • Active listening: reflecting back what a colleague said before responding, so they feel heard.
  • Giving feedback about behaviour and its effect (“when X happened, I felt Y”) rather than attacking the person.
  • Disagreeing directly and respectfully, instead of routing messages through juniors or going silent.
  • Naming a rupture and taking a small step to repair it.
Reflection prompts
  • When did I last feel truly listened to at work? What did that person do?
  • Is there a relationship on this team I've let go cold? What is one small repair step?
  • How do I usually give difficult feedback, and how does it tend to be received?

2.5 Responsible decision-making

Making constructive choices about your own behaviour and team interactions, weighing consequences and the wellbeing of the whole team: including the choice to address conflict rather than avoid it.

Example behaviours in a field team:

  • Choosing to surface a conflict early instead of hoping it resolves on its own.
  • Co-creating fair team norms (how we run meetings, how we disagree, how decisions get made) and holding to them.
  • Weighing how a decision affects juniors and the other district, not just yourself.
  • Owning your part in a problem rather than assigning blame.
Reflection prompts
  • What is one team norm that, if we agreed on it, would prevent a recurring conflict?
  • What is a small choice I can make this week that would improve how the team works?
  • Where am I currently avoiding a decision, and what is the cost of that avoidance?

3. Designing adult, experiential SEL activities

SEL competencies are built through experience and reflection, not lecture. A useful rhythm for adult learners is: a short experience (an activity, a story, a paired exercise), then reflection (what happened, what came up), then connection (what this means for how we work), then application (what we'll do differently). Keep these design notes in mind for this particular team:

  • Start with safety. Trust is low; begin with low-stakes activities before anything personal or conflict-related. Ground rules co-created with the group help.
  • Use pairs and small groups. People who won't speak to the full room, especially Garhwali-first juniors, will often speak in a pair. Mixing across origin teams and seniority can build bridges.
  • Make it concrete and local. Stories, role-play and scenarios drawn from field life land better than abstract concepts; flipchart drawing works without a projector and across literacy levels.
  • Protect dignity. Never put a specific conflict or person “on the spot” in public. Work with the patterns (e.g. feeling dismissed, stereotyping) rather than naming individuals.
  • Be language-inclusive. Let people reflect and speak in whichever of Hindi or Garhwali they prefer; avoid activities that depend on writing fluent Hindi.
Your task. Choose the competencies most relevant to this team's tensions and design experiential activities around them for a half-day, flipchart-only, bilingual workshop. The Team Profile Summary tells you who is in the room and what is going on; this framework gives you the building blocks. How you sequence and facilitate them is your design.
