Grameen Shakti · Mid-Term Review Summary

Mid-Term Review: Saksham Aajeevika

Conducted Months 16–18 (Nov 2024 – Jan 2025) · 5 blocks, Chhattisgarh

How to use this document. This is document 2 of 3. It summarises the findings of an independent mid-term review. Read it with the programme design document and the demographic profile to ground your GESI audit in evidence.

1. Review purpose & method

At the programme midpoint, Grameen Shakti commissioned an external mid-term review to assess progress against targets and surface delivery issues before the second half of implementation. The review team combined:

  • An extract from the participant database (all 4,180 enrolments to date);
  • 14 focus group discussions with participants and 9 with non-participants, across all five blocks;
  • 22 key-informant interviews with mobilisers, master trainers and the partner bank;
  • A drop-out tracing exercise covering 240 women who enrolled but did not complete training.

On the headline gender indicator the programme is performing well: 72% of the 4,180 participants to date are women, comfortably above the 70% target. Training throughput is on track. Loan and market-linkage numbers, however, are lagging, and the review found that the shortfall is concentrated among women participants.

Headline finding. The 72% women's participation figure masks a series of structural barriers. Women enrol in large numbers, but a disproportionate share drop out before completion, far fewer reach a loan, and whole groups of women are effectively absent from the programme. Participation breadth is strong; participation depth and equity are not.

3. Key findings

3.1 Training timing excludes mothers of young children

Training runs 10:00 a.m. to 3:30 p.m., six days a week, for 30 days. The 3:30 p.m. finish clashes with the primary-school pick-up and the start of the afternoon care and cooking load. The drop-out tracing exercise found that of 240 women who enrolled but did not complete, 61% cited the training schedule: specifically the afternoon timing and the lack of any childcare, as their main reason for leaving. 3.2 The male-guarantor rule blocks women from loans

The partner bank requires a male guarantor for every micro-enterprise loan. The review found this is the single biggest reason women complete training but never reach a loan. Among trained women, the loan conversion rate is less than half that of men, and it collapses for women without an available male relative.

Widowed, separated and never-married women, and women in female-headed households, are most affected: many cannot produce an eligible male guarantor at all, and the bank will not waive the requirement.

“I finished the tailoring course and made my plan, but the bank said my signature is not enough. I need my husband or my brother. My husband passed away and my brother lives in another state.”, trained participant, Dantewada block

3.3 The trades on offer track traditional gender roles

All three trades (tailoring, backyard poultry and mushroom cultivation) are extensions of work women already do at home, and command relatively low and seasonal returns. No higher-return or non-traditional options (digital and data-entry skills, two-wheeler / pump mechanics, electrical work, food processing at scale) are offered, and women are channelled overwhelmingly into tailoring.

The mid-term income data show the trades women cluster in earn the least; men are over-represented in the higher- earning mushroom trade. The programme's average-income target of ₹3,000/month is not being met by the tailoring units that most women run.

3.4 No accommodations for persons with disabilities

Two of the five block training centres are on first floors with no ramp or accessible toilet; none provide sign- language support, large-print or audio materials, or transport assistance. Mobilisers reported that they do not ask about disability during enrolment and “have not really thought about it.” The participant database contains no disability field. Against an estimated working-age disability prevalence of roughly 2.2% in the target area (see Document 3), the review could identify only 9 participants with a visible disability out of 4,180 (0.2%): a clear sign that persons with disabilities are being screened out at the door.

3.5 PVTG tribal women are largely absent

Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups (PVTGs) (principally Abujhmaria, Hill Maria and Kamar communities in these blocks) make up about 18% of the target-area population but only 3% of participants. PVTG hamlets are remote from the block training centres, the 30-day centre-based format is incompatible with their livelihood calendar, training is delivered only in Hindi (not in Gondi or local dialects), and mobilisers concede they spend most of their outreach time in larger, road-accessible villages. PVTG women face all of these barriers at once.

3.6 No disaggregation by disability or caste

The participant database records sex but not disability status, caste/tribe category, marital status or whether the household is female-headed. As a result the programme cannot see any of the patterns above in its own routine data: the review had to reconstruct them by hand from registers and field visits. Reporting against the results framework shows only the 72% women's figure, which is why the equity gaps had gone unnoticed for 16 months.
