1. What this model is

Intervention B delivers nutrition care at the village level. Trained ASHA workers conduct home visits and group sessions covering infant and young-child feeding, growth monitoring, hygiene, and early identification of faltering. Moderately malnourished (MAM) children receive counselling plus take-home supplementary rations; the most severe cases are referred upward. The model is light on infrastructure and leans on the existing ASHA cadre, so its per-child unit cost is roughly one-seventh that of the facility model, but recovery is more modest because care is less intensive and adherence varies.

3. Fixed vs variable split (for scale-up modelling)

The cost structure here is almost the mirror image of the facility model: about 71% is variable (ASHA incentives, supplements and case-level M&E that rise directly with the number of children reached) and only ~29% is fixed supervision, training and overhead. This is what makes the community model attractive for wide coverage: per-child cost stays roughly flat as you scale, and there is no bed-occupancy constraint.

4. Pilot total (illustrative)

Over the 18-month pilot the model reached 11,000 children with an average follow-up of about 3.8 months, giving roughly 41,410 child-follow-up-months. At ₹1,200 per child-month that is an indicative pilot spend of about ₹4.97 crore: only slightly less than Intervention A in total, despite reaching 4.6× as many children. Use the month-by-month figures in the outcome dataset to derive your own series.

Note for modellers: because per-child cost barely changes with volume, the interesting question is not total spend but cost per child recovered, and how that shifts once you weight outcomes by intake severity (Intervention B treats a far higher share of moderate cases). Deriving and defending that adjustment is central to the task.
