The Right to Information Act, 2005: Key Sections

A plain-language study summary for accountability campaigners · Government of India statute

This is a study summary, not legal advice. It paraphrases the main provisions of the Right to Information Act, 2005 (a public-domain Central statute) to help you draft a strategy for the challenge. Always check the bare Act, the relevant Information Commission's rules, and your State's RTI Rules before filing. Section numbers below follow the Act as enacted; some fees and procedural details are set by State / Central RTI Rules and vary.
How to use this document. This is one of three case files for the challenge “Draft an RTI Strategy for an Accountability Campaign”. Read it with the PMGSY guidelines summary and the sample RTI application format, then design Jan Sarokar's phased RTI strategy for the 14 PMGSY roads.

1. What the Act is for

The RTI Act, 2005 gives every citizen a legally enforceable right to access information held by, or under the control of, “public authorities”. Its stated object is to promote transparency and accountability in the working of public authorities, to contain corruption, and to keep the governed informed. It applies across India and covers the Central Government, State Governments, local bodies (including Panchayats and municipal bodies), and bodies substantially financed or controlled by government, which makes it directly usable against the agencies that sanction and build PMGSY roads.

2. Definitions s.2

Section 2 defines the terms the rest of the Act turns on. The ones that matter most for a campaign:

  • “Information” (s.2(f)): any material in any form (records, documents, memos, e-mails, opinions, advices, press releases, circulars, orders, logbooks, contracts, reports, papers, samples, models, data held in electronic form) and information relating to a private body that a public authority can access under any other law.
  • “Public authority” (s.2(h)): any authority, body or institution of self-government established under the Constitution, by Parliament, by a State legislature, or by government notification: including bodies owned, controlled or substantially financed by government, and NGOs substantially financed by government.
  • “Record” (s.2(i)): documents, files, microfilm, and any material produced by a computer.
  • “Right to information” (s.2(j)): the right to access information held by a public authority, including the right to inspect works, documents and records; take notes, extracts or certified copies; take certified samples of material; and obtain information as printouts, diskettes, tapes or in any electronic form. (Inspection of works, e.g. physically inspecting a built road, is an explicit, named right.)

3. Right to information s.3

Section 3 is the operative grant: subject to the provisions of this Act, all citizens shall have the right to information. Only citizens hold the right; the requester does not have to be a resident of the area, an affected party, or give any reason for wanting the information.

4. Proactive (suo-motu) disclosure s.4

Section 4 places ongoing obligations on every public authority, independent of any request. Authorities must maintain and computerise their records, and must publish on their own initiative (“suo motu”) a wide range of information: under s.4(1)(b), seventeen categories including their functions and duties, the powers of officers, norms for decision-making, rules and records held, budgets and the manner of execution of subsidy programmes, particulars of beneficiaries, and a directory of officers. Section 4(1)(c) and (d) require authorities to publish relevant facts when framing important policies and to give reasons for administrative decisions to affected persons.

Campaign relevance. Much PMGSY information (sanctioned works, costs, contractor details, progress) should already be in the public domain under s.4 (and on the PMGSY online monitoring portal). Where a request asks for something that ought to have been disclosed proactively, that strengthens the case and weakens any claim that supplying it is burdensome.

5. Public Information Officers s.5

Every public authority must designate Central / State Public Information Officers (PIOs) in all its administrative units to receive and decide requests, and Assistant PIOs at the sub-district / sub-divisional level to receive applications and forward them. A PIO may seek the assistance of any other officer to discharge their duties (s.5(4)), and that officer is then treated as a PIO for liability purposes (s.5(5)). Identifying the correct PIO, e.g. the PIO in the Block / Rural Works / PMGSY implementing division, is a practical first step.

6. How to request information s.6

  • A request must be in writing or electronically, in English, Hindi, or the official language of the area, with the prescribed fee, to the PIO or APIO of the concerned authority.
  • Where someone cannot put the request in writing, the PIO must render reasonable assistance to reduce it to writing (s.6(1) proviso): useful where applicants have low literacy.
  • No reason need be given for requesting the information, and no personal details are required beyond what is necessary to contact the applicant (s.6(2)).
  • If the request is for information held by another public authority, or more closely connected with another authority's functions, the receiving PIO must transfer it (or the relevant part) within 5 days and inform the applicant (s.6(3)).

  • If the PIO fails to decide within the time limit, the request is deemed refused (s.7(2)), which opens the right to appeal.
  • Where additional fees are charged for providing information, the PIO must give an intimation with calculations; the time between that intimation and payment is excluded from the time limit (s.7(3)).
  • Free information for delay: if the authority misses the deadline, the information must be provided free of charge (s.7(6)).
  • A person below the poverty line (BPL) pays no fee (s.7(5) proviso). Fee amounts and modes are fixed by the Central / State RTI Rules (commonly a nominal application fee plus a per-page copying charge).
  • Any rejection must state the reasons, the period for appeal, and the appellate authority's particulars (s.7(8)).
  • Information is normally to be provided in the form requested unless that would disproportionately divert resources or harm the safety/preservation of the record (s.7(9)).

8. Exemptions from disclosure s.8

Section 8(1) lists the categories that may be withheld. The ones an authority is most likely to invoke against an accountability request:

  • (a) information whose disclosure would prejudicially affect sovereignty, security, strategic, scientific or economic interests, or relations with a foreign State, or incite an offence;
  • (b) information expressly forbidden to be published by a court or whose disclosure would be contempt of court;
  • (c) information whose disclosure would breach the privilege of Parliament or a State legislature;
  • (d) commercial confidence, trade secrets or intellectual property, the disclosure of which would harm the competitive position of a third party, unless the larger public interest warrants disclosure;
  • (e) information available to a person in a fiduciary relationship, unless public interest warrants disclosure;
  • (f) information received in confidence from a foreign government;
  • (g) information that would endanger the life or physical safety of a person, or identify a confidential source / informant;
  • (h) information that would impede the process of investigation or apprehension or prosecution of offenders;
  • (i) cabinet papers (with a proviso releasing decisions and reasons once a matter is complete);
  • (j) personal information with no relationship to any public activity or interest, or which would be an unwarranted invasion of privacy: unless the larger public interest justifies it.
The public-interest override. Under s.8(2), a public authority may allow access even to exempt information (and even to matters under the Official Secrets Act, 1923) if the public interest in disclosure outweighs the harm to the protected interests. Section 8(3) generally opens records that are more than 20 years old (subject to a few carve-outs). For PMGSY contracts, an authority may try to claim s.8(1)(d) “commercial confidence”, but contract values, payments from public funds, quality reports and muster rolls are public-money records, and the public-interest override is the standard ground for getting them.

9. Severability s.10

Even where part of a record is exempt, the PIO must provide access to the part that is not exempt and can reasonably be severed from the exempt portion (s.10(1)). A blanket refusal of an entire file because one item is exempt is improper; the PIO must notify what has been severed and why (s.10(2)).

10. Third-party information s.11

Where information relates to or was supplied by a third party and treated as confidential, the PIO must, within 5 days, give that third party written notice and an opportunity (within 10 days) to make a representation before deciding (s.11). The third party's objection is not decisive: the PIO weighs it against the public interest. This is the procedure that typically applies when you seek a contractor's records, and it is one reason a third-party-affected request gets the longer 40-day timeline under s.7.

11. Appeals s.19

First appeal: s.19(1)

If you are refused, get no reply within the time limit (deemed refusal), or are unhappy with the decision/fee, you may appeal within 30 days to the officer senior in rank to the PIO in the same authority (the “First Appellate Authority”). A delayed appeal can be admitted if there was sufficient cause. The first appeal is to be decided within 30 days (extendable to 45 days for reasons recorded).

Second appeal: s.19(3)

A second appeal lies within 90 days of the first-appeal decision (or the date it was due) to the Central Information Commission (CIC) or the relevant State Information Commission (SIC): for a Jharkhand State public authority, the Jharkhand State Information Commission.

  • Burden of proof (s.19(5)): in any appeal, the burden of proving that a refusal was justified is on the PIO, not the appellant.
  • The Commission can require the authority to provide the information, impose costs, and direct other relief (s.19(7)–(8)). Its decision is binding.

The challenge expects you to lay out exactly this ladder (first appeal, then second appeal) with the grounds (e.g. deemed refusal, improper s.8 claim, no s.10 severance, public-interest override).

12. Penalties s.20

Section 20 is the Act's teeth. The Information Commission may impose a penalty of ₹250 per day, up to a maximum of ₹25,000, on a PIO who, without reasonable cause, refuses to receive an application, does not furnish information within the time limit, malafidely denies a request, knowingly gives incorrect/incomplete/misleading information, destroys requested information, or obstructs the work. The Commission can also recommend disciplinary action against the PIO under their service rules (s.20(2)). The penalty is recovered from the officer personally: a powerful lever where an authority stonewalls.
