Gandhian Method · Working Framework

Satyagraha Strategy Framework

A structured toolkit for applying Gandhian non-violent strategy to a contemporary movement

How to use this document. This is one of two case files for the challenge “Apply Gandhian Strategy to a Contemporary Social Movement”. Use it together with Gandhi’s Key Writings (Excerpts): the excerpts give you the primary sources; this framework turns the philosophy into elements you can test against a real, present-day movement.

Gandhi never reduced satyagraha to a checklist: for him it was a way of life as much as a method. But across his campaigns (South Africa, Champaran, the Salt March, Quit India) a recognisable structure recurs. The elements below distil that structure into a working framework. Treat it as a lens, not a recipe: your job in the challenge is to ask how well each element transfers to the movement you choose.

1. The core elements

ElementWhat it meansQuestion to ask of a modern movement
Truth-force (satyagraha)Hold firmly to a truth/just claim and appeal to the opponent’s conscience rather than coerce them.What is the simple, undeniable truth at the movement’s core? Can it be stated so the public, and the opponent, recognise it?
Non-violence (ahimsa)Refuse to injure the opponent in body, word or intent, even under provocation.Can the movement maintain discipline online and offline, where a single violent act can discredit it?
Self-sufferingThe satyagrahi accepts suffering, arrest, hardship, rather than inflicting it, to convert opponents and onlookers.Who bears the cost in this movement, and is that suffering visible enough to move public opinion?
Means as endsThe methods used must mirror the just outcome sought (“as the means, so the end”).Are the movement’s tactics consistent with the world it wants to build?
Swaraj / self-relianceBuild the community’s own capacity and institutions, not just demands on authority.What does “self-rule” look like here: what can people build for themselves?
Constructive programmePositive, everyday work that builds the alternative alongside resistance.What is the constructive counterpart to the protest: the thing being built, not just opposed?

2. Conditions for satyagraha

Gandhi argued satyagraha could not simply be declared; certain conditions had to be met first:

  • A genuine grievance and a just cause: the claim must be true and defensible, not merely advantageous.
  • Exhaustion of ordinary remedies: petition, negotiation and persuasion attempted before direct action.
  • Self-purification and discipline: participants trained to remain non-violent under provocation.
  • Willingness to suffer the consequences: including arrest and penalty, openly and without evasion.
  • An opponent capable of conscience: the method works by appealing to a shared sense of justice.
  • A constructive alternative: resistance paired with building self-reliance.

3. The escalation ladder

Gandhi escalated deliberately, beginning with the mildest steps and moving up only when necessary: each rung non-violent, public and disciplined.

  1. Negotiation & petition. State the grievance, seek redress through dialogue and formal channels.
  2. Agitation & publicity. Make the injustice visible: meetings, the press, public education.
  3. Ultimatum. A clear, reasonable demand with a deadline, leaving the opponent room to concede.
  4. Constructive work & self-reliance. Build the alternative (e.g. spinning khadi, local institutions) to reduce dependence on the opponent.
  5. Non-co-operation. Withdraw co-operation: boycotts, resignations, refusal of titles or services.
  6. Civil disobedience. Openly, non-violently break an unjust law (as with the salt law), accepting the penalty.
  7. Parallel institutions. Where co-operation is fully withdrawn, run alternative structures of self-rule.

4. Do’s and don’ts for the satyagrahi

Do

  • Keep demands clear, limited and just.
  • Stay non-violent in word, deed and intent: even when attacked.
  • Be transparent: announce intentions in advance.
  • Accept the legal penalty willingly and openly.
  • Respect the opponent; seek to convert, not crush.
  • Pair resistance with constructive work.
  • Be ready to negotiate and to suspend if discipline breaks.

Don’t

  • Don’t humiliate, hate or seek to harm the opponent.
  • Don’t use secrecy, deception or surprise attacks.
  • Don’t escalate beyond what the cause requires.
  • Don’t evade the consequences of breaking a law.
  • Don’t exploit the opponent’s weakness or distress.
  • Don’t continue if followers cannot stay non-violent.
  • Don’t treat winning as the only measure of success.

5. Apply it: contemporary movements

Choose ONE of the movements below (or another of your own). Use the framework to test how Gandhian strategy could apply, what would need adapting for a 21st-century context (social media, surveillance, globalised power), and where it might fall short. The prompts are starting points, not a script.

Right to Information (RTI) activism in India

Apply: The RTI movement turns a simple truth-claim, citizens’ right to know, into disciplined, lawful action; how does it map to truth-force and swaraj (the capacity to check abused authority)? Adapt: activists face real risks, including violence against whistle-blowers. Limits: can self-suffering convert an opaque bureaucracy that has no single conscience to appeal to?

Climate justice (e.g. Fridays for Future)

Apply: school strikes echo non-co-operation and visible, disciplined protest; the “truth” is scientific consensus. Adapt: the “opponent” is diffuse, states, corporations, all of us. Limits: who exactly is meant to be converted by self-suffering, and on what timescale, when harm is global and delayed?

Forest & land rights movements (tribal / Adivasi)

Apply: constructive programme and self-reliance map closely onto community forest governance and customary self-rule. Adapt: communities may face displacement, criminalisation and asymmetric force. Limits: can non-violent self-suffering withstand the loss of land and livelihood when the stakes are survival itself?

Digital privacy & surveillance resistance

Apply: Gandhi’s insistence on openness sits in tension with a movement that often relies on encryption and anonymity. Adapt: “civil disobedience” may mean openly refusing intrusive systems and accepting the penalty. Limits: does the satyagrahi’s demand for total transparency even fit a struggle for privacy?

Anti-caste movements

Apply: constructive programme, dignity and self-respect resonate strongly. Adapt & contest: note the substantive critique, Dr B.R. Ambedkar argued that Gandhian moral appeal left caste hierarchy intact and that annihilation of caste required structural and legal change, not the conversion of oppressors’ hearts. Limits: where the opponent’s “conscience” is itself shaped by caste, can appeal to it deliver justice? Engage this tension directly.
