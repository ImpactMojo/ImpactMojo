Gandhi’s Key Writings (Excerpts)

Short, attributed passages on satyagraha, ahimsa, swaraj and civil disobedience · for study

How to use this document. This is one of two case files for the challenge “Apply Gandhian Strategy to a Contemporary Social Movement”. Read it alongside the Satyagraha Strategy Framework, then choose one contemporary movement and analyse how Gandhian principles could apply, what would need adapting, and where they might face limits.
A note on these excerpts. The passages below are short extracts from the public-domain writings and speeches of Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi (1869–1948), reproduced for study. Gandhi’s pre-1948 works are in the public domain in India. Sources and years are cited for each excerpt; where wording varies across editions, the standard published text is used. Each excerpt is followed by a one-line gloss explaining the concept: the gloss is editorial, not Gandhi’s words. For full context, consult the cited source or the Collected Works of Mahatma Gandhi (CWMG).

1. Satyagraha: truth-force

“Truth (satya) implies love, and firmness (agraha) engenders and therefore serves as a synonym for force. I thus began to call the Indian movement Satyagraha, that is to say, the Force which is born of Truth and Love or non-violence…”
M.K. Gandhi, Satyagraha in South Africa (1928), ch. XII.

Concept: Satyagraha is not passive resistance but an active force grounded in truth and love; the resister holds firmly to truth while refusing to injure the opponent.

“Passive resistance is a method of securing rights by personal suffering; it is the reverse of resistance by arms. When I refuse to do a thing that is repugnant to my conscience, I use soul-force.”
M.K. Gandhi, Hind Swaraj or Indian Home Rule (1909), ch. XVII, “Passive Resistance”.

Concept: “Soul-force” works through voluntary self-suffering and conscientious refusal, not through coercion of the opponent.

2. Ahimsa: non-violence

“Non-violence is the greatest force at the disposal of mankind. It is mightier than the mightiest weapon of destruction devised by the ingenuity of man.”
M.K. Gandhi, in Harijan, 11 July 1936.

Concept: Ahimsa is treated as a positive, active power: a source of strength, not weakness or mere abstention from violence.

“Non-violence in its dynamic condition means conscious suffering. It does not mean meek submission to the will of the evil-doer, but it means the putting of one’s whole soul against the will of the tyrant.”
M.K. Gandhi, in Young India, 11 August 1920.

Concept: Non-violence is opposition without injury: it confronts injustice directly while refusing to retaliate in kind.

3. Means and ends

“They say, ‘means are after all means’. I would say, ‘means are after all everything’. As the means so the end… There is no wall of separation between means and end.”
M.K. Gandhi, in Young India, 17 July 1924.

Concept: For Gandhi, just ends cannot be reached by unjust means; the method shapes the outcome, so the means must themselves be pure.

“The means may be likened to a seed, the end to a tree; and there is just the same inviolable connection between the means and the end as there is between the seed and the tree.”
M.K. Gandhi, Hind Swaraj or Indian Home Rule (1909), ch. XVI, “Brute Force”.

Concept: The seed-and-tree image is Gandhi’s core argument that violent means cannot grow non-violent ends.

4. Swaraj: self-rule

“It is Swaraj when we learn to rule ourselves. It is, therefore, in the palm of our hands… But such Swaraj has to be experienced, by each one for himself.”
M.K. Gandhi, Hind Swaraj or Indian Home Rule (1909), ch. XIV, “How Can India Become Free?”.

Concept: Swaraj is self-rule in a double sense: political independence and the self-discipline of individuals and communities; freedom begins from within.

“Real Swaraj will come not by the acquisition of authority by a few but by the acquisition of the capacity by all to resist authority when it is abused.”
M.K. Gandhi, in Young India, 29 January 1925.

Concept: Genuine self-rule is distributed: it lives in the everyday capacity of ordinary people to check the misuse of power.

5. The Salt March & civil disobedience

“At present India is a House on fire… I want world sympathy in this battle of Right against Might.”
M.K. Gandhi, statement on the eve of the Salt March, Sabarmati Ashram, 11 March 1930 (reported in the press, March 1930).

Concept: The Salt Satyagraha framed a simple, universal grievance (the salt tax) as a moral contest, deliberately seeking the sympathy of onlookers and the world.

“Civil disobedience is the inherent right of a citizen. He dare not give it up without ceasing to be a man. Civil disobedience is never followed by anarchy. Criminal disobedience can lead to it.”
M.K. Gandhi, in Young India, 5 January 1922.

Concept: Gandhi distinguishes civil disobedience (open, disciplined, non-violent, and willing to bear the legal penalty) from lawless “criminal” disobedience.

6. The constructive programme

“Civil Disobedience without the co-operation of the millions by way of constructive effort is mere bravado and worse than useless.”
M.K. Gandhi, Constructive Programme: Its Meaning and Place (1941; revised 1945).

Concept: Resistance must be paired with constructive work, building self-reliant institutions and communities, or it has no foundation.
