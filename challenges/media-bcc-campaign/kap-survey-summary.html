1. Survey design

  • Population: unmarried and married adolescent girls aged 15–19 in 18 sample villages across the Muraul, Bochaha, Kanti and Sakra blocks of Muzaffarpur.
  • Sample: 1,200 girls selected by stratified random sampling (proportionate to block population, oversampling out-of-school girls).
  • Method: structured face-to-face interview by trained female enumerators in Hindi/Bhojpuri, plus a finger-prick hemoglobin test (HemoCue Hb 201+) for every respondent.
  • Margin of error: approximately ±2.8 percentage points at the 95% confidence level for whole-sample estimates.
  • Anemia cut-offs (WHO, non-pregnant girls 15–19): any anemia Hb < 12.0 g/dL; mild 11.0–11.9; moderate 8.0–10.9; severe < 8.0 g/dL.

2. Anemia prevalence

More than two in three adolescent girls were anemic. Prevalence rose with age and was sharply higher among girls who had left school.

Iron supplementation is framed as “medicine for the sick,” and menstruation taboo suppresses the conversations that would normalise anemia care for girls.

Reported behaviour lags far behind even the modest knowledge base: most girls had not eaten an iron-rich food in the past day, and only one in five had taken the weekly iron tablet.

6. Structural environment

The supply side is thin. Most girls have no adolescent-friendly health service, are not reached by ASHAs (who concentrate on maternal health), and, if out of school, have no institutional touchpoint at all.

7. What the data implies for campaign design

  • Knowledge is necessary but not sufficient: even where girls know iron-rich foods, practice (28% ate one yesterday) is far lower: barriers are economic, household and gendered, not only informational.
  • Out-of-school girls are the hardest-to-reach and worst-off on nearly every indicator: they cannot be reached through schools alone.
  • Reframing iron supplementation away from “sick-person medicine” toward strength, energy and aspiration is a core message-design problem.
  • Menstruation taboo (71% uncomfortable) constrains both message content and the choice of messenger.
  • Household decision-makers (mothers, mothers-in-law, fathers) shape the “girls eat last” norm and must be a distinct audience.
