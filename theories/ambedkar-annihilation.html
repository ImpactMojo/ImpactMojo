Theories of Development · Anti-caste · 1916–1956

Annihilation of caste and state socialism

Caste is a division of labourers rather than of labour, held in place by endogamy and by religious sanction, and no economic programme that leaves it standing will reach the people at the bottom of it.

B. R. Ambedkar · Credits and sources ↓

The argument

What it says

Ambedkar's first published work on the subject, a seminar paper written at Columbia in 1916, is about a mechanism. Caste is not simply occupational specialisation. What makes it self-reproducing is endogamy: a rule of marrying within, superimposed on a society that was previously exogamous, which requires a surplus man and a surplus woman in every generation to be disposed of. Sati, enforced widowhood and the marriage of girls are, in his reading, the means by which the boundary is maintained. A caste is a closed class, and closure is the thing to explain.

Annihilation of Caste, written twenty years later as an address he was never allowed to deliver, draws the political conclusion. Caste is graded inequality, which is why it is stable: every group except the lowest has someone beneath it and therefore something to defend. It cannot be dissolved by inter-dining, or by economic reform alone, because its sanction is religious. Ambedkar's demand is against the authority of the shastras themselves, and it is the reason the conference that invited him withdrew the invitation.

The economic programme is set out in States and Minorities in 1947, submitted to the Constituent Assembly on behalf of the Scheduled Castes Federation, and it is more radical than anything in the Congress platform. Key industries owned by the state. Insurance a state monopoly. Agriculture a state industry: land taken over, consolidated, and leased to collective farms worked by village residents without distinction of caste or creed. He called it state socialism, and he wanted it written into the constitution so that no legislature could undo it.

The reason for that programme is caste rather than class. A landless Dalit household given formal equality but no land, in a village where land and credit and labour hiring all run along caste lines, is free in a sense that does not bite. Ambedkar's answer is to remove the asset from the field where caste operates, which is also why he had no patience at all for the village as a unit of self-government.

Where it sits

Four placements, and the reason for each

The four scores are editorial. They run from -3 to +3, they were assigned by the ImpactMojo editorial team from the theory's own texts, and each theory page shows the sentence that justifies its placement so the placement can be argued with. They are a way of arranging a shelf, not a measurement.

Who allocates
The state decides Markets decide

State socialism, written as a constitutional obligation rather than a policy. Ambedkar distrusted the market for the same reason he distrusted the village: both were arenas where caste already held the positions.

What comes first
Redistribute first Grow first

As far towards redistribute-first as the shelf goes, and explicitly so: political democracy without social democracy beneath it will not last.

Where hierarchy sits
Hierarchy is its own cause Hierarchy follows from economics

The defining position. Hierarchy is a cause with its own machinery, and a purely economic programme is what he spent his career arguing against.

Who moves
Movements and the poor Experts and the state

Both, uneasily. Organisation of the oppressed themselves, and a state strong enough to act against the society that produced it. He did not think either alone would do.

What happened

The theory against the record

Each entry takes one claim the theory makes and reports what the evidence says about it, with a named source and a year. This section is the reason the library exists; a catalogue of positions without it is a reading list.

The claim Endogamy is the load-bearing mechanism.

It has barely moved. The India Human Development Survey found around five per cent of Indian marriages crossing caste boundaries, a figure that has changed little across survey rounds and is low enough that the boundary Ambedkar identified is still doing what he said it does.

India Human Development Survey, NCAER and University of Maryland · 2012

The claim Legal abolition would end the practice of untouchability.

Article 17 abolished untouchability in 1950 and the practice survives it. In the same survey, around a quarter of households said someone in the household practised untouchability, a figure obtained by asking directly and therefore likely to be an underestimate.

Amit Thorat and Omkar Joshi on IHDS data, Economic and Political Weekly · 2015

The claim Industrial and urban employment dissolves caste.

Not on its own. Sukhadeo Thorat and Paul Attewell sent matched job applications differing only in the applicant's name to private-sector vacancies and found Dalit and Muslim names called for interview at significantly lower rates than equivalently qualified upper-caste Hindu names. The modern sector reproduced the boundary rather than dissolving it, which is the outcome Ambedkar's theory predicts and Lewis's does not.

Sukhadeo Thorat and Paul Attewell, Economic and Political Weekly · 2007

The claim The asset base would shift.

It has not, in the way the programme required. Land reform in India transferred a small fraction of operated area, ceiling legislation was widely evaded, and the collective farming proposal of States and Minorities was never attempted. Scheduled Caste households remain disproportionately landless and disproportionately dependent on wage labour, which is the condition the programme was designed to end.

Agricultural Census and NSSO land and livestock holding surveys · 2019

What this does not settle

The part of Ambedkar's programme that was adopted is the part that could be written into a constitution, and the part that was not is the economic half. That makes the natural experiment unavailable: nobody can say what state ownership of land leased to caste-mixed collectives would have done, because it was never tried anywhere in India at scale. What the record does show is that the constitutional half alone has produced representation and mobility for some without dissolving the mechanism, which is close to what Ambedkar predicted would happen if the two halves were separated. He said in 1949 that the country was entering a life of contradictions, and named this one.

In India

How it landed here

This is the Indian theory that the international shelf usually leaves out, and its absence distorts everything around it. Read next to Lewis or Rostow it identifies exactly what they assume away: that a worker is free to move, that a market hires on productivity, that the village is a neutral container. Read next to Gandhi it is the sharpest disagreement in modern Indian thought, conducted between two people who agreed on independence and on almost nothing about what should follow.

Credit where it is owed

Whose theory this is

B. R. Ambedkar 1891–1956

Economist first, by training: a Columbia PhD and an LSE doctorate on the rupee before he was a jurist or a leader. The economics is not a separate part of the work, and reading the caste writing without it makes the programme look like moral exhortation, which it is not.

Start with these

  • B. R. Ambedkar, Castes in India: Their Mechanism, Genesis and Development (1916). The Columbia seminar paper. Short, and the place where endogamy is set out as the mechanism.
  • B. R. Ambedkar, Annihilation of Caste (1936). The undelivered address, published by the author after the invitation was withdrawn. Later editions carry Gandhi's response and Ambedkar's reply to it.
  • B. R. Ambedkar, States and Minorities (1947). The economic programme, submitted to the Constituent Assembly. The section on agriculture as a state industry is the part least often read.

Open access, in Development Discourses:

  • Caste, Discrimination and Exclusion in Modern India — Sukhadeo Thorat, Katherine Newman (2010)
  • Land Reform, Poverty Reduction, and Growth: Evidence from India — Timothy Besley, Robin Burgess (2000)
