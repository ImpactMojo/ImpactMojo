Theories of Development · Indian nationalist · 1909–1948

Village swaraj and the economy of permanence

An economy should be organised around the village as a largely self-sufficient unit producing for local need with local labour, because centralised industrial production concentrates power, displaces the labour of a country that has labour to spare, and cannot be sustained by everyone.

M. K. Gandhi, J. C. Kumarappa · Credits and sources ↓

The argument

What it says

Hind Swaraj is not primarily about the British. Its argument is that modern industrial civilisation is itself the affliction, and that a free India running the same machinery from Delhi would have exchanged the rulers and kept the disease. Swaraj means rule over oneself before it means rule by Indians, and an economic arrangement is judged by whether it leaves people capable of that.

The unit is the village, and Gandhi's 1946 statement of it is precise: a complete republic, growing its own food and cotton, with its own school, its own sanitation, its own panchayat, dependent on neighbours for what it cannot make. He drew the picture as an oceanic circle of villages rather than a pyramid, in which the outer circle does not crush the inner but gives it strength.

Two arguments carry the economics. Machinery that displaces labour is destructive in a country whose surplus is labour, so the test of a technique is not output per worker but whether it employs the people who are there. Gandhi was clear that he was not against machinery as such, and pointed out that the spinning wheel is one. And wealth is held rather than owned: trusteeship asks the rich to administer their property for the society that produced it, which is the part of the programme that convinced almost nobody and which Gandhi defended anyway.

Kumarappa gave it the ecological formulation. An economy of permanence is one that lives on nature's income rather than its capital, and by that standard an industrial economy is not merely unjust but temporary. He asked in 1945 how many planets an industrialised world would need, which is the ecological footprint argument arriving about forty years early.

Where it sits

Four placements, and the reason for each

The four scores are editorial. They run from -3 to +3, they were assigned by the ImpactMojo editorial team from the theory's own texts, and each theory page shows the sentence that justifies its placement so the placement can be argued with. They are a way of arranging a shelf, not a measurement.

Who allocates
The state decides Markets decide

Neither state planning nor markets. Production for local use, coordinated by the village itself, with the state pushed down rather than expanded.

What comes first
Redistribute first Grow first

Sufficiency rather than growth. The last person is the test, which Gandhi put as a talisman rather than a metric.

Where hierarchy sits
Hierarchy is its own cause Hierarchy follows from economics

The hardest to place and the most contested. Gandhi campaigned against untouchability for decades and defended varna as an ideal of duty for much of his life, moving late and incompletely. Ambedkar's charge is that the second position undermined the first.

Who moves
Movements and the poor Experts and the state

Villagers, constructive workers and the moral conversion of the powerful. Not planners, and not a state acting on society from above.

What happened

The theory against the record

Each entry takes one claim the theory makes and reports what the evidence says about it, with a named source and a year. This section is the reason the library exists; a catalogue of positions without it is a reading list.

The claim Village self-government can be constituted.

It was, though not in the form proposed. The 73rd Amendment in 1992 made three-tier panchayats mandatory with regular elections and reserved seats, producing roughly a quarter of a million panchayats and around three million elected representatives, a third of them women by law and more than that in states which went further. What was withheld is money and staff: devolution of the subjects listed in the Eleventh Schedule has been partial in most states, so the institution exists with a fraction of the powers.

Ministry of Panchayati Raj; Constitution (Seventy-third Amendment) Act 1992 and the Eleventh Schedule · 1992

The claim Local control produces decisions closer to local need.

Supported where it has been tested properly. Raghabendra Chattopadhyay and Esther Duflo used the random assignment of women-reserved pradhan seats in West Bengal and Rajasthan and found that reserved panchayats invested more in the goods women in those states named as priorities, drinking water in West Bengal and roads in Rajasthan. Kerala's People's Plan Campaign from 1996, which moved a large share of state plan funds to local bodies, is the strongest Indian case that the devolution can be made real rather than formal.

Chattopadhyay and Duflo, Econometrica; Kerala State Planning Board · 2004

The claim The village is a suitable unit for the people at the bottom of it.

This is where the theory takes its heaviest damage, and the criticism is Ambedkar's rather than a later economist's. A unit in which land, credit and employment are held along caste lines does not become fair by being made autonomous, and the persistence of untouchability practice reported by around a quarter of households in national survey data is measured in villages that now have elected panchayats.

India Human Development Survey; Ambedkar's Constituent Assembly speech of 4 November 1948 · 2012

The claim Village industry can hold employment against factory production.

Only where it was protected, and the protection had costs. The Khadi and Village Industries Commission has supported village industry continuously since 1956 and khadi remains a live sector, but the reservation of products for small-scale industry, which was the policy instrument, kept firms below the size at which they could invest and was progressively dismantled after 1991 for that reason.

Khadi and Village Industries Commission; Ministry of Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises de-reservation record · 2015

What this does not settle

The employment argument and the ecological argument have separated, and each is now doing better than the tradition that carried them both. Choosing techniques by how many people they employ is being argued again under other names, and Kumarappa's question about what an industrialised world would take from the planet is now a discipline with journals. What has not survived is the claim that the village is the right container for either, and Ambedkar's objection is the reason. Whether a Gandhian economics can be detached from the village and still be Gandhian is a real question, and Schumacher's answer in 1973, that it can, is the one most people since have taken.

In India

How it landed here

Every argument about Indian development runs into this one eventually, usually through the 73rd Amendment, through the debate about labour-intensive against capital-intensive industry, or through the environmental movement. Its opponents in the Constituent Assembly won: the Indian constitution is centralised, the village appears in the Directive Principles rather than in the operative articles, and Nehru told Gandhi plainly in October 1945 that he did not accept the picture. That the panchayat was written back in half a century later, and then not funded, is the shape the argument has taken since.

Credit where it is owed

Whose theory this is

M. K. Gandhi 1869–1948

The economics is scattered across forty years of journalism rather than gathered into a treatise, which is why it is so often summarised badly. Hind Swaraj in 1909 states the position; the Harijan columns of the 1930s and 1940s work out what it means for policy.

J. C. Kumarappa 1892–1960

The trained economist of the group, Columbia-educated, who turned Gandhi's positions into something with categories and a costing. Economy of Permanence, written in prison in 1945, is the closest the tradition has to a textbook.

Start with these

  • M. K. Gandhi, Hind Swaraj (1909). Written in Gujarati on a ship, as a dialogue. The economic argument is inseparable from the argument about civilisation, which is the point.
  • J. C. Kumarappa, Economy of Permanence (1945). Written in prison. The categories, the costing, and the ecological argument in its first full form.
  • M. K. Gandhi and Jawaharlal Nehru, Correspondence of 5 and 9 October 1945 (1945). Gandhi restates the village; Nehru refuses it in writing. Four pages that decided the shape of Indian planning.
