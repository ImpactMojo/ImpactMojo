Why an annotated bare Act?

The DPDP Act is not written for NGOs: it's written for "Data Fiduciaries." But if your organisation holds beneficiary lists, runs consent forms, photographs the people you serve, or works with children, you are a Data Fiduciary, and this law is now yours to comply with.

This guide keeps the two things cleanly separated. The statute is exact: transcribed verbatim from the authoritative India Code version, illustrations and all. The annotations are ours: a plain-language gloss and a practitioner's note written for the development sector, what "consent" means for a thumbprint on a field form, why bundled consent for photos-and-data no longer works, the verifiable parental consent you now need before collecting a child's data, and the erasure duty that turns old beneficiary databases into a liability. It's the difference between reading the law and knowing what to change on Monday.

What's inside: the seven sections you actually use

  • § 4, Grounds for processing. The only two lawful bases, consent, or a defined legitimate use, and why every MIS field needs one.
  • § 5: Notice. What your consent form must now tell people, in the language they actually read.
  • § 6 (Consent. Free, specific, withdrawable) and why "bundled" consent for data-plus-photos-forever is dead.
  • § 8: Obligations of a Data Fiduciary. Vendor contracts, security, breach notification, and the duty to erase.
  • § 9 (Children's data. Verifiable parental/guardian consent, no harmful processing, no tracking or targeted ads) the section most likely to catch NGOs.
  • § 11, Right to access. Beneficiaries can ask what you hold and who you shared it with, and you must answer.
  • § 33: Penalties. The Schedule's numbers, what the Board weighs, and the realistic risk for a small NGO.

Each section: the verbatim statutory text (with the Act's own illustrations), a one-line plain-language reading, and a development-sector practitioner's note.

§ 9Processing of personal data of children
Processing of personal data of children.: (1) The Data Fiduciary shall, before processing any personal data of a child … obtain verifiable consent of the parent of such child or the lawful guardian … (3) A Data Fiduciary shall not undertake tracking or behavioural monitoring of children or targeted advertising directed at children.

This is the section most likely to catch development NGOs: school programmes, child sponsorship, adolescent health and child protection all process children's data. You now need verifiable parental or guardian consent before collecting a child's data. Donor-sponsorship models that circulate a child's photo and details need a hard rethink under 9(1)–9(2).

FAQ

Is the statutory text reliable?

Yes. The Act is reproduced verbatim from the authoritative India Code version (Act 22 of 2023), illustrations and all. Nothing is paraphrased. The annotations are clearly marked as ImpactMojo's own commentary.

The Act mentions "as may be prescribed" a lot: is it fully in force?

The Act is passed, but several provisions operate through the Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, which govern operational detail (e.g. how "verifiable consent" is obtained). The guide flags this; check the current Rules for the mechanics. The obligations and principles here are the Act's own.

Is this legal advice?

No. The annotations are practitioner commentary to help you understand and apply the Act in development work: they are not legal advice, and do not create a lawyer–client relationship. For a specific matter, consult a lawyer.
