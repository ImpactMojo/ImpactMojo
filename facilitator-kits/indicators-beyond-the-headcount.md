# Welcome — indicators beyond the headcount

A 45-minute session on the difference between counting what you did and measuring what changed, and on why so many indicator sets never cross that line.

Bring the indicator table from a live programme. Any format. If all you have is the logframe, that works.

Mark each step **done**, or **stuck** if you want the facilitator.

# Sort your own indicators

Before any theory, do the sorting. Take your indicator list and put each one in exactly one of three piles.

**Delivery.** Things your team did. Sessions held, people trained, kits distributed, reports submitted.

**Reach.** Who was touched. Numbers and shares of participants, disaggregated or not.

**Change.** Something that is different in the world, in a way that would still be different if your programme stopped.

Count each pile. Write the three numbers down.

# What the counts usually show

In most programmes the piles come out heavily weighted towards the first two, and the third pile contains one or two items measured annually, late, by someone else.

That is not incompetence. It follows from incentives: delivery is what the team controls, what the contract specifies, and what a quarterly report has room for. Change is slow, contested, and often arrives after the funding cycle.

The problem is not that delivery indicators exist. It is that a set made almost entirely of them cannot tell you whether to stop. Every number can rise while the thing you care about does not move, and nothing in the system will say so.

## An indicator set with no change indicators cannot tell you:

- [ ] Whether the programme was delivered on time
- [ ] Whether the budget was spent
- [x] Whether the programme is worth continuing
- [ ] Who participated

# Write one change indicator properly

Pick the outcome your programme exists for. Write one indicator for it that satisfies all five of these:

1. **It names a unit.** Who or what carries the value — a household, a woman, a facility, a case file.
2. **It states a denominator.** Out of what, counted when.
3. **It could go down.** An indicator that can only rise is a counter, not a measure.
4. **It has a source you can actually reach.** Name the instrument or the register. "Survey" is not a source.
5. **Somebody would argue with it.** If nobody would dispute the result, it probably cannot discriminate between success and failure.

The fifth is the real test and it is the one people skip.

# The tradeoff nobody states

Good change indicators are expensive, slow, and hard to attribute. That is precisely why programmes drift to delivery counting, and the drift is rational at every individual step.

So be explicit rather than pious about it. Decide, in the room:

- Which **one** change indicator is worth real money to measure well.
- Which delivery indicators you keep because an operational decision genuinely depends on them.
- Which you keep only because someone once asked for them, and could be dropped at the next revision.

That third list is usually longer than anyone expects, and shortening it is the cheapest quality improvement available to most MEL systems.

# Close

One action: go back to your indicator table and mark the indicators that exist only because someone asked for them. Bring that list to the next revision.

Then pick your one expensive indicator and find out what it would actually cost to measure properly. Often the answer is less than a quarter of what the programme spends on reporting the cheap ones.
